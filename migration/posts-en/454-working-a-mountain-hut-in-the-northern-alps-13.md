## Carrying the Loads

The helicopter would drop its load on the helipad and head off to pick up the next one.

It took ten to fifteen minutes for the next helicopter to come back.

In that window, every last piece of cargo, several hundred kilos (many hundreds of pounds) in all, had to be cleared off the helipad.

The whole staff focused on carrying loads, but plenty of people still had their regular work to keep up with, and some of what came up had to go into the freezer, so we also needed people to put it away.

In the end, a very small crew had to deal with this huge pile of cargo.

The noise of the helicopters and their acrobatic flying, the grind of running back and forth with heavy loads, the thin air at that altitude, and the natural high that comes from too little sleep after getting up at four in the morning.

On top of all these harsh conditions, the fired-up energy of the veteran staff and the stunning view down from the helipad got everyone worked up, whether they liked it or not.

Some people compared helicopter supply-lift day to a war, and others compared it to a festival.

Either way, it was a special day.

## Boarding

Once most of the supplies were up, it was time for the helicopter we would be riding.

Normally no one but the person guiding the helicopter was allowed on the helipad, and the helicopter never actually touched down, but carrying passengers, as it was this time, was a different story.

We waited at the edge of the helipad, heads low, for it to land.

The helicopter arrived with a roar, and we ran to it, heads still low, and jumped in.

The moment the crew could see our seatbelts were fastened, the helicopter shot upward.

Supply helicopters in the mountains run up a high fuel bill, since they have to climb to such altitudes.

And only veteran pilots with years of experience are allowed to fly supplies up the mountains, so the pilots cost a lot too.

For those reasons, everything involving the helicopter had to be done fast.
