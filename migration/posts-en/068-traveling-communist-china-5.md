# A Train in China

With the train's departure time coming up, I headed for the station.

It was to run all night and reach Xi'an sometime after noon the next day.

I found the right train and got on.

Ticket in hand, I went looking for my seat.

Since I can read kanji — the characters Japanese took from Chinese — the general meaning of what was printed on the ticket came through, and that made me happy.

The ticket had said soft seat, and that is what it was: a seat with a soft cushion.

The train left exactly on schedule, slipped through the Chinese streets, and headed out toward the outskirts.

The vast land went on and on without end.

# Soft Seat

Tired by then, I leaned against the wall and dozed in and out.

Sunk deep into the cushion, resting, I began to feel a rubbing pain around my tailbone.

The train rocked hard, and between that and the soft cushion and the angle of my body, the fabric was rubbing at exactly the spot over my tailbone.

There was nothing to do but stay in the seat, so I put up with it and stayed.

A soft seat on an overnight train was, apparently, a serious mistake.

There was another class called hard sleeper, a hard wooden bunk, I think, and that was the one I should have taken.

I did at least write the diary I had planned to keep every day, and then went to sleep.

In the end I barely slept all night, and arrived in Xi'an with my rear end still aching.

# Xi'an

As soon as I got to Xi'an, I went looking for a place to stay that night.

I was worn out from not sleeping on the train, and holding the same position the whole way had been rough on me.

A cheap guesthouse turned up right by the station, so I took a room there.

In Xi'an, nobody turned me away the way they had in Shanghai.

Here too I wrote in the diary before going to sleep, but that entry was the last; I never wrote another.

I recognized that I am the sort who quits after three days, not the sort who keeps a diary faithfully.

Rather than keeping a record in writing, I decided I would burn each moment into my eyes.

# Travelers

The next day, young enough that one night's sleep had my strength back, I bought a ticket for the train to my next destination, Xining, and got on board right away.

Here a big stroke of luck swung my way.

On the same train were two Japanese exchange students studying in Beijing.

Of course you can't tell a Japanese person at a glance, but something about them set them apart from the ordinary Chinese passengers around them.

I watched them a while, heard Japanese, and went over to speak to them.

They had come by train from Beijing all the way to Xi'an.

From here, they said, they would take about a month to pass through Xining and go on into Tibet.

Which was exactly the route I had planned.

They were exchange students based in Beijing, but they had something of the same air as the travelers I had met on Koh Phangan in Thailand.

Fed up with a country where I could not make myself understood, uneasy about traveling alone through a communist world that made no sense to me, I asked whether I could come along with them.

They took me in without a second thought, as if to say that a journey needs company and life needs kindness.

# China Behind the Scenes

Talking with them, I found out why the cheap places in Shanghai had been so hard to get into: guesthouses at that price apparently don't hold the permit required to take foreign guests.

If a place gets caught with one, it has to pay a fine to the government.

And it isn't only lodging — entry fees at tourist sites and the like come at a Chinese price and a foreigner price.

But their Chinese was fluent, so they could travel passing as Chinese, they said.

They told me all kinds of interesting inside stories about China.

This was back in 1998, so I have no idea how it stands now, but at the time, nuclear tests carried out in the interior of China were becoming an international issue.

The tests were being run where ordinary Chinese people knew nothing about them, but at the international university where these two studied there were a lot of foreigners, and all kinds of information came in from abroad.

A few of the foreign students and a Chinese student kept an argument going, each side laying out evidence.

The more they talked, the more the Chinese student's case, frantic as he was to defend the government, swung at nothing but air.

Backed into a corner, the story goes, he threw out a single line — "Actually, radiation is good for you!" — and fled.
