# A Passion for Film

I worked at Tsutaya, rented movies by the armful, and watched three a day; film sat at the center of my life.

I covered every window in my room with aluminum foil to shut out the light from outside and remodeled the place into a screening room — that was how far it went. A healthy life in the daylight mattered less to me than a darkness where I could give a movie my whole attention.

To me, art films were the most valuable thing there was, and I believed that watching them with full concentration let me take in their essence.

Just a rabid film nerd, really — but by then the thinking had become a religion, an object of worship.

My creed went like this: the more movies I watched, the sharper my taste would get, and by watching a great many art films during these impressionable teenage years, I would come closer to my dream.

Going through the emotional swings of the characters on screen was, I think, a way of tilling my own heart — getting it ready to feel.

Between a childhood in a Jehovah's Witness household and being the kid who got bullied, my emotions had been pressed down so long that they had gone numb.

Watching movies loosened those stiffened feelings and brought them back to life.

# The Movie Brain

Every time a film hit me, I could feel my values widening, feel something shaking me awake.

Living through one world after another through the eyes of one protagonist after another gave me, I think, something like an objective point of view.

Maybe, past a certain number of films, I crossed a critical point.

It felt like a sense for film I had never had before — a movie brain, more or less — was developing.

As the movie brain developed, films that had bored me no matter how many times I watched them, films whose appeal I had never been able to find, began to open up.

And the interesting part was that once one film opened up, so did the films it had drawn from, and the films of a newer generation that had drawn from it.

Listening to Tortoise's music had opened up all kinds of other music for me, and the same phenomenon was now happening with film.

What I wanted was that feeling movies produced in me — a "sharpening of artistic sense," a "human growth of some kind."

The sense of growing felt like proof that the road I had chosen was the right one.

Whether I actually grew as a person is doubtful, but there is no question that my mental capacity and my sensibility got sharper.

I think I got better at taking in a whole story from a few scraps of information without consciously working at it, and at reading where things were headed the same way, which made the overall shape easier to catch.

# Genres

Deep in movies as I was, I kept changing the genres I watched to match my inner growth and the shifts in my sensibility.

For a stretch it was American classics from the thirties, one after another, steeping me in the good old days; for a stretch it was Japanese underground films from the seventies, which gave me the strength to smash conventional thinking; for a stretch it was science fiction, one after another, sending my dreams out into space.

Watching one genre back to back let me take in the sensibility particular to it, and I think it let me identify with the films more deeply.

At that impressionable age I was influenced easily, and a short run inside one genre hit hard: my personality reflected whatever I had been watching in those days.

And the influence I took in, a little each day, stacked up and slowly shaped the person I became.

I wanted this — wanted movies to work on me — and I made a point of choosing the ones that would work on me the most.
