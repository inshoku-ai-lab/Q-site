## The Japanese Guesthouse

I had heard there was a place in Delhi's cheap-lodging district called the Navrang Guest House, cheap to stay at and popular with Japanese travelers.

I paced up and down the street, asking around for it.

It turned out to be one alley in from the middle of the Paharganj strip.

You could fairly say it sat dead center in the flow of travelers.

It was a big place, five stories or so, packed with dorm rooms and private rooms.

Set on saving every way I could so the trip would last as long as possible, I took the cheapest dorm bed without a second thought.

Six beds lined up in a dim, grimy room.

Privacy was out of the question, but no other travelers seemed to be staying, so I would have the room to myself.

It was a cheap room in Delhi, so I had no expectations at all about cleanliness, and even so, the filth was heavy enough to catch the eye.

To be fair, the floor had been swept and then gone over with a wet mop, the sheets had been washed, and something like disinfectant hung in the air, so as far as germs went the place was probably clean — but a room and a building worked to death for decades can only look so clean.

My guess is that nothing had been renovated or rebuilt since the sixties, when hippies came pouring into India.

No dust had settled on the walls, but stains of every kind lay over one another in layers, until the whole thing had the pattern of camouflage fatigues.

Even the bedsheets, crisp and straight out of the wash, carried stains of unknown origin, camouflage again.

The crowning touch was the air vent.

Roaches lived in that vent, which led who knows where, and every so often one would look out to see how things were going on my side.

I had picked the place thinking it might be interesting to meet other Japanese travelers, but I was already thinking that next time I came to Delhi I would stay somewhere else.

## Japanese Food

Right outside the guesthouse entrance, next door to it, stood a bare-bones Japanese restaurant.

Someone had converted what looked like a warehouse into it — half food stall, half garage.

The menu was extremely basic, mostly the fried noodles and fried rice you get at any Indian street stall.

Among them, though, was something called "okra donburi with a fried egg on top": boiled okra, chopped and mixed with soy sauce, spread over a bowl of rice, then a fried egg laid on top of that and more soy sauce poured over it.

I had just left Japan, so I felt no lack of Japanese food, but for the Japanese who had been traveling a while, that menu was an oasis.

## Cannabis

I got to the guesthouse, put down my pack, ate, rested a little, and then started moving right away to get hold of cannabis.

At guesthouses where travelers gather, it is generally someone connected to the place, or someone living near it, who sells cannabis.

It is a kind of cannabis concession, and it is part of the business of running a cheap guesthouse.

I got mine from one of the staff straight off and had a smoke.

The tension of having flown in from Japan came loose all at once, and I felt myself settling further into India.

Body and mind gone slack, I rode the current of the universe, and just letting my thoughts run ahead to the journey to come filled my chest to the brim.
