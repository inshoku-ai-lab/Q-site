# The Hot Spring in Tatopani Village

I decided to spend a night in Tatopani, the first village on the Nepali side, with the American traveler who had come down from Tibet with me.

We found a cheap guesthouse and took separate rooms.

According to the man at the guesthouse, Tatopani means "hot water" in Nepali, and true to the name, the village had a hot spring.

The fatigue from that breakneck run out of Tibet had been piling up, and a hot spring was more than welcome.

I dropped my bag in the room and headed for the public bath.

The spring sat near the middle of the village, and the villagers seemed to use it as a matter of daily routine.

The shared bath was free; the private baths cost money.

We paid the small fee and took a private one.

The difference between shared and private came down to an iron grate between two adjoining spaces, so there was no privacy to speak of, but at least we wouldn't have to soak packed in shoulder to shoulder, and in the state we were in that counted for a lot.

Men and women use it together, so I got into the tub with my underwear still on.

The water ran a little cool, the sort of temperature you can sit in for a long time. Thirty-nine degrees Celsius (about 102 Fahrenheit), maybe?

My American friend and I looked back on how rough the trip from Tibet had been.

The feeling was mutual, but the trouble was that I couldn't understand English.

About the only thing I did understand was that we both liked a punk band called Rancid.

When the talk turned to where we were headed next and I said I was going to Goa, in India, he said he was going there too.

He asked me, "How long will you stay?" — but I had no idea that "how long" was a question about time.

I understood "how" and I understood "long," so I explained the distance to Goa, and the conversation refused to line up.

In the end he gave up on finding out how long I was staying and changed the subject.

I didn't learn that "How long?" was asking about a span of time until a few months after that day.

While I was soaking there comfortably, a local older guy climbed in.

We could have sent him away, but it wasn't worth being stingy about, so we let him come in.

He made no attempt at conversation and just soaked.

After a while, another older guy got in.

Then an elementary school kid joined, and then a few of his friends.

By the end we had reached the same population density as the free bath, and there was no longer any point in the private one we had paid for.

It was too late to throw anyone out, so we gave up and settled in to enjoy the bath with the locals.

# The Children of Tatopani Village

Out of the bath, I wandered around the village.

I stepped into a shop to buy some snacks and was startled by how different it was from China.

Unlike in a communist state, the sheer number of things on the shelves was unreal.

As it happened this was also a village for trade with the Chinese side, so it probably carried more than other Nepali villages, but even so it felt like about five times the selection you would find in China.

If I had come into Nepal straight from Japan, the culture shock might have been capitalist poverty; coming from communist China, the culture shock was how rich Nepal looked.

For whatever reason, this village had an enormous number of children.

Life expectancy here is around fifty, about what Japan had in the Edo period, and since people die young they apparently marry young and have a lot of children.

The kids were all open and kind.

They must have been studying English in school, because they came up and spoke to me in simple English.

It was about the same level as my own, and we enjoyed our simple conversations.

Where are you from? What's your name? How old are you? Where are you going?

Nothing like this had ever happened to me with children I didn't know, not in Japan and not in China, and the warmth of it sank in.

My American travel companion was leaving for Kathmandu the next day, but I decided to stay on in that warm village and rest off the trip for a few more days.
