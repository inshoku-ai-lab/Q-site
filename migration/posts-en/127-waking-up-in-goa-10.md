## Renting a Motorcycle

As my circle of friends widened and the area we went out in widened with it, going on foot or by bus or taxi became inconvenient, and I started renting a motorcycle.

I didn't have much money to spare, so I shared one with Y to keep the cost down.

In India, Thailand, and places like them, you didn't really need a license to rent a motorcycle.

Not one person wore a helmet, either.

In Goa, you either handed the owner your passport or left a large sum of cash as security, and that got you the bike.

## Riding Without a License

The fact that nobody checked didn't make riding without a license legal, and if the police caught you, they could fine you.

In practice, though, almost every local Indian rode unlicensed and without a helmet, and some of the riders were children of about elementary-school age.

Illegal on paper as it was, it was far too ordinary a thing to collect fines over from locals and schoolkids, so the police naturally turned their aim toward foreign travelers.

They would flag down a foreigner riding a motorcycle and ask to see an international driver's license.

Almost no foreigner walked around carrying such a thing, so the fine applied.

But the officers' real aim wasn't the fine; it was the bribe they got for letting the fine slide.

This kind of bribe is called baksheesh, the same word used for alms, and in India it turns up in every situation imaginable.

The crackdown wasn't real police work in the first place — often enough it was an officer on his day off, in uniform, moonlighting for pocket money, with no legitimate authority to inspect anything.

And some of them weren't police or anything else: they put on a fake uniform and took the job of squeezing baksheesh out of travelers.

Behind it all, of course, there was the mafia, and turf money changed hands.

The mafia, in turn, was connected to the top of the police station, and would arrange for the low-ranking officers to stay out of certain areas.

## Who the Police Target

Police traps were set up all over the place like that, but the foreigners on long stays in Goa mostly had guts, and a cop trying to stop their bike didn't bother them.

They would slow down as if they were pulling over, and the moment they were right up on him, open the throttle wide and go tearing away.

Once they were clear, they never forgot to look back, put up a middle finger, and shout "F●ck you."

Hard-faced older white men were more than the police could handle, so instead they went after young Japanese who had just arrived in Goa for the first time and could barely speak English.

Which is to say, us.

Riding alone, if a cop had flagged me down, I would have stopped without fail, taken whatever bogus complaint he threw at me, and let him shake the baksheesh out of me.

But the one riding was Y, a former bōsōzoku.

He had built up a reflex of running at the sight of police, and the instant he spotted them he stopped the bike, made a U-turn, and ran.

Deeply reassuring.

The same situation came up several times, and every time we got away clean.

Several of my other Japanese friends were caught and shaken down for baksheesh, so I had reason to be thankful.
