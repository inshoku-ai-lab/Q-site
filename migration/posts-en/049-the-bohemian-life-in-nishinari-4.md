# Krautrock

G was a musician, so of course he knew his music, and the ground he knew best was the underground psychedelic rock scene of 1970s Germany — the one people call German prog, or krautrock.

The band he worshipped as a god was CAN, a group formed, so the story goes, when a Japanese traveler was caught making strange noises in a German park back in the sixties and got recruited as their vocalist.

Their radical approach to music holds up completely, even now.

https://youtu.be/8QgOGl5Oes0

CAN had been inactive for a long time by then, but right around that moment their vocalist, Damo Suzuki, came back for a limited run of shows, and G and his crowd went to the first one, got his autograph, and were beside themselves.

Because of G, the equation at that job was krautrock = music nobody knows about but that is undeniably cool, and it got to me hard.

When Faust, one of the heavyweights of krautrock, reunited and came all the way to Osaka, I went to see them play.

The rumors that had been going around for years were right: the set they laid out was staggering, and past the level of music as such, it put me through an overwhelming firsthand experience of sound.

A giant farm tiller was set up in the middle of the stage, and the show opened with the tiller under a spotlight, starting to turn, putting out a deep bass rumble.

Once the low, repeating rhythm of that vibration had carried into the crowd, the spotlights snapped onto the musicians and they began playing to the tiller's rhythm.

A giant farm tiller rotating sub-bass inside a cramped live house — one of those small Japanese music clubs — and the trance came on, like being churned around inside a washing machine.

https://youtu.be/eglDjuo9ZdU?t=83

The heavy, strong vibration hitting my body directly, the playing tuned so beautifully to that rhythm, the mass-hypnosis effect of the small room — it all came together into a strange, pleasant sense of oneness.

As the song built to its peak, sparks shot out from the stage.

They were on a scale that had no business existing in a basement club, reaching the seats and the ceiling.

Is this all right under the fire code? — that thought, and the momentum of a band vaulting clean over rules like that, made the show better still.

I realized that the rapture of a live show could change the way my body felt, and that music held the possibility of a trance experience, and it moved me to the core.

# Musical Awakening

Meeting G sharpened my musical antenna dramatically; the reception kept getting better.

What I found around this time was the underground avant-garde scene in Chicago — post-rock, or the Chicago sound school as it was called in Japan — one of the places part of krautrock's line of influence had finally arrived.

Tortoise was the representative band, and listening to their music set off a certain kind of musical change in me.

Their second album carries a mysterious title, *Millions Now Living Will Never Die*, which turned loosely into Japanese comes out something like "eternity is now, life goes on."

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gDt4_n6xP0M

Listening to those songs and understanding what was good about them, music I had played over and over without ever hearing anything in it suddenly started to sound alive.

Until then I had listened almost entirely to techno and rock and punk — music with an easy groove, the kind young people can get into right away.

Friends had recommended jazz, and classical music, and blues with real flavor to it, and difficult techno, and experimental music, and none of it ever quite clicked.

This Tortoise album was music that gathered the essence of all of that and carried it to the next dimension: through their songs with jazz in them I understood jazz, and through the fun of their experimental side I came to know experimental music.

My musical sense had crossed a critical point, so it wasn't only the difficult music — now I could take in, without prejudice, the appeal of pop songs and kayōkyoku, the old-fashioned Japanese pop I had never had the slightest interest in.

It was as if a musical awakening had happened to me: from that period on, the range of music I liked felt about twenty times wider, and I felt I understood music as a whole more deeply and was connected to it more strongly.

Looking back from where I am now, meeting Tortoise was what set my life as a musician in motion.

It wasn't starting to make music with my brother, and it wasn't starting to compose on my own on a computer — what gave me a reason to face music seriously was a deep understanding of music in general, and the awareness that came with it.
