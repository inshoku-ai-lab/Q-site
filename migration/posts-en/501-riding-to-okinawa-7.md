## Henro Pilgrims

I was cycling across Shikoku on my way to Okinawa, and I noticed that some of the travelers there had come for Shikoku alone.

They were henro pilgrims, making the henro pilgrimage, a circuit of eighty-eight temples around Shikoku.

I'd heard the word henro before, but I never imagined I'd see people actually doing it in this day and age.

Yet riding along the national highways, every now and then I'd pass someone on foot with a backpack.

At first I took them for ordinary pedestrians, but they kept turning up, and some were dressed like ascetic monks, so it dawned on me that these were henro pilgrims.

## A Comfortable Place

Could this long history of henro pilgrims be the answer to why Shikoku felt so comfortable to a traveler?

I turned the thought over as I pedaled on.

Sometimes, when I walked into a small restaurant, someone would ask, "Are you a henro pilgrim?"

When I said no, I was just traveling by bicycle, they were surprised.

That's how common pilgrims were, I guess, and how rare cyclists.

Some restaurants even gave pilgrims a discount, and some places let pilgrims rest for free.

## Kindness, Freely Offered

The respect and special treatment given to pilgrims reminded me of the customs in India.

In India, too, pilgrims and wandering ascetics get generous support.

Nobody mandates it, not the government and not religious institutions; ordinary people step forward and offer help of their own free will.

That kind of kindness, offered freely and on your own initiative to total strangers, has all but disappeared among people who live in cities.

But on Shikoku it was part of everyday life, and just coming into contact with it did something to heal the heart.
