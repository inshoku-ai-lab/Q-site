# The Ghats

Walking on from the Varanasi station along the Ganges, I came to a ghat that felt right.

A ghat is a flight of steps built down to the water, where Hindu pilgrims come to bathe, the local women do their laundry, and boats pick up tourists.

Near the ghat I found a cheap guesthouse that felt right too, so I stayed there.

# Cremation

The ghats serve a number of purposes, and burning the bodies of the dead is one of the important ones.

Hindus believe that dying near the Ganges lets you break out of the cycle of reincarnation, so a great many people come to Varanasi when they are close to death.

Burning the body on firewood afterward and letting the ashes go down the river is a religiously important rite, and as the Indians say, "The cremation ground is not there for the city — the city is there for the cremation ground."

True to that, the smoke from burning bodies never let up over the city, and depending on which way the wind blew, the smell of it filled the streets.

Have you ever smelled hair burning?

The same kind of smell comes drifting at you, thinned out by the wind.

Frankly, it is a genuinely nasty smell, but I decided to take it as holy smoke and let it pass.

# Filth

I am no Hindu at all, but having come to the Ganges, not bathing was not an option.

The sacred water of the Ganges washes the defilement from your soul.

The air was warm, and I had no hesitation about getting into the water.

My hesitation was about hygiene.

The water was as holy as water gets, and biologically as filthy as water gets.

The city's sewage surely does not drain into it, or so I assumed, but people with no home still relieve themselves on the bank.

People in the houses along the river throw out their leftovers, and people walking past drop their trash in.

On top of that, the ashes from burned bodies go into the water, and the bodies of infants and pregnant women are not burned at all but sunk into the river as they are.

The locals wash their bodies in that water, and their clothes.

![People in bright clothing washing at the water's edge on wide stone river steps, with buffalo standing behind them](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog94-1.jpg)

Worries like that made me afraid to bathe.

But after the flood in Calcutta, I had built up a little tolerance where hygiene was concerned.

I did not want to get used to filth, but having come to India, there was nothing to do but accept it.

# Bathing

I went down to the ghat, stripped to my underwear, did a few loose warm-up stretches, and walked into the Ganges.

There was no telling what was underfoot, so I kept my sandals on.

Once I cleared the hygiene worries out of my head, being in the river felt purely good.

I had been swimming an easy stroke for all of a moment when, by pure accident, a boat passing right beside me caught me on the head with its oar!

![Two rowing boats carrying several people silhouetted on a wide river at sunset, the sun low over the far bank](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog94-2.jpg)

The blow was not hard, but I was mid-stroke, and it threw me off balance.

I had just moved a little way out from the bank, my feet could not reach the bottom, and I panicked.

I scrambled back toward the bank, and somewhere in the middle of the panic I swallowed a little water.

What I had swallowed was not seawater and not chlorinated pool water — it was, of all the water in the world, the sacred water of the Ganges, with everything that goes into it.

The drowning lasted an instant, I was back on the bank right away, and my head was fine where the oar had hit it.

But I had swallowed the river.

Any way you looked at it, this was bad.

Not knowing what else to do, I rinsed my mouth with the mineral water I had on me and then drank a great deal more of it, the plan being to dilute the river water in my stomach.

Whether it was the plan working or plain luck, my stomach held for the next several days.

Parasites or amoebas may well have gotten into me, but no direct symptoms showed up, and I called that good enough.
