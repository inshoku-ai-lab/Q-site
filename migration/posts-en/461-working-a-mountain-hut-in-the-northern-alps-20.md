## Sharpening a Knife

J really did teach me from the absolute basics.

It's useful knowledge, so I'd like to pass some of it along.

The first thing he taught me was how to sharpen a kitchen knife.

The idea of sharpening a knife had never even crossed my mind, so this was extremely useful to know.

First you soak the whetstone thoroughly in water and lay a damp dishcloth under it so it won't slide.

Then, keeping a gap of about the thickness of a ten-yen coin (a millimeter and a half, or about a sixteenth of an inch) between the blade and the stone, you rub the knife across the stone, moving from the edge side toward the spine, and it comes out beautifully sharp.

You can tell whether the knife is sharp enough by running a finger sideways across the edge.

It has a rough, gritty feel.

## The Grip

Once the knife was sharp, the next thing he taught me was how to hold it.

I had never once thought about how I held a knife, so this surprised me too.

There are apparently all sorts of schools of grip, but the one he showed me as the beginner's grip was to rest the index finger on the spine of the blade and hold the handle with the other four fingers.

That way, he said, you can keep the knife pointed firmly where you want it and still put your strength into it.

After I'd learned the right grip, it was on to how to square up to the cutting board.

To work at cooking for long hours as a professional, you can't afford to get tired.

That meant thinking about everything from your posture while cutting to the height of the table.

I'm tall, so an average table feels a little low to me.

In that case, spreading your feet wider lowers your center of gravity and makes the work comfortable.

You can keep your back straight and put your strength in without straining.

## Cutting

There was technique to cutting, too.

To me, cutting meant bringing the knife down, but to a professional, a knife is something you slide.

It wasn't just straight down from above: you had to master pushing the blade away from you, or pulling it back toward you, or using both.

What I learned was the beginner's version, sliding the knife away from you.

Done that way, the cut surface isn't crushed but comes out clean, and the fibers stay fresh, so the flavor comes through clearly and the texture has a good bite to it.
