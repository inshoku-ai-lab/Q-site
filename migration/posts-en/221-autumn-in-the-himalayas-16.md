## Parvati Valley

Manali, where I was staying, is famous for its cannabis, but the title of India's greatest growing region has to go to the Parvati Valley.

It lies a few hours south of the town of Manali by bus, a valley with a great many villages scattered through it.

The valley takes its name from Parvati, the wife of Shiva, and it has become a holy place for the sadhus who follow him.

In general, mountains stand for Shiva's male aspect and valleys for the female.

Read as the yin and yang of a landscape, it makes sense to me.

The place produces top-grade hash, and enthusiasts gather here from all over the world.

The secret of that quality gets explained in any number of ways — that the valley runs east to west, which puts the sunlight on the plants at the ideal angle and makes for good cannabis; that it comes down to the family lines that have grown it here for hundreds of years, one generation after another; and then the humidity, the energy of Shiva, this and that — but I don't really know.

Still, its quality has made the name ring around the world, and in the coffee shops of Amsterdam — where smoking cannabis is permitted — hash from this valley trades at a very high price.

## Followers of Shiva

For the sadhus of Shiva, smoking hash out of a chillum is something close to a job, and the thinking is that by smoking hard from morning to night and staying stoned without a break, you can cut consciousness loose from the physical world and reach an awakening.

Because of that tradition among the sadhus, cannabis has effectively been legalized in the valley, and as long as you smoke within it, it almost never becomes a problem.

You can smoke cannabis in most of the restaurants, and along the roads, in the squares, and down by the river, sadhus have their chillums going.

And mixed in among them, naturally enough, are travelers who have gone deep into the worship of Shiva.

The travelers smoke chillums at the roadside, wave to the policemen passing by, drink chai with them, and then start smoking again.

The Parvati Valley was a holy place for the sadhus, and it was a holy place just as much for the travelers and hippies who followed Shiva.

As long as you showed respect to the local people you could do as you liked here, and complicated rules and regulations did not enter into it.

Being a hippie holy place in the Himalayas made it the same as Manali, but Indian tourists did not come to Parvati, so the air there was denser still, something particular that belonged to travelers alone.
