## What Else the Cops Were After

In India, driving without a license was so ordinary that arresting people for it was out of the question, so the charge worked as a bluff — a way to scare you into handing over baksheesh.

And the pretexts did not stop at driving without a license; if anything, the search for drugs was the real point.

Not, of course, because they had any interest in enforcing the drug laws.

Unlike an unlicensed driver, someone with drugs could genuinely be arrested, which meant they could demand a much bigger bribe.

Once you had actually been taken in, the price went up again, so anyone on the receiving end wanted to pay and be done with it as early as possible.

You kept all of that in mind as you dealt with the police.

The nastier ones brought drugs of their own and pretended the stash had come out of a traveler's bag while they searched it — plenty of men were low enough to take their baksheesh that way.

## Dealing with the Cops

Indians have some of the finest minds and deepest spirituality in the world, and, alongside that, plenty of them are like kindergarteners with their appetites right out in the open.

Broadly speaking, most cops were the second kind, which made every bribe a nakedly greedy transaction.

Being ruled by appetite like that made them animal-like, brimming with wildness. And like most animals, they went quiet when you acted strong and pushed harder when you acted weak.

Our weak spot was driving without a license and, on some days, having drugs on us.

Theirs was that the stop itself was illegal, that taking a bribe was illegal too, and that they had no power to arrest anyone.

In short, two sides with something to hide bluffed at each other, one trying to grab what it could and the other trying to get away clean, and that was the drama.

## Parties and Baksheesh

This corrupt little drama with the police was the tiresome side of Goa, and it was also the thing that made Goa Goa.

There was a big upside to it, too: for foreign travelers to take drugs the law forbade and have their fun at parties, police corruption was a requirement.

Travelers, police, mafia, and local businesses all wanted exactly the same thing, and police corruption was what kept it in place.

By now it has probably turned more commercial, with bigger mafia outfits and government authorities mixed in, but in those days most parties were put on by the DJs.

A DJ who got popular in Goa could go home and make a career off the title "back from Goa," so they were all racing to throw parties.

The recipe for a party back then was dead simple.

1 — The DJs get hold of a generator, an amp, and speakers.

2 — Set the speakers up anywhere that will do, jungle or beach or some empty lot nearby, without asking anyone's permission.

3 — Pay baksheesh to the neighbors who will have to put up with the noise.

The baksheesh in this case wasn't a bribe so much as alms — or turf money, maybe.

For the locals, one night of putting up with the noise meant cash in hand, and they were grateful for it.

4 — The DJs cover their costs by selling LSD to the travelers who turn up.

If you were lucky the police never came, and it ran until morning, or past noon, or, depending on the DJs' mood, until evening, or sometimes for about three days nonstop.

5 — If the police come, you pay baksheesh.

Small party, small amount; big party, big amount. Back then a small party cost about two thousand yen a visit — some sixteen dollars.

A shockingly small bribe, and yet it could come to several days' pay for a pair of cops.

Come morning they would be back, demanding more baksheesh.

If the DJs were flush they paid it themselves, and when that wasn't enough someone would carry a hat around the party, collect what people put in, and hand the lot to the police.

The cops took the money and left, and depending on how much it was, came back again after a while.

Then someone passed the hat once more, collected, and paid.

This went around a few times, and the party ended at the point where the amount of money and the mood of the police stopped matching up.

Paying bribes so a drug party can run through the night is an extremely dark piece of business, but there was no darkness in it at all — it had become an everyday sight in Goa.
