# The End of Living Alone

Once I decided to go to India, the preparations moved along smoothly.

I gave notice at work.

At Tsutaya the manager couldn't stand the slack, indifferent way I dealt with customers, and he'd been telling me all along,

"If you're that unmotivated, just quit."

So he was glad to take my resignation.

The job was never the point; the movies were, and I'd clung to it anyway, so my leaving must have come as good news to him.

He probably wanted the entire overnight crew gone.

My coworkers were glad about the road I was taking.

You'll manage somehow, so go and enjoy it for all it's worth, they said.

Out of the hundred-plus part-timers at Tsutaya I'd always been the youngest, and I think that made people take me lightly, but the decision to set out seemed to win me some recognition.

At the other place, the small video store, they were kind enough to be sorry to see me go, and told me to come back anytime.

Though it came with a disclaimer: the shop might not still be here by the time you come back.

I also got in touch with the older woman who managed my building.

She had a son about my age, apparently, and she had always looked out for me in small ways.

She was the only neighbor I ever said hello to.

In Kansai, when you rent a place you pay a security deposit on top of everything else — two months' rent — and if you leave the room clean you get the whole thing back.

The catch is that the money doesn't come until a good while after you've handed the room over.

I explained to the building manager that I was leaving for India and asked her to have the returned deposit wired to a particular account.

The pay from each job was the same story: I couldn't collect it yet.

# Money in 1998

I left in July 1998.

Computers back then were nothing like they are now.

I had no idea webmail like Hotmail existed, and this was before Gmail.

No one in my family used the internet, or email either.

There was no online banking, naturally, so collecting money later and spending it in India took a bit of a trick.

From what I could find out, the thing that worked best for travel in India was a kind of debit card issued by Citibank — a traveler's card, though I've forgotten what it was actually called.

Go to a Citibank branch or an affiliated bank in any major Indian city and you could withdraw cash from the ATM there.

These days you can pull money out of any ATM in town with the Visa or Mastercard attached to your bank account, but back then I don't think debit cards existed, and a freeter couldn't get a credit card.

I left my bank card with my mother and asked her, once the wages came through and the deposit came back, to transfer it all into the account behind my traveler's card.

Cash on hand: about eighty thousand yen, a little over six hundred dollars.

I decided to carry part of it in yen, part in U.S. dollars, and part in U.S.-dollar traveler's checks.

Traveler's checks were checks the banks issued back then for people on the road; they cost a fee up front, but they could be reissued if you lost them, which made them handy to carry.

Another part of the appeal was that exchange counters around the world gave a better rate on them than on cash.

I had no idea how much the pay and the deposit would come to, but I figured it would work out.

Peru, and then Thailand, had already taught me in my gut that things work out.
