## Haggling 2

I kept the exasperated act going and demanded half of the half he had offered.

And I still held the pose of a man about to stroll away.

Seeing that, the driver decided anything beat losing the fare, and named a figure a little above the midpoint between his price and mine.

I asked for a little below that, he came back a little above, I went a little below again, and after several rounds of the same exchange we settled at about a third of what he had first asked.

Of course I knew that figure was still higher than the going rate.

Even so, as a traveler in a foreign country, I was pleased to have talked it down to a third.

And the driver was pleased to have taken more than a quarter above the going rate.

Ignorance is bliss, and everybody wins.

This kind of haggling is a real hassle, and bargaining over what comes down to a single yen is completely absurd, but it also carries a heavy responsibility.

When travelers neglect the haggling and pay the asking price, prices across the town shoot straight up, and that destroys not only the travelers' society but the society of the locals along with it.

By bargaining properly down to the going rate, travelers keep a town's prices inside a normal range.

## Delhi's Cheap-Hotel Quarter

I climbed into the auto-rickshaw I had just settled with and headed for Paharganj, the street that was the center of traveler life in Delhi.

We reached the street, and as I got out I paid.

Having no coins, I handed over a bill worth a little more than the agreed amount, and, exactly as I had expected, he told me he had no change.

Pushing right up to the very last second — there is nothing to call that but India, and yet, out of respect for his passion for living, I let him keep the change as a tip.

Being the cheap-hotel quarter, Paharganj was overflowing with travelers from all over the world.

What made it different from Khao San Road in Bangkok, another cheap-hotel quarter, was that there were no tourists.

India's tourist industry back then hadn't developed the way Thailand's had, and it wasn't a country safe enough to sightsee in on a whim.

Everyone here was a traveler who had braced himself and dived into India, that country of chaos, with nothing but a backpack.

Every traveler I passed on the street was reveling in a freedom bought by shouldering all the risks of coming to India, and their eyes were bright and alive.

## Organic Filth

They were nothing like the people with dead-fish eyes I used to see on packed commuter trains in Japan.

Japan's cities: clean, but boxed in concrete, with no feeling of life in them.

Delhi, by contrast, overflowed with an organic filth, and dust rose in clouds off the unpaved roads.

Enormous cows strode down the street, fighting the stray dogs over scraps.

Cows and dogs alike dropped their dung and piss right where they stood on the road.

Once it dried, it was the raw material of the dust drifting in the air — part of the contents of every breath I took.
