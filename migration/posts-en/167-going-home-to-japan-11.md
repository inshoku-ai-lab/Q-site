## Boxed In

Not long after I started the scaffolding job, a feeling of being boxed in began to eat at me.

Right after I got back to Japan the days were good ones — delicious Japanese food, friends I hadn't seen in a long while, all the manga that had piled up while I was away — but once that first round of pleasure settled down, the reality of Japan started to come into view.

On the train, every face was pale, and everyone stood with their head down and the eyes of a dead fish.

They went through their days like zombies, without a trace of the glow of life anywhere on them.

Japan had not changed while I was traveling, of course; having stepped outside the frame and into a world full of completely different light, I was simply seeing the gray of Japan for what it was.

Until I left on the journey I had been sunk in that same environment myself, so the dead-fish eyes had been like air to me, but after a world as alive as India there was no unseeing how abnormal they were.

## The Gap

I had grown enormously through the journey and was overflowing with confidence, but in Japanese society I was a high school dropout who had fallen off the education track, a freeter, and no one there understood what I was worth.

I met up with the artist friends from Tsutaya, where I used to work, and tried to hand over the excitement of the road, but travel to them was one experience among many in a life, and my story of a life turned upside down by it spun its wheels and never got through.

The friends I had run around with in India were all from Kanto, the Tokyo side of the country, so there was no one nearby who shared the same sense of things, and a certain loneliness set in.

In India, a foreign country, I had met people I could call my soul family, relaxed all the way down and overflowing with life; that this kind of loneliness would find me in Japan, my own birthplace, in the very house I grew up in, had never once crossed my mind.

## The Frog in the Well

Just a few months on the road, and it was as if I had gone off into another world; the one I came back to was no longer the same one.

For a moment I thought it would have been better never to have known the world of travel at all, but having known it, I made up my mind: there was no living out my life pretending I hadn't seen it.

I knew the usual criticism aimed at people who travel, that they are escaping reality, but for me the real escapism would have been to live with my eyes turned away from the reality of travel now that I knew it, and having decided to face that reality instead, I was a pioneer and an adventurer going up against an unknown world.

The frog in the well who has learned there is an ocean can never go back down into the well again.

## One Way

At that party in Goa I had felt it — that once you stepped in, you could never get back to the world you came from — and that instinct, unfortunately, seems to have been right.

For better or worse, I understood all the way down that I could no longer go back into Japanese society.

Looking out at Japanese society with that boxed-in feeling gnawing at me, I got sick of how little life there was in any of it.

But everyone around me was buried inside that society, and there was no way to tell a single one of them what I felt.

In the end the pent-up feeling turned into anger — how is it that not one Japanese person has ever taken LSD? — and then into: how can you stay that sober and do nothing but work, every day of your life?

Completely unreasonable anger, but that is how badly the gap with Japanese society was hurting me.

Then again, I had not the slightest intention of bending myself to fit Japanese society, so there was no helping it.
