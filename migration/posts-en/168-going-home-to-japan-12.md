## The Letter

Not long after I started the scaffolding job, a letter came from a girl I had met in Goa, India.

Back then, email still wasn't a normal thing.

What travelers traded with each other wasn't email addresses or Facebook accounts, but the address of your family home and the phone number that went with it.

The letter had come addressed to the family home address we'd traded.

I-chan was a year older than me and lived in the same city, and I met her and her friend just after I got to Goa, but they left for another town almost immediately, and we never really got to know each other.

Still, since we came from the same city, we said it would be good to meet up back in Japan, traded addresses, and parted ways.

The letter that arrived was written in silver pen on solid black drawing paper, and it looked stylish.

It said something like, if you're back in Japan, let's hang out, and it came with a purikura photo — one of those little photo-booth stickers.

Getting a letter from a girl was a first for me, so it left me a little embarrassed and a little happy at the same time.

And there was something in it that was playing up her girlishness, which got my attention.

## Something Shared

Her cell phone number was in the letter, so I called, and she was glad to hear from me.

We decided to get tea somewhere, and set a day.

Her place was about twenty minutes by bicycle from my workplace, not far at all, so I found an opening and went to see her.

We had only spent about two days together in India and hadn't talked about anything much, but we could share the feel of traveling there, and the conversation took off.

Both of us had run into the wonder of India and the free air of the traveler's world while we were still young, but back in Japan there was no one around us who could share that, and even at home, surrounded by family, we each carried a certain sense of isolation.

We complained to each other about how closed-in Japan felt, lamented a society stiff with rules, and felt sorry for the people on the trains with the eyes of dead fish.

We confirmed for each other that we weren't the only ones who felt this way, and we were glad to have someone who shared the same sense of things.

After that we kept meeting regularly and spending time together.

I had started working, so I had little spare time and felt the stress of it, but I-chan, on savings from her years as an office lady and the ease of living with her parents, was getting by comfortably without a job.

Maybe that gap in how much slack we each had was actually a good thing.

We became a comfort to each other, and over time we came to love each other.
