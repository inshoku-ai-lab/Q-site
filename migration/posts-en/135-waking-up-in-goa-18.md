## Down Syndrome

The food was what pulled us in, obviously, but the family feel of the place was part of the draw too.

The father was the chef, the mother backed him up, and when the reliable second son — nine years old at the time — was home, he was the one who came to take our order.

The eldest son, A-kun, had Down syndrome, and whether it came from growing up free in all that light, or from a name that means the sun is shining, I couldn't say, but there was no bottom to his cheerfulness.

He was full of happiness all the time, and whenever we turned up he came bounding over, delighted, scattering smiles, hello hello.

I think his Down syndrome was fairly severe. He lived completely in a world of the senses, and socially he could do almost nothing at all.

In another country he might have been put in an institution, but here there was nothing from the state, and in exchange for that, A-kun lived entirely free.

He had become something like the restaurant's mascot, and a lot of the customers came looking forward to seeing him.

I suppose it just feels good to watch someone raised that free and that bright.

## A Journey Into the World of the Senses

The other side of that freedom was that he also got up to some outrageous things.

In Goa there's a superstition that a restaurant fills up if you play Bob Marley, so this place ran a Bob Marley greatest-hits album on repeat, always.

One of the tracks is "Buffalo Soldier," and at the top of it there's a ta-ta-tan, that magic figure particular to reggae that reaches out and pulls the listener in.

https://youtu.be/S5FCdx7Dn0o

For A-kun, living in the world of the senses, that ta-ta-tan worked as a kind of magic trigger, hauling him into the world of the music.

The instant the sound rang out he arched backward, and as the song came in he pitched forward, then rocked back and forth in time with the guitar's beat, slow and violent at once.

We called this kamigakari, possession by a god, and once A-kun was in that state there was no handling him.

Caught in the world of the music, past all judgment, he would wander the restaurant wherever his heart took him.

If he went outside and put some distance between himself and the speakers, he settled down, and something else would catch his interest and wake him up.

Sometimes where his heart took him was a table of customers in the middle of their meal, and that caused serious trouble.

If the family was nearby they came rushing out to pin him down, but when nobody was around, it fell to us regulars to hold him down.

That a scene that strange was simply accepted is part of why the place felt so comfortable to us.

## The Wait

Big portions at a reasonable price, fresh ingredients, good food — we hung around there constantly.

We hung around not only because the food was good and the place was comfortable; the bigger reason was the absurd length of the wait.

The chef's cooking was first-rate, no question, but prep work was not his strength, and the wait for a dish to come out was not normal.

An hour was standard, and being made to wait two or three hours was not rare.

Go as a group of five and it could easily run to four hours.

Add a shopping run into town on top of that, and you were waiting a serious stretch before the food came out.

## Daifugō

We didn't just sit there waiting; we always carried a deck of cards, and we played daifugō, the Japanese card game known elsewhere as President.

Drinking chai, drinking shakes, eating sweets, we played.

We played daifugō and only daifugō, on and on.

I have no memory of playing any other game.

That you can keep playing it and never get bored must be what makes daifugō the game it is.

Spending hours on cards might look, in a way, like a waste of time.

But you grow closer to friends you've shared the emotional ups and downs with over a game like that, and for building a friendship, a deck of cards is the finest tool there is.

For us, friendship mattered more than anything.
