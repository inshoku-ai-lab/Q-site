## E

J came back from the clinical-trial job in Germany, and what he brought back with him was E, who was thriving in the transgender show business world of Berlin.

The gay and transgender world holds all sorts, and the line blurs between calling someone "-kun," the suffix you put on a boy's name, and "-chan," the one you put on a girl's — but in E's case it was "-kun."

He was the first transgender person I had ever met, and the sheer force of his personality floored me.

He was tall and beautiful, a long-haired, handsome man like Ushiwakamaru, the boy warrior out of the old tales — and to look at, entirely male.

And yet the gestures, the fine-grained attentiveness, the speech: entirely female.

And in character he was manly — the big-sister sort who takes everyone under her wing, ambitious, thriving in Berlin's show business world.

Generous, gutsy, deeply responsible, good at looking after people, a manly character in the mold of an outlaw's code of honor — and against it the gestures of a Japanese woman and the face of a long-haired, handsome man: the two sides contradicted each other and yet fell into perfect harmony, and the result gave off a strange charm.

## Berlin's Transgender Scene

Berlin, it seems, has a well-developed transgender culture, and transgender people who are not at ease in their own countries gather there from all over the world.

E was no exception — in Berlin, however striking the way you dress, whatever your sex and whatever your lifestyle, you are accepted as an ordinary citizen.

It must be that largeness of heart, the way Berlin takes even the extraordinary as ordinary, that draws the misfits in.

E had teamed up with another transgender Japanese man, and the two of them apparently did dance performances in nightclubs.

His dance partner already looked like a woman, so the two of them together may have come across as a man-and-woman couple.

Going by my own assumptions, I pictured strip routines, but no — apparently it was tap dancing and the like, legitimate show business in its way.

They were popular enough, had regular work, and had a certain number of fans.

In my head the image that had settled was of the okama — the crude Japanese word for a flamboyant gay man — the way Japanese television shows them, so okama registered with me as perverts, freakish and dangerous, but all of that, it turned out, was staged for the camera.

E had a powerful individuality and a sexuality outside the ordinary, but none of that made him dangerous or broken; he was living his life, strictly as one human being.

Through this meeting, becoming ordinary friends with a transgender person cleared away the image television had brainwashed into me, and after that I could relate to people one to one, human being to human being.

Meet them in the flesh and they are all just people.
