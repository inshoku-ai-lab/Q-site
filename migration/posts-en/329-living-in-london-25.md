## Looking Inward

Everyone was enjoying the music and dancing, and at the same time, within the closed space of the club, each of us was facing what was inside.

My journey through my own inner world was about the dream of being a musician.

Back when I was living in Japan, I had made music seriously, the whole time.

I wanted to make it as a techno artist eventually, and once my first journey was over, I meant to put everything into making music.

But the current of the road carried me along, and the traveling never stopped.

These days a few tens of thousands of yen — a few hundred dollars — buys a laptop that handles serious music production, and you can make music while you travel, but given the state of computers back then, traveling with a laptop you could actually make music on was not realistic.

To make music seriously, you had to stay put somewhere and face a computer.

That was part of why I had wanted to settle in London and throw myself into the club music scene there.

Being active in the scene itself, I believed, was what led to making good music.

## The Scene

But when I stood back and looked down on where I was right then, thoroughly enjoying this party, the DJs holed up inside the little box of a club, drunk on fame in a tiny world, looked utterly absurd.

As someone who had cut loose in Goa in India for as long as I had, the neat little stylishness of London's clubs struck me, if anything, as lame.

What on earth did I think was waiting on the other side of musical success? And so I was left face to face with my own dream.

Why did I want to make music in the first place?

Was it fame I wanted?

And just what was musical success?

## Fishmans

While those thoughts were going around in my head, the lyrics of "Atarashii Hito" ("A New Person"), a song by Fishmans, a Japanese band I love, came flowing in.

"What is music

supposed to ring out for?

In a world as quiet as this,

to the people whose hearts tremble,

to the one waiting for a letter,

I wish it would reach them."

So that was it — Shinji Sato of Fishmans had been through the same kind of thing!

That song had come out the other end of the same struggle! — and all sorts of things fell strangely into place.

That day marked the line — the way I faced music changed for good.

The essential relationship between me and music, and the relationship between the music I sent out and the one who heard it, and how that sound made people's hearts tremble.

Musical success and fame in the sense people generally mean stopped interesting me entirely, and that essential part of music was planted at the center of who I was.
