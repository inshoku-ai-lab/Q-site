## E's Melancholy

E was a sensitive woman with an artist's temperament.

Her strong point showed up as focus, her weak point as how hard she latched onto an idea.

And LSD amplified both.

She had a precarious side to her: on LSD she latched on even harder, and once she fixed on something she would sink into a daydream for a good while.

We walked along the beach, wandering in search of a party that might be going on somewhere.

Not that we were desperate about it; we were just knocking around, high on LSD.

Coming out onto the road, we ran into a good-sized cow.

Running into a cow in India is like running into someone wearing glasses in Japan — nothing unusual at all — but this one came at us and jabbed a little aggressively.

It wasn't out to kill anyone, but when a cow is that big, even a shake of the head to warn you off has real force.

The one unlucky enough to get jabbed was E.

She was only pushed lightly and startled, not hurt, but the jab caught her while the LSD trip already had her turning inward, and the shock pushed that brooding into overdrive.

It started with "why is it always me," and moved on to the unfairness of society, the anxiety about whether she could adjust once she went back to Japan soon, her relationship with her boyfriend S, the shock of what C had told her — deeper and deeper into it she sank.

We made a point of talking to her, keeping her from sinking too far into her own thoughts.

## C's Theory

What E had heard from C was a fairly crazy theory, and in C's telling it went like this.

Humanity is locked inside everyday consciousness, cut off from its true power and from free thought.

Governments regulate the drugs that awaken consciousness, LSD among them, so that people don't wake up.

Experiencing every drug there is means experiencing every kind of consciousness, which widens your view and sets your consciousness free.

So take every drug you can get, as much as you can — that was the theory.

There were parts of it I could go along with, but I had disliked C from the start, so I had told E to be careful.

## A Bar Late at Night

After walking the road for a while, we heard music somewhere off in the distance.

Closer up, there was a bar still open, Bob Marley's reggae coming out of it.

This place, unfortunately, looked like it was about to close too, but they said we could stay on the sand inside the bar's grounds, so we ordered chai on last call and settled in for a rest.

Going to a bar and ordering chai instead of a drink is about as Indian as it gets.

A few other customers were still around, and a little way off a Black man and a white woman were all over each other.

We took our seats and started rolling a joint.

Cannabis is illegal in India to begin with, and smoking it in a bar is not exactly permitted either.

But Goans are loose about these things, and smoking a joint in a bar or restaurant that caters to foreigners rarely causes any trouble.

If the police come and arrest you, that is on you, but the bar has no interest in any of it.

The chai arrived, and we lit the joint.

As the joint went around our circle, an older Indian man joined in.

Sharing joints is common among travelers in India, and if you are in the circle, the joint comes to you.

Indians who want a smoke take advantage of that, dropping into a circle out of nowhere all the time.

They will share a joint, but another person's saliva is unclean as they see it, so they pinch it between two fingers and smoke through them, keeping it off their mouth.

This older guy who had suddenly joined our circle was no ordinary man.
