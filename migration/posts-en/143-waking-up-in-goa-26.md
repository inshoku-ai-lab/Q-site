## Goa Gil

The way Goa Gil DJed at this party was nothing like the DJing people picture today.

There was no DJing off a laptop back then, and no CDJs — the CD decks — either.

That hardly meant that traveling DJs coming in from all over the world hauled heavy vinyl records into the swirling dust of Goa.

So the traveling Goa trance DJs of that time played off DAT, digital audio tape.

A square plastic shell a little smaller than a cassette, magnetic tape inside, about an hour of CD-quality recording.

It could be copied, it could be recorded over, it sounded good, and at the time it was the newest gear you could get.

This was not an age when anything you wanted turned up online, and recordings as niche as Goa trance were hard to get hold of.

Which is why the traveling DJs were so frantic about their track-swapping sessions.

Goa Gil recorded his DATs in advance, sets of about three tracks mixed together perfectly, and roughly once every twenty minutes he would work the mixer and bring in the next set.

The sound on a DAT cannot be sped up or slowed down, so the joins sometimes came out a little forced, but a man with that much gravitas and charisma simply pushed through, and you came away convinced that a wobble at the seam did not matter.

And it was precisely because those joins came only once every twenty minutes that he could keep going for hours.

## The Ritual of Awakening

His DJing did not put much weight on the things ordinary DJs worry about, technique or the latest cutting-edge tracks.

What mattered more was the mood of the room, the harmony of the whole, and the reassurance and trust that however wrecked you got, you would still be accepted.

That was exactly why it mattered for one DJ to play for hours on end, weaving a single story across twenty-four hours.

That style of DJing, watching over us with love, created an air of unity in the place, something that made us think of each other as kin.

The tracks he played had character and range, every one of them good, every one of them getting us dancing without fail, and you could feel the practiced hand behind it.

He knew exactly which track at which moment would make the whole party react which way, and he had the floor completely in hand.

Almost everyone in the crowd was wrecked out of their minds on LSD — a very delicate, precarious state.

His position: to guide people in that state, to watch over each person's own awakening through LSD and Goa trance.

Our trust in that position sent us deeper and higher into the trip.

For him, DJing was a ritual of awakening, and he fulfilled the role of the shaman presiding over it.
