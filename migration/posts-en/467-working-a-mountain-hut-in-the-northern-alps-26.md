## Nap Time

It takes a while for the pipe to reach all the way up to the hut.

Laying seven hundred meters (2,300 feet) of it while climbing a mountain is no easy job.

They said it would take about two hours, so I ate the rice balls I'd brought along and settled in for a nap.

J had already told me the best spot for one.

A five-meter (sixteen-foot) boulder sits in the middle of the valley with a clear view, and when you lie down on top of it, you're looking straight up at the summit.

After all that hard labor, stuffing your face with rice balls and dozing off under a blue sky with the summit in view feels wonderful.

## The Walkie-Talkie

I was stretched out comfortably when a call came in over the walkie-talkie.

It was the signal to start the engine, run the pump, and send the water up to the hut.

I'd written down everything they taught me in a notebook, so the job went off without a problem.

Driven by the pump, the water surged up toward the hut.

This part also took about two hours, which meant it was nap time again.

I went back to the rock and lay down, checking on the pump now and then and waiting for the next call.

## Mission Accomplished

Once the tank at the hut was full, another call came in over the walkie-talkie.

As instructed, I shut off the engine and hid the pump and engine behind a rock where no one would see them.

That, more or less, was how the *mizuage* mission was accomplished.

Living in a city, you never give a thought to where your water comes from, or what it goes through before it runs out of the tap.

But actually drawing water from the source and pushing it up the mountain with a pump gave me a far more grounded feeling.

How the water gets here, goes into my body, and keeps me alive.

Maybe it was the mountain hut itself, a life lived so close to nature and with so little in the way of things, that let me feel that firsthand.
