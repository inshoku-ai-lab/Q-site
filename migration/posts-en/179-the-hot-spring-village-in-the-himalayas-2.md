## The Japanese Guesthouse

I followed along behind K, the traveler I had just met on his way out of the bath.

We went on along a narrow stone-paved mountain lane scattered with cow dung, and the guesthouse was at the end of it.

My friend N had gone out somewhere and wasn't there, but I had to have a bed for the night either way, so I decided to stay at the same place.

The building stood on the mountainside, which made for a beautiful view: the mountain facing us across the river, and the ranges visible far downstream.

## The Sacred Hot Spring

Once I had set down my pack and caught my breath, I headed straight for the spring.

According to K there was a rule that you must never be completely naked, and that one, at least, was worth keeping.

The baths were divided by sex, but the custom seemed to be that men and women alike kept their underwear on.

Two minutes' walk from the guesthouse brought me to the temple that held the spring.

The temple was dedicated to a sage named Vashisht, with small shrines standing in rows.

A few years after this I found out he is a fairly famous sage.

Sages and shrines held little interest for me, so I gave them no thought at all and went to the room with the bath.

Partitioned off by walls of brick and stone, the bath room had no ceiling, which gave it the feel of an open-air bath.

Stone statues that had stood there since ancient times were set around the source where the water spilled out, and the whole place had a fine atmosphere, traditional and mysterious.

## Scalding Water

The water pouring out was a good deal hotter than I had imagined — forty-five, maybe fifty degrees Celsius (113 to 122 Fahrenheit).

Water from the source ran into the tub and out of the tub through a pipe, and the system seemed to be that you washed yourself with what came out of the pipe before getting in.

The same style as Japan, which pleased me.

I took off my clothes, stripped to my underwear, and went to the washing area.

Getting my body used to water that hot took a while, but a body born and raised in Japan does seem to be built so that the moment hot water touches it, it relaxes on its own and a smile comes up.

I rinsed off and got into the tub.

The water was hot enough that I could only lower myself in a little at a time, but by degrees I got used to it and sank all the way in.

The long sigh that comes out when you get into a bath arrived through clenched teeth instead, the water being too hot for anything else, but even so, nothing else can replace the pleasure of soaking.

Sitting in the water looking at the mossy stone walls and the mountains and sky off in the distance, the feeling of being on the road came welling up and left me deeply satisfied.

Between my last time in India and coming back now, I had spent about four and a half months in Japan, working, but my heart had stayed in India the whole time, and the moment that traveling feeling came back I was already connected to where the last journey had left off.

This journey was not a new one; it lay on an extension of the last, part of the same organic whole.
