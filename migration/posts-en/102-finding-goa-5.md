# Moving

Y figured that being at Anjuna Beach would lead him to more information, so he moved back there.

I had paid my rent in advance at the guesthouse on Calangute Beach, so I stayed where I was.

After a few days alone — quiet, but dull — Y showed up at my place out of nowhere.

He had gone on with his research on the ground, and he had established that everything interesting was happening in Anjuna, not Calangute.

Calangute had been interesting up until fifteen years ago; now it was a resort for rich old tourists.

The monsoon wasn't what made it dull. It was a resort for old people, and the monsoon on top of that made it duller still.

Goa's history goes like this: the flow of travelers began in the south, and the villages that grew because travelers gathered there turned into resorts.

Once a beach had turned into a resort and filled with rich old people, the travelers found it boring and gathered in a village farther north.

Then that northern village grew too, turned into a resort, and became boring.

Then farther north again — and that, apparently, has been going on for more than thirty years.

These days, he said, Anjuna, or Vagator a little farther north, was where things were liveliest.

Which meant I had gone and found myself a room in Calangute, a little behind the times.

In Anjuna, heading toward Christmas in December, events were piling up and things were about to get interesting.

So move to Anjuna now, Y said.

When I hemmed and hawed about having already paid the rent and so on, he told me to throw that away, that it was worth coming right now, and that I should at least ask whether I could get a refund.

His pushiness and my adaptability are a good match.

I took his idea and went to negotiate a refund with the owner of the guesthouse.

He couldn't return the full amount, unfortunately, but he could give back about eighty percent.

That was good enough for me, and I decided to move to Anjuna.

### To Anjuna

The next day I packed my things and left the house in Calangute.

Changing buses along the way, I headed for Anjuna.

When the bus pulled in, the restaurant where M worked was right there in front of it.

M and the others were in the same seats as always, smoking hand-rolled cigarettes over a game of backgammon.

Next to M, besides Y, was another Japanese man.

This was R, twenty-eight, who had shared a room with M in Anjuna eight years earlier.

Back then, apparently, even M had money for rent.

They had lived together through six months of monsoon, and you could see the deep friendship in it.

This was R's second time in Goa, so he had all kinds of things to tell us, but his last stay had been in the off-season, so he knew nothing about the high season we wanted to hear about.

What he had instead was useful local knowledge: which motorcycle shop was cheap and trustworthy, which money changer had the best rate, where the curry was good and where to get a good lassi.

I asked R what his stay in Goa eight years ago had been like.

"Ah, back then — see, I was hooked on heroin, so I just stayed inside the whole time..."

R let something outrageous slip in a completely breezy tone.

Back then it hadn't been only M and R; there had been an Englishman as well, and the three of them spent the six months of monsoon hooked on heroin.

"I was going to travel, but I got hooked, you know."

Still perfectly light about it.

The three of them had lived quietly and plainly, more or less the way the local people did.

Which, it seemed, was exactly why he knew such useful things about the area.

The way R told it, there are people who get hooked on anything, and he was precisely that type — he got hooked on everything.

Which was why, this time, he was steering completely clear of heroin, he said.

R had a dark chapter of heroin addiction behind him, but he was so easygoing and warm that I had the feeling we would be friends fast.
