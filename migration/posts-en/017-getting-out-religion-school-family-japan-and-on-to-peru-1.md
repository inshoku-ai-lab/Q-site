# Dropping Out of High School Becomes Real, a Manifestation

In the second half of summer vacation during my second year of high school, I finally managed to leave the Jehovah's Witnesses, something I had wished for so long.

At long last, I had gotten the freedom from the Jehovah's Witnesses I had spent years dreaming about.

Now I no longer had to go to the Jehovah's Witness meetings that had been so painful they gave me nervous diarrhea before I'd even left the house.

I was free, too, from putting on a suit and tie and going door to door spreading the truth.

Released from stress that had been building up for years, I was left in a daze.

A floaty, hollow feeling.

The framework I had lived inside until then had crumbled away, and a new way of seeing things was coming into view.

Summer vacation was drawing to a close, and the second term would start up again in September.

I hadn't done any of my homework, and had no motivation to.

September came, and with it the day of the opening ceremony, but I felt no desire whatsoever to go to school, so I skipped it.

I skipped the next day too.

And the day after that.

After a few days of this, my mother, worried, started telling me to go to school.

A few days after school had started, things gradually became clear to me.

I didn't want to go to school.

A too-obvious voice inside me.

For me, this high school was something I didn't need at all.

I didn't want a job where having a high school diploma would actually matter.

I wanted to go into the film industry, or the comedy industry.

I wanted to quit high school early and live in a world where honing my sensibility would actually count for something.

The only reason I had been going to school at all was to fit myself into the framework my parents and society were forcing on me.

Now that I had quit the Jehovah's Witnesses, my parents' pressure had lost its footing, leaving only society's pressure as a reason I should go to high school.

Freed from stress and left in a daze, even that pressure from society no longer meant anything to me.

Every single person in my family said the same thing: "At least go to high school, for now."

But none of their arguments did anything more than push society's common sense on me — none of it stirred up any actual desire in me to go to school.

I had no hesitation and no fear.

I had my mother call the school, and we sat down for a three-way meeting with my homeroom teacher.

My mind was made up, and the meeting ended as soon as I had said my piece.

Even my homeroom teacher couldn't say anything to shake my resolve.

With that, I had officially escaped the school I never wanted to go to in the first place.

Two weeks earlier, quitting high school hadn't even crossed my mind, but once the idea occurred to me, deciding and acting on it took no time at all.

An obvious fact, beyond any doubt.

I didn't want to go to school.

# Time on My Hands and Unease

I had quit the Jehovah's Witnesses and quit high school, but my mother never followed through on the threat she'd been making all along to throw me out of the house.

In the end, she was still my mother.

She might have said the words, that she'd kick me out, but there aren't many mothers who can actually go through with kicking their own child out.

I had braced myself to be thrown out and end up homeless, so the peaceful outcome caught me off guard.

My plan to live as a homeless criminal had suddenly vanished, and now, still living at home, I had nothing to do.

Until then, school, homework, and Jehovah's Witness activities had eaten up nearly all my time, so now that I had suddenly quit both high school and the religion and found myself with a huge amount of time on my hands, I had no idea how to use it.

I kept up my part-time job stocking shelves at the supermarket near my house, so I still had some kind of foothold in society, at least.

I would go to work, then stop by the bookstore afterward to browse the books without buying anything.

They were idle days, but I think it was time I needed in order to come to terms with such a dramatic change in my life.

But from my mother's point of view, she was apparently quite angry that I had quit the most important thing, Jehovah's Witness activities, only to kill time working a part-time job and browsing bookstores.

By this point, my mother and I had stopped talking almost entirely.

We each carried our own frustration, anger, and anxiety, and kept the peace only by not talking to each other.
