# Counterculture

Two of the clerks I worked the late shift with at Tsutaya had real charisma.

One of them, M, knew subculture inside out, and the counterculture of the sixties and seventies most of all.

The most interesting part was hearing how the hippie movement of the sixties went on to shape the music industry and the film industry.

The way M told it, the worldwide counterculture that broke out in the sixties left its mark on a huge amount of the music, film, fiction and manga that came after, and its effect on society today is just as large.

The culture never came into Japan directly; it was imported through the commercial filter of the media, worn down into something trite, so we don't know it for what it is — but this and that, he said, all of it traces back to the culture of that time.

He shared what he knew and what he had lived through inside Tsutaya, and it left its mark on people.

The unfamiliar culture he kept introducing me to, old and new at once, set my intellectual curiosity going hard.

Take the sensibility I already had, extend it straight out, and at the end of that line stood the counterculture he was showing me.

Counterculture: a culture that sets itself against the mainstream, traditional one.

A proper explanation would mean far more information than fits here, so for the specifics I'll defer to

[Professor Wiki](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/1960%E5%B9%B4%E4%BB%A3%E3%81%AE%E3%82%AB%E3%82%A6%E3%83%B3%E3%82%BF%E3%83%BC%E3%82%AB%E3%83%AB%E3%83%81%E3%83%A3%E3%83%BC)

and leave it at that.

The big media never pick it up, so it isn't widely known, but the counterculture of that era has been handed down, unbroken, all the way to the present.

# Goa

That culture had my interest, and without really understanding it, I loved the music of those years.

Whenever the talk turns to it, M says the same thing.

"You're interested in this culture, but you haven't got hold of the part that matters. Frankly, you haven't woken up yet."

I ask, "Then how do I get that awakening, the part that matters?"

He says, "Take LSD. The counterculture of the sixties happened because the public took LSD. Talking about sixties culture when LSD hasn't opened your brain is putting the cart before the horse."

Me: "I want to, but I have no connection to get any, and when I went to Koh Phangan I let the chance slip."

Him: "I've done it and I have the connections, but you're still too young. Giving you an LSD experience means taking on the responsibility for changing your life at the root, and I can't carry that."

Me: "Then what do I do?"

Him: "Go to Goa, in India. Go there and travelers from all over the world are gathered, playing with LSD. Get yourself there on your own two feet and take it on your own responsibility."

Me: "Goa..."

"Go to Goa, in India" — for me those words were a revelation.

I didn't decide to go to Goa on the spot, but with the travelers I'd met on Koh Phangan also in the picture, Goa became a place I wanted to see someday.

Goa is a beach resort on India's Arabian Sea coast, and it began in the early sixties, when Western travelers fleeing a civilization that had started to go insane gathered there and settled.

It is still called one of the holy places of the hippies, and travelers come there from all over the world.

What I'd heard was that dance parties ran all year round to a lyrical kind of music called Goa trance — four-on-the-floor techno with Eastern-sounding synthesizer laid over it — and that people with every restraint gone took LSD and other psychedelics and danced themselves wild on the beach.

For me — just getting into hippie culture, in love with techno, curious about LSD, and wanting to travel to India before too long — it sat dead center in my curiosity.
