## The Newcomers

The two summer part-timers were excellent: serious, hardworking, easy to get along with, and cheerful.

Back at the big hut, where I'd been before coming to this small one, I'd been warned that the part-timers fought among themselves every year, and that we'd have to get through the busy season under a heavy load of stress.

At the small hut where I was, though, that kind of infighting seemed like pure fiction, and day after day we got to work in a happy, friendly atmosphere.

Part of that was probably the manager's personality, part of it was that the hut was small, and maybe we just got lucky with the people.

Either way, good relationships, and physical work in a good environment, made the mountain-hut job all the more rewarding.

## The Busy Season

You might picture work at a mountain hut as a slow, easy life on top of a mountain, but the hut I worked at, at least, was seriously busy.

Part of that was exploitation by a profit-first president, but even apart from that, the sheer amount of work at a mountain hut is on another level from convenient life in the world below.

In town, you flip a switch and the light comes on, but in the mountains, gasoline has to be flown in by helicopter before anyone can run the generator.

And running the generator isn't just flipping a switch, either: you check the engine oil, then yank the pull cord again and again until the engine starts.

Everything went like that, and just meeting the basic needs of daily life took an enormous amount of work.

On top of that, climbers came to stay the night.

Taking care of ourselves already ate up a huge amount of time, and we also had to look after dozens of guests.

No wonder we were busy.

The stretch from Marine Day, a national holiday that fell on July 20 back then, to the Obon holiday on August 15 was especially hellish.

At our hut, at least, there was no stress between people, and that was one small mercy.
