# My First Experience Abroad

At the Lima airport, my sister was waiting, as planned.

She was glad I had made it safely, and welcomed me warmly.

There were no smartphones or internet back then, so we had arranged the meeting through international calls that cost something like a hundred yen a minute (about a dollar).

If some accident had meant we could not meet where we had arranged, I wonder what I would have done.

From the airport I took a bus toward Huancayo, the town where my sister was living, neighboring the capital, Lima, two hundred kilometers (125 miles) away.

My body was weak and my constitution delicate, and carsickness and seasickness hit me hard.

Lima and Huancayo are both in the Andes, so the road had to run through the mountains.

It did not twist violently, but it wound enough to make anyone unused to it carsick.

Half in tears from the carsickness, I finally reached the house where my sister lived.

A long, long, lo-o-ong journey.

How long it took I do not clearly remember, but all told I think it was three or four days.

![A bare mountain range in warm sunset light, a thin road winding through golden foothills](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog19-1.jpg)

For someone with no life experience whatsoever, only just starting life over, it was fairly stimulating.

The fact that I had gone to Peru alone and arrived safely gave me a great deal of confidence in my ability to live.

# My Sister's House in Huancayo

The long trip from Japan was over, and at last I had arrived at my sister's house.

A kitchen, a living room, a bedroom.

I shared the bed with my sister.

She had only just moved there herself, so the place did not feel lived in.

Her husband-to-be apparently lived nearby.

The shower was cold.

A culture gap this soon made me recoil.

I wanted a shower to wash off the fatigue of the long trip, but for the moment I put up with it.

In Japan I had been a sensitive boy, borderline germophobic, who shampooed his hair first thing in the morning, so not being able to take a warm shower easily every day was quite a torment.

Here it was standard to go to a public sauna, and there was apparently no culture of hot showers or bathtubs.

More than the differences of culture or language, what hit me hardest was the difference in altitude.

The town of Huancayo sits partway up the Andes, at an altitude of 3,350 meters (11,000 feet).

The eighth station up Mount Fuji is at 3,400 meters (11,200 feet), which should give a sense of how high that is.

Perhaps because of the altitude, the cold in the mornings and evenings was harsh too.

The air was thin as well, and in the early days simply getting through an ordinary day left me short of breath, and walking even a little winded me.

Unused to the thinness of the air, I once nearly collapsed in the sauna from lack of oxygen; another time, at an outdoor pool, the water was so cold that my heart pounded and my breath gave out, and with too little oxygen, I panicked.

For the locals this altitude was ordinary daily life and nothing to think about, but for a pale, feeble boy who had come from Japan it was a harsh reality.

# A Boring Life in Huancayo

Exploring an unknown town interested me, but walking around alone was dangerous, they said, and I was not allowed out.

Now, speaking from the experience of having traveled all over the world, I can say that a high school student walking around on his own would absolutely have been fine, but my sister and her husband must have felt the responsibility of having me in their care; they were strict about going out.

So basically it was always the two of us at home, my sister and me, and once in a while, when she went shopping, I went into town with her.

The whole roast chicken and fried potatoes she bought me each time were among my few pleasures.

Life in Huancayo was boring far beyond anything I had imagined.

To start with, not speaking Spanish, I could converse only with my sister or her husband (a Peruvian, but he spoke Japanese).

Since going out was forbidden, my sister was my only companion, but there was no way to hang out with a sister six years older, and so my time was spent alone.

![A boy seen from behind gripping a tall chain-link fence with both raised hands](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog19-2.jpg)

When I spent time alone in Japan, there had been television and games and books, and I could go to a bookstore and stand reading, or rent a video and watch a movie, but naturally Peru had no Japanese-language culture.

I had thought that flying out of Japan had made me free, but what actually began was a life of semi-house arrest, alone with my sister.

All I had brought were two books and one notebook.

Having reread those books a few times, I soon tired of them and lost even the will to read.

My sister told me to study Spanish and go out and spend time with the local Jehovah's Witness children close to my age, but neither interest nor motivation would come.

Put in the same situation now, I could think of any number of things to do — exercise, meditate, cook, make something — but back then, having done nothing but go to school and take part in Jehovah's Witness activities, the only thing that came to mind was to endure the boredom.

Years of attending Jehovah's Witness meetings had accustomed me to putting up with things, and that proved useful, in a strange sort of way.
