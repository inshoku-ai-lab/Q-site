# Record Yakuza

Around the same time Tortoise's music woke me up to what music could be, my brother and I threw ourselves into a new hobby.

Among the subculture obsessives of the day, a phenomenon called the record yakuza had taken hold.

It was a culture of buying up records in bulk with the greed of a yakuza gangster, and of cherishing every kind of B-grade music there was.

If anything it was my brother's thing — he has a collector's streak that runs to just about everything — but it got its hooks into me too.

He collected Star Wars figures and rare sneakers as well, and to this day one room in his house is a storeroom for nothing but the collection.

# The Legendary Record Store

Whenever we found a day with time to spare, we would start in the morning and make the rounds of the used record stores.

The one we went to most was an enormous used record store under the elevated train tracks near Umeda, in central Osaka.

The stock was staggering: three hundred square meters (3,200 square feet) of floor space lined with cardboard boxes crammed full of records.

There were no records in there by major, popular artists. What was in there were artists who had been popular twenty years earlier, artists who had been completely unpopular thirty years earlier, and the like.

Being unknown didn't make the music bad, and when I turned up a great track by some nameless minor artist, the happiness was hard to put into words.

What made the store interesting was the pricing, which covered a tremendous range, from collectors' items carrying a premium down to leftover stock at throwaway prices.

Records over five hundred yen — about four dollars — were rare; most were under three hundred.

Some came in under a hundred yen, and there were tags that read twenty yen, five yen, two yen.

The ultimate ones were tagged minus forty-seven yen, or minus a hundred yen, and if you carried a record with a negative price up to the register, they knocked that much off what you owed.

# Rare Gacha

Those negative prices were what got us fired up.

It was like chasing a rare pull in a gacha game — the paid random draws people talk about now — and we would spend hours digging through the mountains of records after a minus tag that could only have turned up about once in a few tens of thousands.

When we did dig one out, it was usually a record that made it perfectly clear why nobody had ever wanted to buy it, and that precise degree of unpopularity was what tickled the obsessive streak in us record yakuza.

The store had nowhere to listen to anything, so with an artist whose name meant nothing to us, the whole standard for buying was whether the cover art clicked — buying on the cover alone.

You head for the section of whatever genre you're after and work through the records one at a time, checking the cover and the price tag.

At first you study the artwork closely, but once you get the hang of it you speed up to something like two seconds a record.

Flipping along in a good rhythm, you stop your fingers at a cover that catches you.

Look at the price and the cover again, and if it seems worth considering, set it aside.

Then back to flipping. And repeat.

Every single time, we spent hours choosing records and never got tired of it.

Both my brother and I had part-time jobs at a video rental store, so free music was available to us in any quantity we liked, and we had enough musician friends to borrow CDs from.

We weren't buying records because we wanted to hear music so much as going to record stores because we wanted to be near music.

More than listening, or playing, or dancing, it was flipping through records that satisfied our appetite for music.

And for the tape collage unit the two of us were running, this was an essential act.

We would dig a record out of the mountain one step short of the trash, cut a sample from it, and bring it back to life as collage music.

There was a joy of creation in it, like putting life into a robot built out of junk.
