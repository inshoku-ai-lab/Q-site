## Biker Gang

Y and I had come to Goa for the same reason.

We had both come for the rave parties they hold in Goa — I had found out about them in an underground magazine, and Y said he had heard about them from a friend.

A rave works like this: a DJ keeps techno running all night, and the crowd takes drugs and keeps dancing all night.

Among all the kinds of raves in the world, Goa's parties occupy a peculiar position, though I would not understand that until much later.

At the time we simply wanted something exciting and fun to do.

Neither of us had ever been to anything like it, and we arrived in Goa full of expectation — only to find no parties, and barely any travelers.

We wanted to figure out why nobody was there and whether any parties existed at all, but neither of us could speak English properly, so we never quite got a handle on the situation.

I said it was because the rainy season had only just ended; Y said Goa had been good back in the day and that by now the parties were gone and travelers had stopped coming.

Bored, we spent whole days smoking joints — hash mixed with tobacco — and playing round after round of cards.

Then one day Y suggested we ride out to a big city called Panaji, about an hour away by motorcycle, and check out the discos and clubs there.

His theory: the parties were not on the beach but in the city.

We rented a motorcycle for three days and decided to explore the area.

In India, traffic law might as well not exist: to rent a motorcycle and ride it you need neither a license nor a helmet.

Leave your passport as collateral, pay about three hundred yen a day — roughly two and a half dollars — and a 150cc scooter is yours.

Y was a former member of a bōsōzoku, one of Japan's motorcycle gangs, and he handled the bike like it was part of him.

A grown man with a joint clamped in his mouth, he started weaving us back and forth across the road.

On the back I was of course not used to any of this and clung on in terror, but Y kept at the zigzagging until, little by little, I got used to it.

Once I was used to it, the childish swerving started to feel like a roller coaster.

Shrieking and whooping, we tore down an empty country road.

## Saturday Night Fever

It was a Saturday night, and we rode into Panaji looking for the buzz of a young crowd.

In the center of town we found a mid-sized disco.

Heavy bass was thumping out into the street.

Hoping we might have found something good, we looked inside.

What was going on in there was a Bollywood music party thrown by rich Indian tourists.

If you have ever seen a Bollywood movie — Bollywood being India's Hollywood — you know the scene: a whole group of men and women, all beaming, singing and dancing.

[via GIPHY](https://giphy.com/gifs/bollywood-party-time-sidharth-malhotra-3o6wrq8VYeGXuQTGZa)

Interesting in its own way, and a rare thing to see, but a completely different animal from the hippie party of cool, crazy travelers we were after.

We walked back out deflated.

Maybe Goa really is over, we said, and stopped to rest in a park in the middle of town.

Might as well smoke a joint, one of us said, and we lit one we had rolled ahead of time.

Cannabis is extremely easy to get in India, which does not make it legal.

## The Police

After a while, police came over.

To them, foreigner equals easy prey, and even with nothing to charge you with they will invent a case and squeeze a bribe out of you.

But I was standing there with a lit joint in my hand.

They know what hash smells like, so there was no bluffing my way out.

The instant they spoke to me I realized how bad this was, put the joint out, and flicked it behind me into the grass where they would not see.

They clearly suspected us and were waiting for any opening to take a bribe.

They asked what we were doing. Nothing in particular, I said.

By this point I was thoroughly wrecked, my eyes not focusing properly.

One of them put his face right up to mine, staring into my eyes, sniffing at me.

Exactly like a frog frozen under a snake's stare.

My eyes were swimming, and at the same time I was absolutely certain I was fine.

They had not seen where the joint went, and in that darkness there was no way they were going to find it.

And even if they did, they were hardly going to fingerprint it and tie it to me.

Apart from the joints we had rolled in advance, we had brought nothing incriminating with us.

Whatever happened, I would be fine.

The officer told me to show him what was in my pockets.

Normally I would have refused, but I wanted the whole thing over with, so I let him search me.

Search all they liked, all we had was a plastic bottle of water and a little cash.

Once the police understood there was nothing to be taken from me, they gave up and left.

I had nearly been set upon by police in a dark park in India, and my knees were shaking with fear.

Y, in the same situation, was unshakable — exactly what you would expect from an ex-bōsōzoku.

Mere police were not going to rattle him.

He waited until the officers had moved off, then went and picked up the joint I had thrown and brought it back.

From the moment they came over he had kept his eyes wide open, and he had seen exactly where the joint landed.

Nothing to do but be impressed.

According to Y, a fighter does not look away from fear.

Deflated, accepting that Goa had no good parties, we got on the motorcycle, smoked the joint, and headed home.
