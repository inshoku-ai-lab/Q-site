# Heavy Rain

The rainy season in Nepal and India is pretty fierce.

Darjeeling was no different.

If anything, the rain may be worse in Darjeeling, high up in the mountains, than in Pokhara down by its lake.

I looked it up: the area is supposed to be one of the rainiest regions in the world.

With the traveler's sense I have now, the best route would be to extend the visa and stay on in Tibet — dry, with no rainy season at all — until the end of September, then head into Nepal as the rains were tapering off.

But back then I did not even know a rainy season existed, and it never crossed my mind that you might plan a route to avoid one.

The rain in Darjeeling was heavy, and I spent most of my time in my room.

Alone for the first time in a long while, I looked back over two solid months of nonstop travel and let my mind run ahead to India.

I had left a good deal of shame behind me on the road, but all of it seemed to be feeding into growing as a person and sharpening my sensibilities, and I was thoroughly satisfied.

# Darjeeling

Maybe I had braced myself harder than I needed to against what I had heard before coming — that India was intense, in all sorts of ways.

The India I finally walked into, keyed up as I was, turned out to be not much different from Nepal, and it left me confident I could handle it easily.

Darjeeling means tea, shipped out from there to the whole world, but to Indians it is a famous hill station, somewhere to get out of the heat, with plenty of sights to see.

![A close-up of young green tea leaves and upright new shoots filling the frame](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog86-1.jpg)

That only holds in the hot season, though; Darjeeling in the rains had no life in it at all, emptied of tourists, travelers and migrant workers alike.

In all that emptiness I met one Japanese traveler and passed the time eating with him, but he moved on to the next place almost at once, and I was alone again.

Travel and boredom go badly together for me, and within a few days I was bored and decided to head for the next place.

# The Road to Calcutta

Next was Calcutta.

The place held no interest for me whatsoever, but to get anywhere else from Darjeeling you have to pass through it.

I changed buses and rode several hours down to Calcutta.

Indian buses, like Nepali ones, sit at the very bottom of the comfort scale.

Looking back, the buses in China had been better.

You are packed tight into a narrow seat, and then another layer of people is pressed in above that.

Sometimes chickens and goats get on as well.

That no cows or horses get on is, I suppose, a small mercy.

Inside the bus the smell of spice seemed stronger than in Nepal, though that may have been my imagination.

In Nepal the most common meal is dal bhat: a fairly mild curry of dal — beans — eaten with white rice.

There the dal is the main dish; in India it drops to a side, and sabzi, the vegetable dish, takes the center.

And with that, the spice and the salt both go up.

It may have been all in my head, but the salt and the spice seemed to have worked their way into the whole town, into the smell of the bus, into the people.

# How I Traveled

When the bus got into Calcutta I took the guidebook's word for it and made for the Paragon Guest House, a cheap place said to be full of Japanese travelers.

Next door was the Maria Guest House, where a lot of the volunteers working at Mother Teresa's homes were said to stay.

The guidebook I had picked up at a guesthouse in Kathmandu was earning its keep.

It was what was left after someone had cut out the pages they needed and gone on; whole sections were missing, and it was still enough for me.

In practice my travels ran on that guidebook, but in principle I meant to travel without one.

I wanted to be a traveler, not a tourist.

![A yellow and white camper van driving away along a road between towering red rock formations](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog86-2.jpg)

For the same reason, I refused to take a camera.

I did not own one, but I could have borrowed one from my family.

I wanted the real experience, the kind that does not fit on film, and I believed there were moments that only shine when there is no camera.

I still think the choice I made then was the right one.

Not carrying one meant no worrying about having my luggage stolen and no puzzling over what to photograph, and I could give the traveling my whole attention.

Had I chosen to carry a camera back then, my life now would be a different one.

That said, if I had carried one, I could have put all sorts of good photographs in this blog — which is the kind of thing I find myself thinking these days.
