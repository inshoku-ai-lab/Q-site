# Steps Toward Freedom

The delusion that I could go on living even if it meant committing crimes, even if it meant becoming a beggar, gave me more strength than I had imagined.

Caught up in this idea, I let my head fill with all sorts of criminal ideas.

Looking back now, it was a childish, unrealistic, eighth-grader's fantasy, but it was enough to rouse a heart that had been cowering and shrunken.

Thanks to this delusion, I was able to take a step toward quitting the Jehovah's Witnesses.

Freedom was what I wanted more than anything, and if becoming a beggar was the price, I had resolved to accept that too.

But telling my mother I wanted to quit the Jehovah's Witnesses was, for me, the single most terrifying thing in the world, and I could not imagine possessing that kind of courage.

For her this was not the simple matter of a child rebelling against a parent — it was an outrageous declaration along the lines of "I have been walking the path of God's truth, but now I will throw away everlasting life in the earthly Paradise, walk the devil's path of desire, and kill myself."

Schooled for years in the loving discipline of Jehovah's Witness teaching, I reacted to the idea of defying my parent with reflexive revulsion.

![A young man in a grey suit with a name badge, head bowed and hands clasped, seated in a crowded arena](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog16-1.jpg)

But without getting past this, there would be no way out of a life in darkness, and I would go on living in prison for the rest of my life.

Growing old like that was the one thing I wanted to avoid, whatever it took.

After thinking it through, searching for some method, I decided to write a letter.

Having never defied my parent, never spoken my true feelings to anyone, I had no way of getting the words out but a letter.

# The Letter of Resolve

Finishing the letter took, I think, several days.

I wanted somehow to get across the most taboo of taboo messages: I was absolutely going to quit the Jehovah's Witnesses.

Working it over and over, I hammered the wording into shape.

The details escape me now, but what I conveyed was something like this.

"Living all this time exactly as you told me, I have felt like cargo on a freight train running along a track that was already laid.

That train is not headed where I want to go, and a life of nothing but being pulled along is not the life I want.

I want to decide my own direction and walk it on my own two feet."

In reality it rambled on far longer, but that was the gist.

I handed this letter over on a Thursday evening, before leaving for the Jehovah's Witness meeting.

The letter had been ready for a while, but I could never bring myself to hand it over, so I only acted when I hit my limit — the point where I truly, truly did not want to go to the meeting.

That day, having read it, my mother said, "Just stay home for now," and left for the meeting.

That night we went to bed without a single word between us.

# Release From the Curse

A few days later, an interview was arranged with the elder of the Jehovah's Witness congregation I attended — the man in charge of our area.

My mother, I think, was hoping I would change my mind.

![A man speaking outdoors with two young women standing behind him and red-roofed houses in the background](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog16-2.jpg)

There, for the first time in my life, I put into spoken words what I actually felt: that I did not want to continue as a Jehovah's Witness.

At last, officially and in the clear, I was able to withdraw from the Jehovah's Witnesses.

The first action in my life taken of my own will.

I had faced the most terrifying thing there was and changed the course of my future life by my own action.

Learning what it meant to face fear, I gained the confidence that no matter how terrible the things ahead of me might be, it would never get worse than this.

For the record, from that time to the present, I have never experienced fear or courage on that scale again.

For me this was a bigger event than twenty-plus years of wandering the world.

Just before summer vacation of my second year of high school ended, I stood for the first time at the starting line of my life.

It was the moment when my life, which had pointed downward ever since I entered elementary school, first began to point upward.

The lid that had held me down for a full twelve years finally came off, and the free current of my own will began to flood out.

The process of demolishing the rails of a life I did not want at all.

That process of demolition would go on for a while yet.
