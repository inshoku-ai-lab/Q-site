## The Two of Us

Only two of us had come up to the hut: me and the manager.

A third person was due to arrive on foot in a few days.

There were two other huts about an hour's walk away, but apart from those, there was nothing.

Just mountains.

Not even trees, let alone a forest.

We were well above the tree line, in an unusual environment where no plant can grow bigger than fifty centimeters (twenty inches).

## Opening the Hut

The empty hut sat buried under the snow, its storm shutters sealed tight.

It stood about two hundred meters (650 feet) higher than the big hut where I'd been until now, and because it was hemmed in by mountain walls, the snow still hadn't melted.

The ritual of opening the hut for the season began with digging the entrance out from under the snow.

We took out the shovels that had been stowed safely on the outside of the hut and took turns clearing the snow.

Once the snow was out of the way, we pried off the sturdy storm shutters, which were firmly fixed in place, with a crowbar.

It took about thirty minutes before we could finally get inside.

## The New Hut

The big hut could apparently hold as many as eight hundred people at its busiest, but this one topped out at eighty.

A tenth of the size, with everything compact and neatly put together.

A small kitchen, a handful of guest rooms.

The hut was small, and so was the land around it.

It looked as if someone had found a narrow gap between the mountains and wedged the hut into it.

We carried everything the helicopter had brought into the hut and put the food away in the freezer.

There was a freezer, but no electricity yet.

## The Generator

The next job was getting the generator running.

The manager and I headed to the generator room outside the hut to get it ready.

A generator left alone deep in the mountains for eight months doesn't start that easily.

After adjusting the choke and yanking the starter cord again and again, we finally got it going.

Now the frozen food wouldn't thaw.

The next supply lift was about two weeks away, so if we'd neglected this, we would have run out of things to eat.

At long last, the bare minimum of work we needed to get done that day was finished.

It had been day after day of getting up at 4 a.m. and hauling loads nonstop, so we ate an early dinner and called it a day.
