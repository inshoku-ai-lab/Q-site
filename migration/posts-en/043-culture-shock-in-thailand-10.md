# To Chiang Mai

From Bangkok, Thailand's capital, I took a night bus up into the mountains of the north.

Chiang Mai, the second-largest city in Thailand, is the gateway to that part of the country.

The night bus had its air conditioning cranked far too high, as usual, but I had already experienced how cold Thai buses get and had come prepared, so it was no problem.

When I arrived in Chiang Mai, it felt a little cooler than Bangkok — or maybe I only imagined it.

The sun and the humidity both seemed gentler.

The difference between a coastal city and a mountain city, maybe.

I already had a place to stay in mind.

I had looked it up in the guidebook beforehand.

The guidebook was written for Japanese travelers, so the places listed in it collect a lot of Japanese.

Not being able to speak English, I traveled more efficiently where other Japanese gathered, and had more fun, and felt safer.

Walking around, what struck me was that Chiang Mai felt calmer than Bangkok, more relaxed.

The city was laid out with more space, which probably left more room to breathe.

# The Mountain Tour

According to the guidebook, this guesthouse ran a two-day, one-night tour that took you to visit the hill tribes on elephant back.

While I was checking in, I asked the guesthouse staff for details, and they brought out all sorts of photos.

Travelers up on elephants, hill tribe people smiling, the view from a mountaintop — the kind of photos that stir up the urge to go.

The unknown pulled at me.

There was also a notebook of reviews from people who had done the tour, and reading through them it sounded pretty good, so I signed up.

The next morning we gathered at the guesthouse entrance, before dawn.

We picked up the other participants from around the city and headed for the mountains.

Seven of us in all.

Five Japanese and two Westerners.

As it happened, this trip had a lot of Japanese on it.

Which mountain we were going to, I never really knew.

I was simply taken along wherever they led.

The car reached the foot of the mountain, and from there no vehicle could go any further.

We started up on foot.

I was wearing Converse sneakers back then, shoes made for asphalt — flat-soled and slippery, no good for walking in the mountains.

Slipping over and over, I went up and down the mountain.

We weren't going to be riding elephants the whole way; first we had to reach the hill tribe settlement where the elephants were.

Along the way I talked with the other Japanese on the tour.

Three girls on a graduation trip — the trip Japanese students take between school and their first job — and a boy on his own, on the same kind of trip.

I wanted us to get friendly, but the conversation stayed stiff.

The air around them was quite different from the travelers I had met on Koh Phangan.

More like the people I'd known in my life back in Japan.

Maybe they had only just arrived in Thailand and were nervous too.

# The Hill Tribe

With breaks along the way, I think we walked about five hours.

At last we reached the hill tribe settlement.

Not a village — a settlement.

A few houses of assembled timber, raised up on stilts.

On Koh Phangan I had been amazed at how the bungalows were about as simple as storage sheds, but this went further.

The huts had a roof, a floor, and walls.

That was it.

No furniture, no doors, no windows, just a gap to go in and out through.

People, animals, mosquitoes — all free to come and go.

In that climate the cold was probably never an issue.

Built as if keeping the rain off was all that mattered.

This, it seemed, was where we would sleep that night.

Still, there were futons and mosquito nets, so sleeping looked like it would be fine.

While we were sorting out our bags in the hut, the food was ready.

Vegetable and meat curry with rice. Easy to eat, light on the oil and salt and spice.

Deep in the mountains, five hours' walk from the bottom, a hot meal was genuinely something to be grateful for.

Did they carry it up from town every time?

After the meal we all went off to a waterfall near the settlement.

The two Westerners stripped naked and got into the falls, looking blissful.

The Japanese among us had neither the courage to strip nor the nerve to get in, and watched from a distance.

Looking back, we were soaked in sweat and could have at least gone down to our underwear and rinsed off, but something about it embarrassed us.
