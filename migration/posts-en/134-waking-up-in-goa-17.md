## Our Regular Restaurant

By December the dry season had set in completely, and there was no trace left anywhere of the rain that had been falling only a few weeks before.

By the calendar it was the dead of winter, but out under the midday sun the heat could nearly knock you over.

The nights, though, got surprisingly cold, and riding a motorcycle late at night, a winter jacket was about right.

In the dry winter the area where I lived turned into a kind of desert, red earth with nothing growing on it but palm trees.

Across the road you could see the beach and the sea.

Cars were out of the question around there, and no bicycles or motorcycles came through either; about once an hour a person or a cow would walk past, and that was it.

In the evening a few children would come by.

You almost never saw a foreigner.

The area was close to the center of the tourist district, but the road was built as a dead end, so nobody came through, and it stayed a secret pocket.

Here the scenery drifted past slow and peaceful, as if time had stopped.

A friend of mine described it as Penguin Village from the Dr. Slump Arale-chan comics, and I think he had it exactly right.

Inside all that easygoing peace it carried something as unreal as a cartoon.

That quiet stretch with nobody on it was where the restaurant stood.

It was a simple place: on a patch of front yard about ten meters (thirty feet) square stood a shack with a roof and walls made of coconut leaves, and inside it were four plastic tables and twenty or so plastic chairs.

## Spaghetti alle Vongole

Everyone had a favorite dish there, and mine, by a long way, was the spaghetti alle vongole.

Most spaghetti alle vongole is made with clams and white wine; here it was mussels in a tomato sauce.

Yes, mussels mean it isn't vongole at all, but making that point to a restaurant in India is not a classy thing to do, so please don't.

India is a country of simmered food, and most restaurants there hand you spaghetti boiled to death, but here it came out at exactly the right firmness.

Goa is a fishing town, and part of its appeal is the fresh seafood you can eat cheap.

Go to the market and the day's catch is laid out packed edge to edge.

## Laughing at How Good It Was

The first time I ran into this place's spaghetti alle vongole, my eyes went wide and I couldn't stop laughing at how good it was.

Past a certain point, food that good just makes you laugh.

I had Y, who was there with me, taste it, and his eyes went wide and he started laughing too.

We looked at each other and roared — too goooood.

It was so good that I gave Y half of my plate and ordered a second one.

## The Secret Behind It

One secret of that flavor was the freshness of the mussels, and the restaurant kept its stock small to keep them fresh.

So if your timing was bad, they had run out.

That didn't mean you couldn't order it.

The chef, a fatherly old guy, would come out and say, "It's fine, just wait a bit. R, lend me your motorcycle for a minute."

Kind-hearted R would hand the key over just as asked, and the old man would swing onto the motorcycle and ride to a town half an hour away to buy mussels.

On days like that the food took something like three hours to arrive.

Are you kidding me, I would think — but then I would picture fresh spaghetti alle vongole at the end of a three-hour wait, eaten on an empty stomach, and somehow I could stand it. Strange how that works.
