## Sugarcane Cutting

The sugarcane-cutting season starts around February.

By mid-January, B had found sugarcane-cutting work on a neighboring island.

Cheerful and friendly with everyone, B had apparently struck up conversations with just about every person he met on Ishigaki and picked up the tip that way.

Through B's connections, I landed a sugarcane-cutting job too.

The work was on Taketomi Island, right next door: eight-hour days at five thousand yen a day — about forty dollars — with a free dorm and free meals.

It was low pay for such hard work, but for drifters like us, just having a job with room and board was something to be grateful for.

From what I heard, none of the locals wanted to do the grueling work of cane cutting, so the labor came entirely from travelers.

A good example of supply and demand working out for both sides.

The work didn't start until February, so I decided to spend the remaining two weeks lazing around the campground.

## The Campground Closes

I hadn't seen it coming, but with about a week left in January, the campground closed.

According to the caretaker, they closed it for about a month every year.

Running the campground took almost no effort, so it could easily have stayed open year-round, but they closed it at this time of year anyway.

The reason was startling.

It went all the way back to the 1995 terrorist attack by Aum Shinrikyo, the religious cult.

Once the truth about their religion came out and the whole country turned on them, Aum Shinrikyo's followers had nowhere left to go.

They drifted and drifted across Japan until they ended up at a campground on an island at the farthest edge of the country.

They settled in at this campground, lived there, and carried on with their ascetic training.

But naturally, they ran into fierce opposition from the locals and were driven out a few months later.

I'd love to know where they went after being kicked off the island at the end of the country, but in any case, they were gone.

That was what started the yearly closure.

If anyone tried to settle in, the closure was grounds to throw them out, and if campers put up makeshift shacks, the closure was grounds to tear them down.

Apparently some travelers used to live here semi-permanently, but that seemed to be a thing of the past.
