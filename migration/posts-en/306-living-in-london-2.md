## Work

N had asked the owner of the place where she worked whether he could take me on to wash dishes, and the answer came back right away.

To my surprise, the owner trusted N enough that I was hired without an interview.

Come in and start in three days, he said.

Work settled before a place to live — a good omen if there ever was one.

Maybe there was no interview because the job would be illegal, and he had skipped it on purpose.

The hours were Monday through Friday, six in the evening to eleven at night, with two days off a week.

Even counting tips, the pay came to about 700 yen an hour — five and a half dollars or so — as I remember.

It was quite low, but luckily I was coming off years of traveling in India, and it felt like more than enough.

Prices still registered with me in Indian rupees, and by that arithmetic a week of work bought about a month of living cheap in India, which put me in a very good mood.

## The Thai Restaurant

Nothing had come through on housing yet, so I would be heading to work straight from N's place.

About a twenty-minute walk.

N rode a kick scooter to work, which was just starting to catch on in London around then, but as long as she was going in with me, she walked.

At the restaurant I was introduced to the chef, a middle-aged Thai woman.

She was around fifty, apparently the older sister of the owner's wife.

She spoke no Japanese, and no English either, and my own English was broken, so nothing like a real conversation was possible between us — which, for now, seemed to be no problem at all.

Everyone at the restaurant called her by the Japanese word "Oneesan," big sister, and though she was my mother's age as far as I was concerned, I ended up calling her Oneesan too.

Also working there was Oneesan's younger brother, "Ojisan," the word for a middle-aged man, around fifty himself.

Prep work in the kitchen was his job, as I understood it.

What I heard later was that Ojisan was no brother at all, but Oneesan's ex-husband.

Apparently there was all kinds of drama.

And then there was one more, "Ojiisan," grandpa, who was supposedly the actual father of the owner's wife.

He would drift in whenever things got busy, help out a bit, and vanish off somewhere again.

He was a gentle old man, the spitting image of Tomozo, the grandfather in Chibi Maruko-chan.

And so, right in the middle of a Thai family that did not speak a word of English, my London life began.
