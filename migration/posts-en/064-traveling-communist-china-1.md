# The Morning I Left

The day of departure finally came.

Backpack shouldered, out of my family home.

By the time I stepped through the front door, I was already in travel mode.

The few-minute walk to the bus stop felt oddly powerful, something to be proud of.

Even the bus from the house to the station, which I had ridden countless times, looked entirely different now.

It was my first time back at the house in a year and a half, so the buildings and shops along the bus route had shifted a little.

That restaurant went under. A Uniqlo, of all places. Another convenience store. Finding those small changes pleased me more than it ever had.

# Luggage

So I could keep moving easily, I cut the load way down.

Leaving my MiniDisc collection behind at the house was part of that.

For the year and a half I lived on my own, I had music going from the moment I woke up — before I even went to the toilet — until the moment I got into bed.

The only exceptions were reading, watching movies, and eating gyudon, beef over rice, with friends.

Otherwise it never stopped: at home, on the bicycle ride to my part-time job, all the way through the shift.

I was grateful to have lived somewhere that allowed for that.

Once the trip was over and I was back in Japan, I wanted to live at the house and put everything into writing music, so a few months of hearing no music at all beforehand seemed like it might feed good inspiration.

Leaving the MiniDiscs behind was a choice I made out of love for music.

The only book I took was a China guidebook a friend gave me before I left.

A-kun, who had traveled Thailand with me, had apparently planned a trip to China himself once, and he handed over his old guidebook.

That guidebook would bail me out more than once later on.

The other thing I carried was a single notebook.

Once I reached China I planned to keep a travel diary every day.

The kit reminded me of the one I took to Peru, though I had put it together without thinking.

Somewhere underneath, I think, was a wish to face myself by owning nothing.

Maybe it was my mother's religious blood — a musician living in the city, with the mind of an ascetic monk somewhere at the root.

# The Port of Kobe

For now, the destination was the Port of Kobe.

A sleeper ferry called the Shin Ganjin runs between Shanghai and Kobe.

Three days and two nights to Shanghai, supposedly.

For all that I lived in Osaka, I had never once gone as far as Kobe, the next town over.

Everywhere I lived my life, family home included, fit inside a ten-kilometer (six-mile) radius centered on Tennoji in Osaka.

I thought I might get a look at the city on the way to the port, but the train only ran past the station.

The stop nearest the Shin Ganjin's berth came up quickly.

From the house in Osaka it was no distance at all.

A short walk from the station, then check-in at the counter.

The ship looked pretty beat up, but it seemed like it would make Shanghai without trouble.

Immigration right in front of the ship, and then, finally, aboard.

Following the number on the ticket they handed me, I found my cabin.

The cabin was an eight-person dormitory.

Each bunk had a curtain across it, so there was privacy of a sort.

Nobody else had arrived yet.

I went walking through the ferry I would be handing myself over to for the next three days.

There was a restaurant, a ping-pong room, a mahjong room, but no one, being generous, could have called it a comfortable, clean tourist ship.

The bare essentials, set in exactly the space they needed, free to use or nearly free.

Not a ship for a holiday — a working ship for Chinese migrant laborers.

For someone trying to get the largest possible trip out of a limited budget, it felt like a good fit.

Wandering further, in a room set up like a lounge, I met another Japanese traveler.

We got onto the subject of our routes, and I told him mine: south from Shanghai, through Southeast Asia and across Myanmar into India.

What came back was a surprise.

The border between India and Myanmar was closed, he said. To get from China to India you had to cross Tibet.

I was surprised, and he was surprised that I did not already know.

Among travelers it was apparently common knowledge.

On the spot I scrapped the route south from Shanghai and decided to head west and cross Tibet instead.

The plan had barely existed in the first place, so changing it cost nothing.

Tibet had appeared in front of me out of nowhere, a future far too unknown to picture, and it hinted at what this trip might turn out to hold.
