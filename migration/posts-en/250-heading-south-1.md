## The Train South

Back in Delhi, I booked a train to Goa right away.

A long-distance train: more than a thousand kilometers (six hundred miles), about thirty-six hours.

I was used to long-distance trains in India, but thirty-six hours still takes a fair bit of psyching up.

The challenge, for me, was facing thirty-six hours of boredom.

## Conversation Patterns

Indians are always friendly, to an almost abnormal degree, and if you speak to them first they will keep you company for twenty-four hours straight without a break; but after months in the country, I could not work up much interest in talking with them.

The reason is that close to a hundred percent of the time, you get barraged with the same questions.

The questions have an order and a pattern to them: it starts with where you are from, then works through your social attributes in detail.

What do you do? Are you married? How many brothers and sisters do you have? What is your religion?

Things nobody in Japan would ask you straight out come at you from the first words of a first meeting.

At first I couldn't tell whether it was a pleasantry or genuine curiosity, but as I got used to India I came to understand that knowing these attributes about a person is important to them.

Not that I was patient enough to keep answering the same things over and over, and every time the same question came around again, I'd had enough.

Still, brushing someone off wasn't an option, so I managed somehow — this kind of conversation is one of the tiresome constants of Indian travel.

## The Questions Continue

There is more to the sequence, and if you keep at it patiently, the conversation moves to the next stage.

That stage is about your possessions and your money: where did you buy this, how much did you pay — question after question, without end.

And once that round of questions is done, the next stage begins.

The next stage is the gimme stage: they tell you they would like one of the things they just priced, as a keepsake.

Usually electronics: a small music player, a set of speakers — the sort of thing you simply could not get in India at the time.

Even if you could get hold of one, it would cost several times the Japanese price, and on an average Indian wage it was out of reach entirely.

Given all that, and being people with a gift for saying what they feel without dressing it up, they ask you outright to hand it over.

The pattern doesn't apply to every Indian, of course, but it applied to a good share of the ordinary Indian families riding second-class sleeper.
