# Speakers

Bored, shut in my room alone through the rainy season, I did something reckless and bought a boombox.

Reckless because the thing was big and heavy, and from that day on it took up a serious share of the space and weight in my backpack.

Picture a heavy square box, twenty-five centimeters (ten inches) on a side.

There cannot be many objects in the world less suited to travel.

If I had brought a MiniDisc player from Japan, I could have carried a mountain of music in almost no space at all, but I had set myself the challenge of going the whole trip without music, and that is how I came to own a big, heavy boombox instead.

Still, it got me out of the boredom.

The fact that I would carry something this big and this heavy just to listen to it confirmed all over again how enormously music matters to me.

The city was awash in bootleg cassette tapes.

At about two hundred yen apiece — a dollar and a half or so — they were dirt cheap, but I did not have money enough to buy as many as I liked.

So that the few tapes I had would not get old on me from repeat listening, I bought several classic rock albums I had already played countless times.

One of them was a cassette of The Velvet Underground & Nico, by the Velvet Underground.

It is a record you can listen to over and over your whole life without tiring of it, so if it interests you, give it a try.

# Seeing Friends Again

A few days after arriving in Kathmandu, walking through the Thamel district, I ran into the Japanese friends who had stayed at the same guesthouse in Tibet and had come to my birthday party.

It was not entirely coincidence — we were on the same route at the same time, and both of us had figured we would meet again in Nepal.

The three of them, two guys and one girl, had come all the way to Kathmandu.

They were going to see the sights around Kathmandu for a few days, they said, and then move on to Pokhara, the country's second-largest city.

Tomorrow they were walking thirty minutes to see a temple on a hill, and they asked me to come along.

I had nothing else going on, and it sounded fun, so I went with them.

# Swayambhunath

The next morning I got up early and joined them.

By luck, rainy season or not, it was not raining.

To get to the stupa at Swayambhunath you have to climb close to four hundred steps.

Coming from Tibet I was used to altitude, but four hundred steps was still rough.

In building terms, I would put the height at around forty stories.

At the end of the climb, a tremendous view was waiting.

The center of the city was as stagnant with exhaust as ever, but the land beyond it had been washed by days of rain, and part of the mountain range showed through.

It was cloudy, so I could not see all of it, but knowing the Himalayas were right there in front of me made me happy.

The stupa at Swayambhunath is said to be the oldest Buddhist temple in Nepal, a country with a long history of them, and it is registered as a World Heritage Site.

The body of the stupa is painted gold, with an image standing for the face of the Buddha.

From the top of the tower hang great masses of tarcho, the strung five-colored prayer flags I had seen in Tibet as well.

Nepalese stupas look like the Tibetan Buddhist style I knew with something Indian laid over it.

A hilltop is a pleasant place to be to begin with, and with the beauty of the Buddhist architecture added to it, the whole scene made me glad to be traveling.

We bought chai and sat down where we could see both the view and the stupa.

We traded stories from our travels in Tibet, celebrated meeting again here in Nepal, and were glad together to be drinking tea in another strange place.
