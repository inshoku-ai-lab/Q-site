# Saruganseki

To go back a little: around the time I was about to start living on my own, a late-night program called "Denpa Shōnen" was running a segment in which a comedy duo called Saruganseki crossed the Eurasian continent using nothing but hitchhiking, and it was a huge hit.

It is also known as the show that put Hiroiki Ariyoshi — now second in Japan's Twitter follower rankings — on television.

It probably suited the times.

At my previous part-time job it had been a constant topic of conversation, and everyone was excited by a real-time drama of a kind nobody had seen before.

I too got completely hooked on the show, exactly as the producers intended.

Every week I followed the show obsessively, bought the tie-in books as well, and became a huge fan.

They were broke and had nothing, and still they made it from Japan to London on nothing but hitchhiking.

The idea that with guts and nerve you could manage anything, even with nothing to your name, struck me as the coolest thing in the world.

It later came out that the program had staged parts of it, but the emotion and the shock that travel story gave me were real.

Looking back now, the origin of my many years of travel was in that show.

Having traveled for over twenty years, I would like there to be some mystical reason or some great event that set it off, but in reality the biggest travel inspiration I ever received was "Saruganseki."

![A hooded person with a backpack standing beside an empty highway, thumb out toward distant mountains](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog34-1.jpg)

I never dreamed that, out of that inspiration, only a few months later I would actually be going backpacking, but the emotion I took from the show had shaken my soul and planted the seed of a vagabond in me.

# A Trend at the Part-Time Job

The Tsutaya overnight shift group had one charismatic figure, and whatever he said, everyone followed.

After he wrote up a travelogue about touring Vietnam by bicycle and shared it with everyone by email, one by one the artsy types among the Tsutaya part-timers began to travel.

A self-styled "cultural aristocrat," he had the outstanding literary talent to match, and the travelogues he wrote overflowed with a cinematic sense of the road, inviting the reader to travel.

Most of them traveled abroad for a few weeks to a few months, using university breaks and the like.

Many of them were something like backpackers in Southeast Asia, though some did something more like a homestay in Europe.

The durations and the destinations were all over the place, but what everyone who came back from a trip had in common was a look of having shed a skin, of having become polished.

A kind of confidence, or superiority — an air of "I have already been through it" — seeped out of them, and it made them look grown-up.

Everyone was traveling and brimming with confidence, looking as cool as the Saruganseki I had seen on TV, and just as I was thinking that, my techno-music friend from work, A, brought up the idea of a trip to Thailand.

I had gone to Peru a year earlier, so I had a passport.

It had never crossed my mind that someone like me, a mere part-timer at a video rental shop, would go on an overseas trip, but the fact that I already had a passport, and that I would be going with a friend rather than alone, pushed me over the line, and I decided to go to Thailand.

On my own, that idea and that nerve would not have come to me.

# The Travel Plan

We took three weeks off from work in March and made a plan to head for a southern island in Thailand.

At a time when my high school classmates were still students, going backpacking with a friend for three whole weeks violently stirred my sense of superiority.

Back then I had wanted to quit high school and live a life that sharpened my sensibilities, so doing something different from my classmates mattered to me.

![Orange sunset over a calm sea, sun rays fanning through cloud, dark rocks in the shallows](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog34-2.jpg)

The plan was to spend the first week or so on a southern island called Koh Phangan, and a week in the second half of the trip in a mountain town called Chiang Mai.

The remaining week was buffer time in the capital, Bangkok.

Since there was no telling which places we would take a liking to, I wanted the schedule to have a certain amount of slack.

Buying tickets online was not yet a thing back then, so we bought round-trip tickets at the H.I.S. travel agency near our workplace.

I do not remember the price, but I do not think it was cheap.

Even as the day of departure drew near, I felt nothing but excitement — not a trace of anxiety or fear.

Going to Peru alone a year earlier seemed to have given me a great deal of confidence.
