## The Inn on the High Desert

Day one of the bus trip ran until night, and we ended up sleeping at the spot where we stopped for our last meal break.

It was a restaurant and lodging house in one, sitting right in the middle of the high desert road, with no village anywhere around, not so much as a man-made object in sight.

The dead middle of nature itself.

The night sky in Manali had astonished me, but here the number of stars was another notch higher.

There were no town lights for dozens of kilometers (tens of miles) in any direction, and everything was wrapped in pitch black.

On top of that, the thin air at that altitude and the absence of water vapor in the dry air doubled the number of stars that reached the eye.

The lodging was nothing as grand as a hotel — it was one of some twenty beds lined up inside a large makeshift tent.

The quilt was thick and heavy, and it smelled a little.

It probably hadn't been washed in a long time, or maybe ever.

In the mountains of India there is no such thing as a bed sheet; you pull the heavy, cotton-stuffed quilt straight over yourself.

It falls into the simple pattern — new is clean, old is dirty — but having come this far out to the end of the earth, there was no room to complain.

The air outside was sharp with cold, making the high-altitude desert something you felt in your body, but under the quilt I slept warm.

Just being able to keep the cold off was enough to be grateful for.

## My Birthday

The call to gather came early in the morning, and we bolted down breakfast and climbed onto the bus.

But for all the early start, the bus got caught in a jam and would not budge an inch.

Word was that the road had given way somewhere far ahead, and vehicles could only move a little at a time.

That day was my twenty-first birthday, and I would have liked to celebrate it in Leh, but there is no adjusting the uncertain flow of a journey.

There was no sign of moving at all, so we got off the bus and wandered around.

Friends who had gotten off ahead of me were looking down at their feet and making a commotion about something.

Something on the ground was catching the light, and up close it turned out to be quartz.

A closer look showed quartz of all sizes lying scattered everywhere.

There was nothing big and clear enough to pass for a gem, but there were small transparent pieces, and clusters where those had grown together into a larger crystal.

By coincidence — or by necessity — the bus had apparently stopped over a stratum of quartz-bearing rock.

I decided to take the coincidence as a gift from heaven.

With so many kinds of quartz lying around, it was hard to decide which one to pick up.

Greed said take a lot of them, but that meant heavier luggage, and heavier luggage makes life hard for a traveler.

Experiencing the world mattered more to me than owning things, so birthday or not, hauling off several heavy lumps of rock would have had it backwards.

While I stood there weighing it, a cluster caught my eye, about the size of my palm, made of small clear crystals grown together.

It was nothing special next to the others, but I knew on instinct that it was the one, and picked it up.

There was no hesitation.

Quartz received straight from the earth, with no one's hands in between.

It let me feel my connection to the planet — the best birthday present I could have had.
