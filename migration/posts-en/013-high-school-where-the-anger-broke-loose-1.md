# Dropping Out of High School

The other rebel kid in my class, a fellow second-generation Jehovah's Witness, had picked the same high school I did.

Not out of hope or expectation, of course, but out of inertia.

Then, on the fourth day after we started, he suddenly quit.

There was no clear reason, only a passive one — high school was no longer compulsory schooling, you could withdraw of your own accord, so he did.

He withdrew and started a job delivering newspapers.

He wanted to stand on his own and be free, I think.

What I noticed right after enrolling was how high the dropout rate was at this school.

A third of the students dropped out before graduating.

Most withdrew voluntarily, but some left after causing trouble, and others quit when they had to repeat a year.

A dissolute air hung over the place.

It made sense that so many dropped out.

Part of the reason, I think, was that although it called itself coeducational, ninety-nine percent of the students were boys.

Another was that, as a technical high school — a school of specialized trades — it had no class reshuffles, and the same faces stayed together for three years.

On the standardized-score rankings it was faster to count up from the bottom, so having no hope for the future sped up the decay as well.

# From Isolation to Immersion in Culture

Still quiet and inward as ever, in high school too I was one of the weak ones.

At this school, nothing like bullying happened to anyone, but it was basically a rough place, so the line between the strong and the weak was clearly drawn.

Not being sociable by nature and shy with strangers, I had few friends in high school, and after the rebel kid who had gone there with me dropped out, I had almost none at all.

Alone, around this time I began to get hooked on manga.

The bookstore in front of the station, on my way home from school, allowed reading without buying, so with no friends to speak of, I spent my hours doing nothing but standing in that store reading manga.

![A man in a red knit hat and striped scarf browsing a crowded outdoor book stall, holding a paperback](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog13-1.jpg)

I read every weekly manga magazine there was, following thirty serialized titles a week.

Because it was free, I also read the seinen magazines, the young men's titles I would never have bought myself, and manga with an older readership influenced me deeply.

Manga for adults helped my mental growth along.

At home the only information available was basically the television, regulated according to Jehovah's Witness guidelines, but standing in a bookstore reading was something my mother could not fully regulate.

Making use of that free reading, I soaked up information and stimulation fast.

Around the same time, I got hooked on watching movies.

My older brother worked part-time at a video rental shop and secretly let me rent for free, so I ended up watching all kinds of films.

At first it was only easy-to-follow Hollywood action movies, but my brother, who played bass in an experimental rock band, introduced me little by little to more provocative films, and I grew up culturally.

I had never minded being alone to begin with, but in this period my isolation stood out even more sharply, and cultural stimulation was my only friend.

# Grandmother's Death

Right after I started high school, my grandmother started getting sick.

My brother asked me, "Grandma's sick, so I'm going to visit her — want to come?" — but for some reason it never occurred to me that my own grandmother could be sick, and thinking he meant his wife's grandmother, I turned him down.

Spring break, summer break, winter break — every long holiday, without fail, I had gone to my grandmother's house to play, been fed a feast, been given pocket money and had toys bought for me, and yet somehow I did not go over during the spring break just before high school started.

No particular reason.

Then, in the summer of my first year, my grandmother passed away.

![A bouquet of white roses and a white gerbera daisy tied with a green ribbon, beside a dark car](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog13-2.jpg)

The first family funeral I ever attended.

Half dead inside, my heart suppressed to the point where negative feelings no longer registered, I did not grieve much and simply took part in the funeral matter-of-factly.

At the time that was fine, but once I was grown, with my emotions back and able to read situations for what they were, I regretted deeply that I had not been able to visit my grandmother when she was ill.

As an adult I lived about as freely as a person can and had no regrets whatsoever, and yet this one thing stayed in my heart and I went on regretting it.

About twenty years after that day, a friend I trust did a channeling session for me — a séance — and I told her about it.

According to her, my grandmother had wanted to go to the other side quietly, without letting anyone see how she had aged and withered.

So, being closely connected to my grandmother's soul, I had received that message unconsciously, and that is why I never went over to play or to visit her.

My grandmother's soul told me, "Don't blame yourself so much, have more confidence in yourself."

Learning this, I wept aloud and was released from the regret.
