# The Downward Spiral, Double Life

Growing up in a Jehovah's Witness household meant being raised under the strict rules that biblical fundamentalism produces.

Jehovah's Witness children basically have only two options: obey quietly, or get beaten with the rod until you cry and repent.

By junior high the beatings had stopped, naturally enough, but having been "educated" that way since I was small, I could not defy my mother.

Call it trauma, call it conditioned reflex, call it a slave mentality soaked into me.

Like a frog stared down by a snake. It had nothing to do with reason.

When a Jehovah's Witness child hits junior high and enters the rebellious phase, the reaction splits three ways.

One is to submit obediently to the rules exactly as told.

Another is to defy everything openly.

The third is to pretend to follow the rules while doing the exact opposite behind the scenes.

Most Jehovah's Witness children, myself included, chose to live a double life.

In front of our parents we dressed neatly, obeyed, and never argued back.

In front of our friends we did the things our parents had forbidden.

Forced by loving discipline to be honest, we learned to carry a lie through flawlessly.

There was never an opportunity to learn how to open your heart, speak your real feelings, and accept one another.

That was true of my parents, of the Jehovah's Witness adults, of the teachers at school.

Not one person was speaking their real feelings.

Thinking back on it now, there was one.

My homeroom teacher in third and fourth grade.

The great love he showed never reached my heart, but he stays in my memory even now, and brings back a warm feeling.

In a household built on a double life, you speak less and less so the lies won't be found out.

Lies and duplicity became part of my daily life, and acting out a lie on the spot stopped costing me anything at all.

A rebellious streak suppressed during that phase often seems to produce distortions like a double life.

The more obedient a junior high schooler is to his parents, the less anyone knows what he is doing behind their backs.

# The Downward Spiral, Stealing

As conscience wore away, resistance to things like shoplifting wore away with it.

As a junior high schooler who could not take a part-time job, raised in a poor household, I justified it to myself: there was no other way to get what I wanted.

There were probably regional and generational factors at work too, but at my junior high shoplifting was extremely common.

Something like seventy percent of the boys in my class had shoplifted at some point, I'd guess.

Shoplifting had spread through our school badly enough that a newly opened bookstore on the way home from school shut down within a year because of the losses.

Three times a week I attended Jehovah's Witness meetings in formal clothes, singing songs in praise of God, and whenever I had free time I went on shoplifting tours with my friends.

![Three boys with their arms around one another, laughing, in black and white](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog8-1-1.jpg)

To keep the crimes from being traced back, we rode our bikes to towns far away and stole over and over.

What we mainly stole was used books.

We would go to a small secondhand bookshop in the next town and lift entire runs of whatever manga we wanted.

Once we had read them, we took them to a different secondhand shop, turned them into cash, and spent it at the arcade.

By then any fear of divine punishment had gone completely numb, so we also went to Shinto shrines and stole the offering money.

We stuck packing tape to a disposable wooden chopstick, slid it through the slot of the offering box, and lifted out the coins.

What I think was truly the lowest of all, though, was stealing money from the wallets of my mother and sister, poor as they were, to feed my own appetite for things.

Faking illness to skip school, taking money from a parent's wallet, and, on the way home from the clinic, spending it at the arcade my parents had forbidden.

All the various distortions of the strain pushed down from society were coming to a head inside me.

Our shoplifting games escalated, until we were taking the train into the city and lifting things on a large scale.

At first we would walk in normally and pocket things while dodging the anti-theft equipment and the security guards.

Experience made us more comfortable by the day, and we began drawing up bigger plans.

Break into a major video game store through the back door and steal game software in bulk.

The plan was to sell them secondhand later and turn them into cash.

At this point it was outright crime.

Not something that could be brushed off as junior high schoolers shoplifting.

Keeping an eye on our surroundings, we opened the back door and went in.

Tremendous tension and excitement.

We spotted a heavy cardboard box just left sitting there.

![An original Game Boy propped against a kerb with two game cartridges beside it](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog8-2-1.jpg)

Believing we had found what we came for, we carried the box out and ran for a nearby park.

The tension was enormous, but so was the joy.

If we sold this off, it would come to a fair bit of money.

But what came out when we opened the box was not game software, just ordinary business envelopes.

Tremendous tension and joy, then a huge letdown.

That incident hit me hard.

From that day on I quit stealing entirely.

Shoplifting, my parents' wallets, offering boxes, all of it.

It was not that I quit out of guilt or pangs of conscience.

The heavy stress and disappointment I went through there made it all feel vaguely stupid, and I lost interest.

My shoplifting partner, though, reacted in the exact opposite way.

He found a new partner and turned into a full-fledged thief.

They were geniuses at stealing who never lost their nerve, and they were taking more than a million yen (around nine thousand dollars) worth of goods a month.

I watched them with a cool head, never once envied them, and stopped wanting things beyond what I needed.
