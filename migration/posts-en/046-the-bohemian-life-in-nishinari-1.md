# Back in Nishinari

The three-week trip to Thailand ended without trouble, and I came back to my one-room apartment in Nishinari, Osaka's skid row.

The trip was fine. My savings were not.

Everything I had put away from the pizza place job had vanished into moving costs and the trip to Thailand.

The rent came out anyway for the three weeks I wasn't living there.

But I wasn't working, so nothing was coming in.

And in Thailand I spent money every day.

Prices there are far below Japan's, but when all you do is spend, the savings naturally go down.

I got back to Japan and was suddenly broke.

The next paycheck was a while off, so until then I had to live cheap.

I grew up in a poor single-mother household, but managing money had been my mother's job.

Having no money through my own doing was a first, and it came with a kind of fear.

Obvious enough, but for the first time I saw how large a share of a paycheck goes to rent, and understood that going to India again for a long stretch would mean giving this room up first, or it would turn into a disaster.

The next trip, I would go without the apartment. A vision of the future was steadily taking shape.

# A New Friend

When I went back to my shifts at Tsutaya, the video rental chain, a new part-timer named T had joined the overnight crew.

He was a year older than me, and by the standards of that staff, still one of the young ones.

Like everyone else who worked there, he was a movie nerd and a music nerd, hooked on culture.

We were both new, both far younger than the rest, and our tastes lined up, so we hit it off right away.

He wanted to be a film director, and was studying at an art college.

Meeting him was a jolt, and we traded information and inspiration nonstop.

For the two of us back then, the most important thing in the world was to hear as much music and watch as many films as we could, and sharpen our taste on them.

I threw myself into it hard enough that it could have passed for a religious cult.

How to get to the most exciting, most artistic films — chasing that, we needed each other's information.

Nothing satisfied our appetite for cultural stimulation; we took in everything we could get.

Working at Tsutaya had its perks, and in those days I watched three films a day, every day.

Clearly an overdose of input, but after years of having my curiosity held down by the restrictions of Jehovah's Witnesses, that was about the right dose.

At Tsutaya the famous directors got their own sections, right down to the obscure old titles.

Whatever I liked, I worked through in order, one film at a time.

At three a day the plots start bleeding into each other until nothing makes sense, but that was fine.

I just wanted to take in everything.

In manga there is that scene where an alien arrives on Earth and learns about the planet by watching movies nonstop. That was exactly me.

Through films I learned about the world, about what living is, about how people deal with each other, and I grew an odd sensibility out of it.

# The Style We Liked

The staff had every taste imaginable, and I took influence from all of them and watched all sorts of things, but what T and I loved most were the seventies underground films — psychedelic, warped, macabre.

The masterpiece, for us, was The Holy Mountain, by the Chilean director Alejandro Jodorowsky.

A seventies psychedelic story about a cult, and the story itself is possessed by something divine.

The plot builds beautifully, and the heat rises in you.

At the very end the film turns over every value you walked in with, and that method left me stunned.

I won't say what happens, since that would spoil it, but it strips your own values off you, so if it sounds interesting, watch it.

Japanese films left a mark on me too. What we liked was Nagisa Oshima's sixties work.

Society was moving hard in that period, and it produced a lot of charged work.

There is an Oshima film called Night and Fog in Japan, a fiercely political one about the Anpo struggle against the U.S.–Japan security treaty, and it comes with a story: the authorities clamped down on speech so hard that the production only got a few days.

Because the shooting time was so short, almost every scene is one long take for efficiency, and the actors had to memorize long stretches of dialogue and land the take on the first try, so they were desperate.

Add the period, the pressure from the authorities, and how extreme the content is, and the film moves with tremendous immediacy.

You can see the taut air of the night before a revolution, the heat of young people rising, the momentum of citizens filled with a sense of justice as they start to act.

Anyone who follows my Twitter account should find it interesting.

It fed my interest in anti-establishment politics, revolution, upheaval.
