## Kasol

We came to Kasol, a village in the Parvati Valley.

The village was the traveler hub for the whole area, and interesting oddballs collected there from all over the world.

People traveling in Asia tended to be strange as a rule, and among them, the travelers who came to India were oddballs, every one of them.

Odder still was traveling the Himalayas in the rainy season, and of those who did, the ones who came to Parvati were, overwhelmingly, a dubious crowd.

The main reason was that this valley had the highest-quality hashish in the world.

The valley drew travelers who had sunk deep into Indian culture and traveled the holy places of Shiva, and hippies wandering in the world of consciousness.

Here, a traveler who had been on the road for years was just an ordinary person, and plenty of them earned their money carrying hash out to other places.

In short, it was a den of couriers and mafia types, and at the same time the spiritual home of hippies with real years behind them.

## Two Faces

The area had two faces: a gentle, relaxed one and a greedy, dangerous one.

There were several reasons for the gentle face. The mountainsides were so steep that most of the villages couldn't be reached by car or motorcycle — you got there on foot or by donkey and no other way — so there was little traffic in or out, hardly any movement at all, and above all it was quiet.

On top of that, the kind, unjaded smiles you only find in villages deep in the mountains, and the ease that came from there being no one around but your own people, made the gentle face stronger still.

And with the best hashish in the world there in quantity at the lowest prices, most of the travelers spent all day smoking cannabis, which gave the whole place a warm, unhurried air.

Low prices were another big part of what let a traveler relax; a room with a window onto that deep-mountain view went for about 70 yen a night — around fifty-five cents — and with food, firewood, and cannabis all figured in, a day there cost about 150 yen, roughly a dollar twenty.

In practice, less than one day's pay from a job in Japan covered about a month of simple living, and for a traveler who wanted to spend time slowly, that was as convenient as it could be.

The travelers smoked cannabis and lived at an easy pace, reading, playing chess, passing time with friends, throwing themselves into making things.

That rich way of spending time was what made the atmosphere so easy to be in.
