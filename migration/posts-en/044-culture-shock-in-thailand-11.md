# The Hill Tribe Boy

The next morning I woke to the sunrise and the calls of birds.

The faintly cold air of a mountain morning felt good.

When I came out of the room, breakfast had already been laid out.

I ate, packed up my things, and got ready to leave.

Today was the day, at last, that we would ride an elephant.

Outside the village, an elephant was waiting, along with the older guy who rode it and his kid.

Seven of us would take turns on the one elephant.

This hill tribe kid was a boy of about eight, and the mountains had clearly toughened him up — rowdy and solid, you could see it at a glance.

What surprised me most was what he had on his feet: not hiking boots, but flip-flops with holes worn through them.

Here I was in sneakers, slipping and struggling to keep up, while the kid went trotting along the mountain trail in his busted flip-flops.

What was out of the ordinary for me was probably just an ordinary day for him.

# The Elephant

We took turns on the elephant as we walked the road to the next village.

After a while, my turn finally came.

It stood maybe three meters (ten feet) tall.

Something like a rope ladder hung down from the saddle strapped to its back.

I climbed up the animal's side and sat down in the saddle.

The view had already been good; now it got better.

The elephant walked slowly, but its stride was long, which added up to a decent speed.

Every time it moved a leg my body swayed, and I felt the weight of each step.

Riding on its back, looking down at its head, I noticed for the first time that elephants apparently have hair up there.

Not thick hair, either — more of a balding head.

The kind of hair the grandfather in the cartoon Chibi Maruko-chan has, or the TV director Ben Wada.

Of course it isn't going bald — that's simply how the hair grows — but inside my head I was grinning to myself: there's Ben Wada.

We climbed on and off in turns until we finally reached the next village.

From there it was on foot down to the base of the mountain.

The descent was even more slippery, and the flat soles of my sneakers made it worse.

I was walking carefully, nervously, and then I stepped on a patch wet with spring water and went down hard.

No injuries, luckily, but half my body was covered in mud.

We came out of the mountains at a different place from where we had started the climb, and a car was there to pick us up.

We made it back to the guesthouse in one piece, and the two-day, one-night hill tribe tour was over.

This time too, there had been a lot of experiences I knew nothing about.

I liked the feeling of it — going from life as a Jehovah's Witness kid in a birdcage straight out into the wider world.

# Ecstasy

The day after we got back from the tour, my legs were still sore.

I was due back in Bangkok the next day, so this was my last day in Chiang Mai.

After this it was Bangkok, then Osaka — city life waiting for me.

I still had the ecstasy tablet I had picked up on Koh Phangan and had been wondering what to do with it, and if I was going to take it, today was the last chance to do it somewhere calm.

I asked the college kid who had been on the tour with me and was staying at the same guesthouse whether he was interested in taking it with me, and he turned me down on the spot.

Which, fair enough.

Nothing for it but to take it alone.

From what I'd heard, it brings on a strong euphoria, with stomach pain and liver damage for side effects.

I steeled myself and swallowed it, and after lying around the guesthouse for a while, the euphoria came welling up.

And that was a shock.

I hadn't known that this was what feeling happy felt like.

The frightening part is that up until then I had never experienced feeling happy.

At least, I had never experienced the same thing as that emotion, the one made of the hormones the ecstasy tablet stirs up.

What I realized was that I had thought of feeling happy as uncool.

Maybe there was no other way for me to accept my own unhappiness, but I held a set of values in which what was unhappy and warped was cool and what was happy and straightforward was lame.

Part of that was brainwashing by the media, and part of it was a means of self-defense.

Feeling that happiness for myself let an acceptance take hold: it was all right to be happy.

Where I had been keeping a tight grip on the reins and fighting the world, now it felt like I could take the world in.

The whole thing lasted only a few hours, but together with what happened on Koh Phangan, it worked as a switch that turned something inside me over.
