# One Notebook

The one thing that made the extreme boredom at my sister's house in Peru even remotely bearable was a single notebook.

Keeping a diary or writing compositions had never been my strong suit; for the diary assignments in elementary school, I wrote the bare minimum number of characters that would still count as homework handed in.

In composition class no words came to me at all, and filling in the blank space on the writing paper was a struggle.

Part of the reason was that my handwriting was extremely bad, that I could not memorize kanji at all, and that I both disliked and was bad at the whole business of writing characters.

Though I loved reading books, for some reason writing characters was something I detested.

An aversion of unknown origin.

And the bigger reason I was bad at diaries and compositions was that my heart was numb and I could not feel much emotion, and that expressing what I was thinking inside did not come easily.

Then I, of all people, got stuck in a single room under a kind of half house arrest, handed infinite time, with absolutely nothing else to do, and so began writing down in a notebook the thoughts I had kept hidden away.

Back then, for some reason, I thought expressing what was in my heart was an embarrassing thing, and keeping a diary made me feel the same way.

So I hid the notebook and wrote in it while my sister was out, and I swore that this notebook would never be seen by anyone for as long as I lived.

![Close-up of a person in an orange jacket writing with a pen in an open notebook](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog20-1.jpg)

My life until then had consisted of nothing but school and the life of a Jehovah's Witness, so there had been no time to face my own thoughts, and even if there had been, living was too painful for me to face them.

For the first time in my life, I had a great deal of time to face myself, and I was completely cut off from the distractions around me.

Add to that the sense of release at having broken out of the framework I had lived in until then, and facing myself came naturally.

# The Complexes I Wrote Out

As I wrote in the notebook, what I was thinking and what I wanted to do from then on faintly began to take shape.

What came out was that there was an outrageous number of complexes inside me.

Any number of complexes, ranging over all sorts of things.

I think I had a complex about every single part of myself.

I suspect I had been contaminated too deeply by the culture of comparison, society's mainstream values.

Mostly they were external, material complexes.

At the time my thinking had not yet reached what was inside.

Some of the complexes still make sense to me looking back now, but there were plenty that made no sense whatsoever.

I am tall, one hundred seventy-eight centimeters (five foot ten), and yet back then I wanted to be taller.

A complete mystery.

Why I thought such a thing, I cannot remember.

Another was that the little wisp of hair growing on my chest bothered me, and I thought about saving up money someday to have it removed.

Even worries that would later seem ridiculous, I think I agonized over fairly seriously at the time.

Not wanting anyone to see my chest hair, and therefore not wanting to get in the pool, is also an indirect cause of my dropping out of high school.

I wrote on and on about things I had never breathed a word of to anyone, and as a way of coming to know myself, I think it was an extremely meaningful stretch of time.

It is not the kind of thing one can easily do without being in a situation like that, and I think it was what I needed most in that period of my life.

# I Want an Ordinary Life

Another thing I realized all over again was that what I wanted was simply a commonplace, ordinary life.

An ordinary household where there is a father and a mother, as there normally are, where the family gets along, where they are not extremely poor, and where they do not practice a strange religion.

Watching television, going out somewhere, playing with friends on Sundays.

![Young people in a street parade wearing tall red woven headdresses and striped costumes](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog20-2.jpg)

Once you got to high school, talking with girls, going on dates, going to karaoke with friends — an utterly commonplace, ordinary life.

An ordinary life was, for me, something absolutely out of reach, and it shone in a way that dazzled me.

To want ordinary things means to feel that you fall short of ordinary, and that, I think, was connected to all those complexes as well.

What I lost in twelve years of life as a Jehovah's Witness was simply too great, and I could not obtain it, but the shape of what I wanted had begun to come dimly into view.
