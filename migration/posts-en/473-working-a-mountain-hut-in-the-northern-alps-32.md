## Marine Day

The manager was always saying it: Marine Day is coming.

The month between opening the hut for the season and Marine Day went into getting ready for the busy season.

About 70 percent of a mountain hut's profit comes in during those three weeks.

At the busiest times, a hut will even go over capacity.

At a mountain hut, turning people away just because you're full is against the law, and the climbers have nowhere else to go anyway, so there's no choice but to take them in.

Our hut held sixty, but at the very busiest we counted eighty-four.

At normal capacity, three people sleep side by side on three tatami mats — a space of about five square meters (fifty square feet).

The bunks come in upper and lower tiers, so that makes six people to every three mats.

That alone is pretty cramped, but at the very busiest, it's eight people to three mats, upper and lower tiers combined.

And there are no showers at a mountain hut, so everyone still smells of the day's climb.

Eight sweaty climbers to three tatami mats makes for a fairly intense scene.

But for all the physical discomfort, the good ache of a body that has climbed all day, the satisfaction of having made it up a mountain, the beautiful views stretching out forever, and the delicious food our manager cooks all come together, and everyone sits down to dinner in high spirits.

You almost never see anyone looking sour.

## Super-Labor

If anyone had it rough, it was the staff.

On super-busy days like Marine Day, the day started at 3:30 in the morning.

With a few short meal breaks in between, we worked straight through until around eleven at night.

Four hours of sleep, at most.

The manager was getting only about three.

And days like that came one after another.

Of course, Marine Day, the weekends, and the Obon holidays in mid-August were busy on another level, and on other days we could sleep a little more.

Still, a day with as much as six hours of sleep was rare.

A day off was out of the question.

It went on like this for three weeks at the very least, and for some people as long as a month and a half.

In a way, working conditions this harsh were a kind of ascetic training.

Getting through those hellish days of training raised the ceiling on how much you could work.

In fact, I came out of that mountain hut job with a grit-first mindset: when you hit your limit, you push past it on willpower.
