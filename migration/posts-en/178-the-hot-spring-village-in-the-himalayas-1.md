## Manali

The long haul on the cramped night bus was over, and we pulled into the stop in Manali.

It must have been raining right up until we got there, because the ground was wet, but the sky had cleared, and the air had that freshness you only get high in the mountains.

Green mountains ringed the town, a world away from a big city like Delhi.

Manali, up in the Himalayas, is where people come to escape the heat, and the town was packed with tourists to prove it.

Well-off Indian families walked the streets, all smiles.

A lot of the locals were Tibetan, and an old woman in the same traditional dress I had seen back when I traveled in Tibet went past leading a donkey.

They had fled the oppression of Chinese rule and come to India, from what I heard.

The country had changed, but their traditional culture seemed hardly touched by it.

Whether it is the Tibetan talent for falling into harmony with whatever is around them, or the Indian willingness to take anyone in, I couldn't say, but from the outside the Tibetans looked thoroughly at home in India.

The long ride had left me hungry, so I went into a Tibetan-run diner near the bus stop and ordered thukpa, the Tibetan noodle soup.

Strangely enough, the thukpa in India is far better than the thukpa in Tibet itself.

There were more vegetables in it and more kinds of them, and it was properly seasoned.

The difference, I suppose, between the food of a high desert and the food of rich green mountains.

Good thukpa in India was an achievement of the Chinese government — that was the sort of stupid thing going through my head.

## The Hot Spring Village

With food in me and my head settled, I set out at last for Vashisht, the hot spring village I had been waiting for.

I got into an auto-rickshaw, and we ran along a river through the mountains.

The mountains off in the distance were beautiful.

We turned off the riverside road at a sharp angle and were now climbing a steep slope in switchbacks.

At the top of the climb was Vashisht, the hot spring village.

I got out of the rickshaw in the village and was looking around, wondering where to go first, when the temple with the hot springs caught my eye.

Families were coming out of it with their hair still wet, so there was no mistaking it.

Watching from a distance outside, I saw an East Asian man with long hair and a beard come out, and something about his clothes said Japanese, so I went and spoke to him.

I asked whether he knew my friends N and R.

And the answer, of all things: "I don't know R, but N is staying at the same guesthouse I am. I'm heading back there now — come with me."

Back in Goa I had already been amazed at how tightly travelers are connected and at the coincidences that bring them together, and this time, too, coincidence stepped in almost the moment I arrived.

That was how my arrival in the hot spring village began — full of promise.
