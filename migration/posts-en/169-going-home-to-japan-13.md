## Trauma

My time with my first girlfriend went smoothly.

But as it went on, I came to see the contradiction and the trauma inside me.

Without realizing it, I had begun hiding the relationship from my mother.

My mother carried her own trauma from the divorce, and in our house the subject of my father was taboo.

On top of that, the Christian fundamentalist values of Jehovah's Witnesses put as much distance as possible between me and anything sexual or romantic.

The two combined, and I grew up in a house where anything to do with sex was a forbidden subject.

Even in an ordinary family, a kissing scene coming on in the middle of a TV drama probably makes things awkward; ours was the same thing pushed to an extreme.

Jehovah's Witnesses treat contact with the opposite sex as dangerous, and a man and a woman spending time alone together is forbidden.

Temptation from the opposite sex equals the devil whispering in your ear — that was about the level of it.

For religious reasons I was kept under strict control all through my teens so that I would have nothing to do with girls, and whenever my mother caught the scent of any interest in one, she would jeer, "just like the father who left," and the whole thing would be pushed back down, guilt and shame along with it.

She was doing it unconsciously, of course, but that pressure from her took root in my own unconscious as trauma.

## The Unconscious

That unconscious trauma was what made me hesitate to tell my mother about my girlfriend.

In the conscious part of me, there may have been something in it that came close to cheating.

Growing up as the son in a single-mother household probably means playing, without knowing it, the role of your mother's male partner.

Anyone raised in a house where dating could be talked about normally won't understand the feeling, but in the world I grew up in, hiding the person I loved was the natural thing to do.

Still, hiding it only went so far — the phone would ring, I would come home late from work — so my mother must have known; we got through it by never touching the subject.

Now that I look back on it, the whole thing was badly warped, sick even, but back then I treated that contradiction, that warp the trauma had left, as part of my personality.

It bothered I-chan, but she was good enough not to press me about it.

None of us — me, my mother, I-chan — had the emotional maturity back then to face reality and build healthy relationships.
