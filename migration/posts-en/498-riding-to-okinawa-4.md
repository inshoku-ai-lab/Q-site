## To the Port

It was the first time in my life I'd slept rough by the side of a road, but the sense of release won out over any nerves or fear.

I hurriedly stuffed my sleeping bag away, folded up my camping mat, and packed everything into the bike bags.

In the morning mist just after dawn, I climbed onto the bike and let it carry me down the mountain road.

Coasting down a mountain road early on a December morning is pretty cold, but the rush is unreal.

By the time I reached the city of Wakayama, the sun was up and the day was starting to warm.

Going by the map, I headed for the port.

As the port came into view, I could feel the sea close by, and ferries and fishing boats caught my eye.

The sense that the journey had finally begun grew stronger.

At the port, I bought a ferry ticket.

The ferry was leaving in about fifteen minutes, apparently.

With the time to spare, I picked up breakfast at a convenience store and got some food in me.

In Japan there's a convenience store wherever you go, so it didn't look like water or food would ever be a problem.

## To Tokushima

The ferry pulled out with my bike on board, bound for Tokushima.

Up to that moment, I'd been a day's ride from my family home, so I could have gone back any time I wanted.

But once I was on a ferry pulling away from land, the option of turning back vanished completely, and there was nothing left to do but push on toward Okinawa.

After a while, Shikoku came into view.

When you travel under your own power, by bicycle, land across the sea starts to take on the air of a foreign country, the way it must have for people in Japan's Edo period, the age of the samurai.

Towns, sea, and land all felt life-size.

## Shikoku

The ferry docked, and I set foot on Shikoku for the first time in my life.

Taken to an extreme, Osaka, where I was born and raised, and Shikoku are next-door neighbors with a stretch of sea between them, but there's not much back-and-forth.

People from Shikoku might come to Osaka once in a while, but people from Osaka have hardly any reason to go to Shikoku except sightseeing.

Arriving in Tokushima, what struck me was that the place really was cut out for sightseeing.

The moment I landed on the Shikoku side, I felt the atmosphere change all at once.

It's hard to describe, but even in December, everything felt warm and easygoing.

My heart leapt at the thought of the Shikoku ride ahead.
