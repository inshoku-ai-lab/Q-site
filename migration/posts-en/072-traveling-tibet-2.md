# The Tibetan Autonomous Region

Past the Tanggula Pass, the seventh highest in the world, we were already in the Tibetan Autonomous Region.

Lhasa, the Tibetan capital, was still only halfway off, and we spent one more night being rocked around in the sleeper bus.

I woke the next day to find we had already crossed into Tibetan cultural country.

The wasteland still stretched on and on, but buildings with a Tibetan look to them began to appear here and there.

The closer we got to the capital, the more Tibetans in traditional dress we saw.

Signs in Tibetan showed up as well, and the feel of the place was clearly no longer mainland China.

# The Guesthouse

The sleeper bus stopped in the center of Lhasa.

That was the end of the long bus ride, and the end of the altitude sickness.

The tightness in my chest, the headache, the nausea — all of it had settled.

We decided to stay at the biggest guesthouse in town, the one listed in the guidebook.

It was an old wooden building, and you could feel the years in it.

It had apparently been a guesthouse for a long time too; travelers had been coming from all over for years, and the place had become the hub for anyone visiting Tibet.

A guestbook sat at the entrance, filled with messages in all sorts of languages.

Plenty of them were in Japanese.

We were put in a large dormitory room.

It slept six: the four of us, plus a German and a Mexican.

Worn out from the long ride, we ate inside the guesthouse and went to bed.

# The Old Town of Lhasa

The next day we left our packs in the room and went out to wander the town.

The bus road was paved with asphalt, but most of the old town was laid with cobblestone.

An old man, the strong sun cut into deep wrinkles across his face, walked alongside a donkey with a load on its back.

Tibetan grandmothers lined the street, selling vegetables and medicinal herbs from their fields, laid out on the ground.

Besides the unidentifiable herbs there were unidentifiable powders and liquids.

Dried meat of every kind, too.

The grandmothers were all wrapped in the distinctive traditional Tibetan dress.

Wearing colored stones seems to be part of the tradition, and every one of them had the same kinds on.

The stones came in red, yellow, and green, left in their raw form — not cut, not shaped.

I learned later that the red was mountain coral, the yellow amber, and the green turquoise, each carrying its own spiritual meaning.

# Prayer Wheels

Many of the grandmothers were devout, keeping a mani wheel — a wooden stick about twenty centimeters (eight inches) long with a metal cylinder mounted on it — spinning without a break.

A sutra is written on the metal part, and one full rotation counts as having read the sutra once. A handy device.

The more you read, the more merit you build up, so the grandmothers keep them turning all day without rest.

What the grandmothers carry is the portable kind, but around town there are big fixed ones as well.

The fixed ones stand in rows along the side of the street, so you can set them spinning as you walk past. Well designed.

And inside the temples scattered through the city there are enormous ones, about two meters (six and a half feet) tall.

Maybe bigger means more merit.

The largest are said to run over six meters (twenty feet).

# Prayer Flags

There are other tools for reading sutras and building merit efficiently.

The famous one is the tarcho, also called the Tibetan flag: a long run of colorful flags in five colors.

Cords strung with dozens, even hundreds of flags — blue, white, red, green, yellow — are put up wherever the wind runs well, on mountaintops and in valleys.

Every time the wind blows and snaps the flags, that counts as one reading of the sutra, which makes it a fairly efficient way to build merit.

There was an efficiency and a science there, particular to Tibetan culture.

# Tibetan Food

On the street corners they sell momo, a kind of Tibetan steamed dumpling.

In Tibet you eat them with chili sauce instead of ponzu, the citrus-soy dip you would use in Japan.

The filling is vegetables, pork, or mutton.

Tibetan cooking uses a lot of mutton.

I could not handle the particular smell of it.

Thukpa, the Tibetan take on udon noodles, is another of the standard dishes.

But in the local diners the thukpa broth was almost always made from dried mutton, and I never got used to that heavy smell.

As far as eating went, things turned plain in a hurry once we reached the Tibetan Autonomous Region.
