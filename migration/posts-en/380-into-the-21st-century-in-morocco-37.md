## Desert Climate

By day we danced half-naked under the desert sun, but at night, with the sun gone, the temperature plunged.

That's simply what desert climates do, but living through it was astonishing.

In a town or a forest, buildings, humidity, and trees hold on to the warmth and the air, so the difference between day and night temperatures never gets very large, but out here in the middle of the Sahara, it was a different story.

Nothing buffered the change: by day the fierce direct sun made it hot enough to scorch you, but at night the place showed its other face, that of a highland one thousand meters (3,300 feet) up.

This was an oasis, so maybe the water held some of the warmth in, but even so, a freezing cold came on.

The heat soaked up during the day went straight back into the sky, and we went through a temperature swing that was almost frightening.

The change was so extreme that we called the daytime summer and the night winter.

## Layers

We had danced ourselves ragged under the direct sun, our skin burning, but we came back to the tent in time for sunset.

Once it got dark, our things would turn into a jumble, so the idea was to get everything sorted while it was still light and have our warm clothes ready.

If you waited until the sun had set, it was already too late: the Sahara's sudden drop in temperature would leave you freezing.

Getting ready for winter, we pulled out every piece of clothing we had and put it on, one layer after another.

The only way to survive a night in the desert is to wear everything you own.

## Sunset

Fully dressed for winter, we settled down by the lake.

The setting sun reflected off the surface of the oasis and threw sharp light back at us.

It was still dazzling, but it no longer had the strength of the midday sun, and we could feel the seasons changing in real time.

In the hour around sunset, we went from early fall to late fall.

Watching the temperature change on a scale I had never known, all within a single hour, was an interesting experience.
