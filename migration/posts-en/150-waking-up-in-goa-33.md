## The Consciousness Revolution

Goa Gil's forty-eight hours of DJing had unmistakably done its work on people's consciousness.

It seemed to have worked on Y, the guy I was out with every day; he was not his usual self.

His stamina was enormous, so LSD did not hit him easily, and at this party he took double, two of the small LSD tabs, which combined with the awakening ritual of Goa Gil's marathon set to give him what he called a superconscious experience.

He said he had grasped the secret of life — I'm invincible now, and there's nothing left for me to worry about.

Back in ordinary life in Japan, if you met someone talking like that, you'd take him for a nutcase and want to keep your distance; in the daily life of Goa, which is about as far from ordinary as life gets, it makes you wonder whether that kind of thing might actually be possible.

He just kept repeating it: I'm free now.

Superconscious as he was, he stayed calm, and he seemed to understand perfectly well that none of it was reaching me.

I'll prove it with the way I live, so just watch, he said.

From that point on, a personality already full of confidence hardened into a solid block of it.

Hesitation went out of him, and every single thing he did came with perfect conviction.

But a man with too much confidence in himself produces friction sometimes.

He was becoming two things at once: a hero who had shed a skin and leveled up, and a dictator, stubborn and deaf to reason.

## Y's Dream

Back in Japan he worked as an assistant director on films, and he had come to India on a few months off between wrapping one picture and the start of shooting on the next.

He dreamed of becoming a director himself one day and shooting his own films.

Then Goa handed him an experience that changed his life, and he began wanting, somehow, to get this Goa miracle onto film.

With the sense that nothing was impossible for him, he made up his mind to drive straight at that dream.

His deadline for going home was a week off, and he was having such a good time that he wanted to throw the whole thing over — his standing as an assistant director, the place in Tokyo's Shibuya he was still paying rent on — and just bury himself in Goa.

At the same time, with responsibilities on him, he could not drop everything overnight, and he had been agonizing over it until the vision of putting Goa on film settled the matter, and he seemed to reach a decision: put up with the present for the sake of the dream ahead.

For now he would go home to Japan, do what needed doing, and come back to Goa.

Because he had the dream now, Y went back to Japan without much of Goa still pulling at him.

I had run around with him every day for two months, traveled with him all the way down into the depths of the dark, and losing a comrade like that left me pretty lonely — but the friendship that would go on from here warmed me.
