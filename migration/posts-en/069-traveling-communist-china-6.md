# One More Traveler

The train ran a few hours out of Xi'an and pulled into the city of Xining.

As we stepped down onto the platform, another Japanese traveler appeared.

He had been in a different car, apparently, and had spotted us and come over, figuring we were his kind.

Like the other two, he was a student studying in Beijing.

Here too it was a case of the road being better with company, and the four of us went on together.

Everyone but me spoke fluent Chinese, which was reassuring.

# Xining

From here, traveling in China got a lot more interesting all at once.

They had been in the country over a year, so they knew their way around.

The four of us stayed at a cheap guesthouse in Xining.

Since they all spoke fluent Chinese and we all looked Asian, we were put up as Chinese guests.

With a room settled, we headed out to the market.

Vegetables and fruit I had never seen before were laid out on the stands.

Food covered every inch of the stall tables.

It did not look especially sanitary, but Thailand had been the same, and maybe it is Japan's sterilize-everything culture that is strange.

With my travel companions translating, I managed to order something like dan dan noodles.

It was stall food in a market, nothing like the expensive meal I had eaten in Shanghai, but this was the home of Chinese cooking, and it was good.

The broth, though, was stained red with chili, and the heat was outrageous.

Back in Japan I called myself a fan of spicy food, and Karamucho, the chili-flavored potato snack, was a favorite — but real Chinese heat was on another order entirely.

That night we all went to a pool hall.

Xining had a Western-style bar, which felt out of place in China, with pool tables lined up inside.

The billiards I had played at the neighborhood bowling alley back in junior high finally came in useful, out here of all places.

Though the Beijing students turned out to be seasoned at it, and I did not stand a chance.

# Golmud

The next day we boarded a train for the city of Golmud.

Traveling with the help of companions I could trust, who spoke both Japanese and Chinese, was a great deal easier than buying my own tickets and working it out alone.

Again we took soft-seat tickets and let the train rock us along.

My backside, rubbed raw on the train out of Shanghai, still hurt.

The name of the city comes from Mongolian, apparently, and we had already left the Han Chinese cultural sphere behind.

We decided to stay a few days and let our bodies get used to the altitude.

The city sits at 2,800 meters (9,200 feet), and the Tibetan capital at 3,700 meters (12,100 feet), only about a thousand meters between them.

Mount Fuji is 3,776 meters (12,388 feet), so staying in Tibet is like staying on the summit of Fuji and never coming down.

The bigger problem, though, was the road to Tibet.

Between Golmud and Lhasa, the Tibetan capital, you have to cross Tanggula Pass, which boasts the world's seventh-highest elevation at 5,231 meters (17,162 feet).

People sometimes die of altitude sickness up there, we heard, so we played it safe and decided to go slowly.
