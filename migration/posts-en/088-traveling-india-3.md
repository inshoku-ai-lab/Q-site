# The Rainy Season in Calcutta

The rainy season hadn't ended just because we'd reached Calcutta, but at least there was none of the high-mountain chill of Ilam or Darjeeling, and I was grateful for that.

In warm weather you dry off fast, and you're never actually cold, so the misery of the rainy season was cut in half.

I was still enjoying that comfortable thought when a violent cloudburst came down on Calcutta.

The rain fell without a pause.

Outside it was pouring, but the guesthouse had no food, so going out to eat wasn't optional.

I steeled myself and went for the door, and past the entrance the street was a flood.

The rain was coming down far faster than the city could drain it.

Inside, luckily, the guesthouse stayed dry, thanks to the step up at the entrance.

A downpour by itself would have been one thing; a downpour plus a flood drained the will right out of me.

I put up with the hunger and decided to wait a while longer and see.

We waited about half a day, but the rain showed no sign of stopping, and the limit of my hunger arrived first.

At this rate the restaurants would hit closing time and the whole day would pass without a bite of food.

So we steeled ourselves, seriously this time, and decided to walk into the flood.

# The Poison Swamp

As far as I could see from the doorway, the flood covered the entire city.

A few dozen meters (a hundred feet or so) from the guesthouse there was a men's public toilet, urinals only.

That filth was in the floodwater too.

The waste of stray dogs and cats had to be in it, and so did the water from a big city's sewers.

![A red park bench half submerged in muddy floodwater, branches caught in the current behind it](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog88-1.jpg)

The thought of what that water might be carrying terrified us.

But the hunger was its own urgent problem, and the shops really were about to close.

We braced ourselves one more time and stepped into the water.

The flood came up to mid-thigh.

I rolled my pants as high as they would go and walked trying to keep them dry.

The mosquito bites I had scratched raw stung and throbbed.

It felt like the germs in the water were eating into me.

My body understood, plainly, that this was bad.

By then there was nothing left to do but laugh, and we goofed our way through the poison swamp, yelling about how bad it was.

Every step felt like it drained a little more life out of me.

My feet were somewhere under the muddy water, invisible.

My flip-flops kept threatening to slip off, but walking barefoot was far too dangerous.

Gripping the straps with my big toe and the toe next to it, I crept the whole way to the restaurant.

# The Restaurant

We were headed for the Blue Sky Café, a restaurant that served both Indian food and foreign food for a little more than a cheap diner charged, which made it popular with the foreign travelers in the neighborhood.

Inside, it was packed with travelers.

They were the sort of people who end up in Calcutta, so of course they were all treating the emergency as one more thing the trip had given them.

The room was full of smiles and noise.

![A green storefront lettered CENTRAL FEED AND SEED CO, with a motorcycle and bicycle parked outside](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog88-2.jpg)

My favorite thing on their menu was the chocolate lassi.

Lassi, the standard Indian yogurt drink, is yogurt, sugar, and water mixed together, and this was that with chocolate in it.

I had never eaten chocolate and yogurt together before, and the balance of bitter and sour and sweet broke my assumptions apart, in the best way.

We finished the meal and loaded up on snacks at a shop on the way back.

Insurance, in case the flood kept going.

We had walked through a flood in a downpour and were soaked to the skin, but our stomachs were full and we had our snacks.

The bug bites on my legs, though, ached and throbbed.

Had we crossed the line after all?

My friends were reporting the same symptoms.

We washed our legs with clean water and rubbed in salt, a lot of it.

It hurt more, but it had to be killing whatever was in there.

Even the pain seemed to change into a healthy kind of pain.

# The Next Day

The disinfecting seemed to have worked — no pain at all.

Aftercare matters, apparently.

The rain had stopped and patches of blue sky were showing, but the flood was still there.

Now that we knew salt would neutralize the poison swamp, we walked into the flood with more confidence than the day before.

It had already become routine.

The day after that.

The sky cleared and the flood was over.

Now the government's cleanup began.

Crews of specialists came through, spraying disinfectant across the city and through the guesthouses.

They kept it up for several days, and the flood episode closed in a hard smell of chlorine.
