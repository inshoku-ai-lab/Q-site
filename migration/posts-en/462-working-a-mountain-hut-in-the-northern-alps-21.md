## Knives

The lessons on the kitchen knife, the foundation of all cooking, weren't over yet.

I was taught to always put a cloth under the cutting board.

Apparently it soaks up the excess shock so the blade doesn't get damaged, and it keeps the board from slipping, so you can go on cutting steadily.

The next thing I learned was how to carry a knife.

In a big kitchen, you sometimes have to move from one table to another with a knife in your hand.

Carry it any old way at a time like that, and you might hurt someone.

To prevent that, there was a set way of doing it.

First, hold the knife in your right hand, level, in front of your stomach, with the edge facing toward you.

Then pinch the tip with your left hand. That steadies the knife, so you can carry it without hurting anyone.

## Protecting the Blade

Protecting the knife was part of a cook's duty, too.

When you cut, don't press the knife too hard into the board.

Moving it up and down is one thing, but never move it sideways.

Just sliding the blade sideways while it's touching the board is enough to damage it, he said.

When moving vegetables from the board into a pot, I was taught to flip the knife over, set the spine against the board, and use it to push the vegetables off into the pot.

The rules were ridiculously detailed and a real pain, but every one of them made sense, so I had no trouble accepting them.

## Kyabesen

Once I could hold a knife properly, I moved on to finely shredded cabbage, commonly called *kyabesen*.

At the restaurant where J trained, apparently the first stretch was nothing but *kyabesen*, over and over.

It sounds like a training story out of a manga, but apparently it's true.

Stand up straight, in exactly the right spot, and take the knife in hand.

With the fingers of your left hand curled upright, hold the cabbage down firmly.

From there, bring the knife down just short of touching your left hand.

Even then, you don't chop straight down. You slide the knife away from you as it goes down, so you don't crush the cut face of the cabbage.

Crush the cut face, and the *kyabesen* loses its crunch.

The width has to be perfectly uniform, too, or the crunch comes out uneven and it's no longer top-grade *kyabesen*.

If you can't hold it up to the light and see through to the other side, it's apparently too thick.

Once it's cut perfectly even like this, the *kyabesen* soaks in fresh, cold water for about an hour before it's served to the guests.

Naturally, the quality of the water, its temperature, and the soaking time all have to be perfect too.

To me it had only ever been shredded cabbage, so professionalism this thorough, over *kyabesen* of all things, came as a culture shock.

I felt as if I'd been shown exactly how far out of my league I was. So this was what a professional looked like.
