# Gay Monkeys

The room at my guesthouse had a balcony, and the Ganges was right there beside it.

I liked to sit out in a chair with the view in front of me and drink chai.

Across the street from the balcony the buildings stood packed eave to eave, and a dozen or so monkeys were playing on the roofs.

I was watching them idly when one monkey climbed onto another and started thrusting its hips.

A male and a female mating, I figured, and went on watching.

But the female in front pulled away almost at once, and the male behind her walked off looking dejected.

Then, from behind that "male," "another male" climbed on and began thrusting, fast.

After about ten seconds of it, the male in front ran away.

Huh???

Male? Female? Male???

What? India? Huh???

For a moment the shock left my head completely blank.

Homosexuality in monkeys?

India, so anything goes?

Faced with a phenomenon I had no idea how to interpret, the only thing I could do was say "That's India for you!" and consider the matter settled.

# Varanasi's Cremation Ground

One day, out walking along the Ganges, I came upon a place where they were burning a human body.

The smell was strong enough that you would not want to be standing downwind.

![Several fires burning on the stone steps of a crowded riverbank below old buildings, wooden boats moored at the water](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog95-1.jpg)

On the cremation platform, the stacked firewood was sending up a tall flame.

Several hours seemed to have passed since it was lit, and the body had gone black and shrunk.

Watching a body of flesh and blood burn away right in front of you is a fairly intense experience.

The last shape a human being takes as it burns down to nothing — not something you get much chance to see as long as you are living in Japan.

Standing in front of that final part of a person, the part that had always been covered over in darkness, put me in a mood I cannot put a name to.

The sense of the bottom dropping out of everything I valued about life — and, at the same time, of realizing that I myself was not going to fall through.

"Death" went from a fear I could not see to a fear I could see and that was not all that frightening.

Having looked at death straight on, life seemed to gain in meaning, and everything in view carried more presence than it had before.

# Pune

Varanasi was interesting in all sorts of ways, but days with the India-ness laid on that thick were starting to wear me out, so after a few of them I decided to head south without hanging around.

For the next destination I picked Pune.

It sits between Varanasi and Goa, and apparently you can study yoga there.

Learning yoga in Rishikesh, where the Beatles supposedly stayed, had struck me as an interesting idea, but Rishikesh is in the north of India, the exact opposite direction from Goa in the south, where I was headed.

So what to do, I thought, and while flipping through the guidebook, the place that caught my eye was Pune.

It was on the way to Goa, so I might as well stop in and take some yoga. A decision that light.

I had no particular interest in yoga — as far as I understood it, it was the Indian stretching a video game character does — but getting a taste of Indian culture seemed like no bad thing.

# Moving On

I booked a train ticket, and the next day waited for the train at the scheduled hour.

The time came and the train did not.

Well, this is India, this is how it goes, I told myself, and settled in to wait.

But I waited and waited and the train did not come.

![A person sitting alone on a platform bench between two trains under the arched roof of a railway station](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog95-2.jpg)

I went to the travel information office a few times to ask, and got nothing but "not yet."

I simply kept waiting, with nothing else to be done, and about nine hours in I went and asked at the office one more time.

According to him, the train had come in on a different platform four hours earlier and had already departed.

Unbelievable. After all that waiting, I had been left behind.

Infuriating, but it was also my own fault for not catching the announcement in English.

I wanted to take it out on the station staff, but it was not their doing, and on top of that I did not know how to complain in English.

In the end I bought a ticket for the next departing train at a slightly reduced price, and with help from the station staff I got on it without further trouble.
