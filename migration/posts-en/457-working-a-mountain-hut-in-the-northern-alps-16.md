## Day One

The small hut wasn't up and running yet.

We were getting it ready to open for the season.

It all started with a top-to-bottom cleaning of the entire hut.

Left untouched for eight months, the place was coated in dust.

We swept every corner clean with brooms, then wiped everything down with wet rags.

There was nothing electric up here, no vacuum cleaner or anything like it.

Nearly all the work was done by hand.

## Beds

Once everything was clean, we got the guest rooms ready.

The tatami mats in the guest rooms weren't the traditional kind woven from rush; they'd been replaced with plastic ones.

In a place as damp as a mountain hut, plastic was apparently prized because it doesn't soak up moisture and doesn't rot.

With the tatami in order, we moved on to bringing down the futons, which had been stored in a dry part of the attic.

Getting them down from the attic, hauling them outside, and spreading them across the roof to air out was a whole routine, and a pretty tough one for just two people.

After several hours of drying, the futons were carried into the guest rooms, and we started fitting a sheet onto each one, one at a time.

The futons used at our company's huts had been developed jointly by the president and an outdoor gear company, and they were excellent: they packed down small, yet were warm and comfortable to sleep on.

They also had their own custom sheets, cut to fit those futons exactly.

## Behind the Scenes

I'm about to let out one of the mountain huts' dirty little secrets: once a fresh sheet went on at the start of the season, it wasn't changed again until the season was over.

If anyone had wanted to change the sheets, new ones could have been flown up by helicopter.

But here the huts seemed happy to lean on their special excuse: "because it's a mountain hut."

The climbers, too, seemed to notice that the sheets smelled, yet they accepted it, going along with the all-purpose clincher: "because it's a mountain hut."
