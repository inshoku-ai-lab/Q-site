# The City of Calcutta

Calcutta was absurdly big — a metropolis on a scale that shocked me.

Before I went there, "city" had meant to me a place where civilization was developed: cutting-edge science, convenient public services. A city in India meant a place with an unbelievable density of people.

Maybe it was the particular area I was staying in, but the number of laborers and children on the street was astonishing.

Around there, living on the street was nothing unusual; adults and children slept out on it with nothing but the clothes they had on.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog87-1.jpg)

There were also a lot of drivers of the hand-pulled carts called rickshaws, and they lived in them, the passenger seat for a bed.

Using one of the street's hand-pump wells and washing right there in the street was the normal thing for them.

Walk down the street at dusk and you'd see ten or so of them, from little kids to old men, stripped to their underwear, all sharing a single pump.

I never saw a woman showering in the street, so I assume there was somewhere else for them.

Seeing a public street shower for the first time, and poverty on that scale, was a culture shock.

But that was only at the beginning; as I got used to India and came to understand the culture, I started to see it differently.

Now I understand that the sense of community they share means something real to their happiness, and I see a kind of beauty in the way they connect to their own world, using the public street like a living room.

That doesn't mean I want to shower in the street, but the kind of connection between people that material wealth can't measure is something I want to hold on to.

# Poverty

Seeing extreme poverty like that firsthand had a large effect on my mental well-being.

I had thought of myself as born into a relatively poor family, but the light in the eyes of people far poorer than me set me that much freer from my own ideas about poverty.

The poverty in that area was on a level you couldn't compare to the lives of the homeless in Nishinari.

Having no house was a given, and going with no possessions at all, in nothing but the clothes on your back, was common enough.

That has to be exactly why Mother Teresa's facility was built here.

Dig into the other side of Mother Teresa and plenty of questionable things turn up, but here I want to tell it from the side of the saint I believed in at the time.

# The Paragon Guest House

The volunteers who came to Mother Teresa all stayed at the Maria Guest House.

They lived day to day in cheap rooms and threw themselves into serving the world.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog87-2.jpg)

Maybe because of that, guesthouses had sprung up in a dense cluster around the Maria Guest House, and this cheap-lodging district, called Sudder Street, had become the gathering place for foreign travelers entering the country through Calcutta.

The Paragon Guest House, where I stayed, was one of them, with a good number of travelers collected in its many narrow single rooms and dormitories.

Unlike the convent-like character of the Maria Guest House next door, what defined the Paragon was a loose, free atmosphere, and travelers from every country gathered in the open space on the top floor and shared some fine hours there.

There wasn't much in the way of sightseeing around Calcutta, so few people came for that; most were serious travelers crossing the continent.

People who had come through Tibet like me, people crossing India by motorcycle, people who had come out of Europe and through the Middle East earning their way with street performances — the place had become a waypoint on long overland routes.

We enjoyed trading live information among travelers who were actually out on the road.

Back then nobody had email yet, and there was no such thing as a culture of looking things up online.

I had only just arrived in India, and advice from travelers who had already crossed it was often worth taking.

The gatherings at that guesthouse became my way into the circle of friendship that forms around cannabis.

Something like seventy percent of the people traveling India long-term smoke cannabis.

Wine in France, beer in Germany, mint tea in Morocco, drinks at a year-end party in Japan — in India, travelers passing cannabis around is one of the ordinary customs.

The atmosphere at the Paragon really was that loose; you could smoke cannabis out on the balcony in full view and nobody minded.

They were probably paying off the police.

At dusk we would all drift together and pass around "joints" rolled from hash mixed with tobacco.

Smoking together heightened the feeling that we were all one, the cannabis put everyone in a good mood, and we howled with laughter at stupid things.

That smoking cannabis together and laughing like idiots could be this much fun went far beyond anything I had imagined, and the friendly atmosphere around it took me over all at once.
