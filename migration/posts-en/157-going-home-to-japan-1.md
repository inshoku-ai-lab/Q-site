## Bombay

From Goa I took a night bus toward Bombay, India's largest city.

The moment I arrived and got off the train, I was thrown straight back into the reality of India.

First came the rush of porters hustling for work.

Then the rush of taxi drivers hustling for fares.

After them the hotel touts, and last of all the unending rush of beggars.

Happy memories of Goa went flickering past one after another, but the hard grind of traveling in India held my attention on the present and left no room to sink into them.

## The Big City

I had a full day free before the night train to Calcutta, so I wandered around the city.

Calcutta and Pune had been cities too, but Bombay was urban on a completely different order.

At street level around the station was slum life at the very bottom of things; in the center of the city, though, high-rises stood in rows, and I found myself wondering whether the place wasn't further developed than downtown Osaka.

Brutal poverty and wealth that sucks everything dry, set against each other and living side by side — that, I suppose, is what the city of Bombay is.

The people on the street were polished and stylish, too.

A long way from the picture of Indians I had been carrying around.

My sense was that they had something much like young people in the developed world.

One street was lined with used record stalls.

Contact with city culture brought the music collector's habit from my Osaka days roaring back.

And there I was, digging through vinyl — no use at all on the road, impossible to play, nothing but a burden to haul.

A while into it, I turned up a real find.

The first pressing of *Autobahn*, the 1974 album by the German band Kraftwerk, the group people call the founders of techno.

A very rare piece, the kind that makes collectors drool, and I had no choice but to buy it.

It cost about a thousand yen, roughly eight dollars, and since it was bound to carry a premium, part of the scheme was to sell it if money ever got tight.

## McDonald's

The country days in Goa had been good, but a city after so long was stimulating and fun.

Tired of walking, I stepped into an air-conditioned McDonald's.

Cool air...

Air conditioning barely existed in rural India in those days, and the cool air inside was more precious than anything.

In Japan a McDonald's hamburger is the very model of cheap food; here in India it was fine dining, about five times the price of a curry at an ordinary eatery. (This was 1999.)

Killing and eating a cow — the symbol of the sacred in India — is the deepest taboo of all, so there is chicken in it instead of beef.

I ate a hamburger in an air-conditioned McDonald's.

An utterly ordinary scene of city life, but after all those days of the extraordinary out in the Goan countryside it felt fresh instead, and there was something settling in it.

Life in Goa had gone so far from anything normal in Japan that this was a necessary process too, a way of easing myself back into city life by degrees.
