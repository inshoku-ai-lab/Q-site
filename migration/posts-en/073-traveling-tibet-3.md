# The Potala on the Hill

In Lhasa, the capital of Tibet, there is an enormous palace called the Potala.

It is the center of both politics and religion, the residence of every leader Tibet has had, and a monastery where a great many monks live and train.

At the same time it is the biggest tourist attraction, and the place most exposed to foreign eyes.

In a city with few large buildings, the scale of the Potala is astonishing, and it tells you how much weight the place carries in Tibetan culture.

It stands on a hill near the center of town, looking down on the city.

It is a symbol of authority of a sort, and an object of worship.

Whatever its actual size, it felt like it took up half the old town.

# Touring the Potala

The Potala sits near the center of town, within walking distance of the guesthouse where we were staying.

We decided to take a whole day over it, slowly.

We bought tickets at the entrance and went in.

We tried to pass as Chinese and get the Chinese price, but the staff at the ticket window had seen enough Japanese travelers to see through us.

Inside, giant prayer wheels stood in long rows, and tourists spun them as they walked, banking the merit of having read the sutras.

I would guess the whole thing runs to about eight stories, but at any rate it is enormous, with rooms of every kind.

One room big enough for hundreds of ordained monks to meditate in at once.

Another with Tibetan Buddhist religious paintings covering every inch of the walls.

Statues of the Buddha and of the gods of Tibetan Buddhism stood everywhere, large and small.

# The Toilets of the Potala

Toilets had been surprising me all the way across China, and the Potala was no exception.

The toilets there empty through a hole in the side wall and drop straight down the outside of the building.

The place has probably stood for more than a thousand years, and since the arrangement has not changed, it must be sustainable enough.

The dung and urine that run out of the wall feed insects, birds, and animals.

Somewhere humid it would turn into fertilizer for plants, but Tibet is dry, so it dries where it falls and blows away on the wind.

It is all so completely dry that even bacteria probably cannot survive.

Anywhere near the toilets, the sting of ammonia and the fermented smell of rot get you, though maybe the monks who meditate every day can hold on to their peace of mind.

# A Strange Experience

There were rooms of every kind, but what I saw most of were the rooms lined with Buddha statues.

There were thousands of them, from giants several meters (ten feet or more) tall down to figures only a few dozen centimeters (a foot or two) high.

The statues were split up among all these small rooms, and maybe each room has a purpose of its own.

In one of those many statue rooms, something strange happened to me.

It was an ordinary room, one of the many, with nothing to mark it out.

If anything set it apart, it was that it was a little smaller than the rest.

The moment I stepped into that unremarkable room, I felt something I have no words for, something like an uncanny presence.

It was as if the air had grown several times denser, as if the humidity had jumped all at once, as if a chill and a rush of heat had arrived together, a strange, wrong feeling.

Unfortunately I had split off from my friend for a while, so there was no one to ask whether anyone else felt it, but for me it was a strange sensation I could not deny.

I grew up in a Jehovah's Witness home, a home that denied the existence of anything paranormal, so I had never believed in spiritual phenomena at all.

In middle school I even stole from shrine offering boxes, irreverent as that was.

Which is exactly why what I felt in that room left me so unsettled.

I had always regarded people who felt such things as suspect, and it had never once occurred to me that I might feel such a thing myself.

Now that I had felt it myself, the frame of my common sense seemed bent out of shape.

I could no longer call such things suspect.
