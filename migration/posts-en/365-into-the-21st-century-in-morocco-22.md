## The Japanese Garden

One day, out of the blue, we were building a Japanese garden in the middle of an island in a desert oasis.

These days you could search "Japanese garden" on your phone and come away with some kind of image or idea, but in the Sahara more than twenty years ago, all there was was an internet cafe that could barely handle email.

The Japanese garden we had in our heads looked like the grounds of a temple, smooth round pebbles laid out in neat rows, so we planned to make something along those lines.

But when we actually got out to the island where the festival would be held, there were no pretty round pebbles — just reddish-brown rocks lying around everywhere.

I know now that round pebbles only become pebbles after years of being worn down by flowing rivers and pounding waves, but back then it never crossed our minds.

Here we are in the desert, no pebbles — now what? That was about where we stood.

## Desperate Measures

There was nothing else for it, so we decided to haul in rough rocks from all over the desert.

We matched them for size as best we could, matched the colors too, and got the whole thing looking more or less nice.

The rock garden was sparse at first, but as the rocks piled up, it slowly started to look like art someone had made on purpose.

Every few days we came out to the site and spent the day on our self-styled Japanese garden.

In the end, some kind of man-made object rose up out of the desert oasis, and we stood back and praised our own work.

You could call it baffling, and it was baffling, but there was some sort of pattern to it, and in a vague way it was beautiful.

If people came across this object a thousand years from now, they might end up studying it.

## Volunteers in the Oasis

When we'd first met Gandalf a while back, he had been alone at the festival site; now about twenty people were spread out across it, working.

R, the French artist, was hard at work too, making art pieces for the chill-out area Gandalf ran.

What he was making were small boats that could hold lit candles; on the festival nights, apparently, they would float at the water's edge and turn into a sculpture of light.

Y had joined the dance floor team and was doing real physical labor, building the festival stage.

When I saw Y again after a few days, he was tanned golden brown and covered in the sweat of manual labor and the dust of the desert, and the Tokyo-writer air he'd brought with him to Morocco had disappeared somewhere.
