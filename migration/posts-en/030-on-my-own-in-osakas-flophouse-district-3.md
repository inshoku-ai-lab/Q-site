# Working Part-Time at Tsutaya

As the first step toward my dream of "getting a job connected to movies," I started a part-time job at a large video rental store.

The store sat at the exit of Kintetsu Abeno Station, a fairly big station.

My wish to try living in a city had come true, and splendidly so.

The population density, the coming and going of people, the scale of the store — all of it was completely different from the life I had known until then.

This store boasted sales figures among the highest in all of Japan, and the foot traffic was no joke.

There were about ten register counters in all, and depending on the day of the week and the hour, long lines would form, so that once you got on a register, several hours of nothing but serving customers followed.

It happened to be the exact same line of work as my older brother's part-time job, but not because I had wanted to be a video rental clerk; I only wanted access to a huge quantity of videos and CDs.

For my shift, I was assigned to the late-night slot, which was short-staffed.

At the earliest I came in at eight in the evening, at the latest at ten, and work ended at two in the morning.

Because of the law, only men worked that time slot.

# The Cast of Characters

The part-timers numbered more than a hundred in all, with friend groups and other factions formed among them, which had a certain big-city feeling to it.

There were three main factions: the wholesome, clean-cut daytime shift people; the stylish party-going evening shift people; and us on the late-night shift, where the movie geeks and music geeks gathered.

![A long row of people holding hands, silhouetted against a dusk sky at the horizon](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog30-1.jpg)

More than half of the part-timers had come to work there with much the same aim as mine, and many of them were movie and music fanatics or people active in the arts, so the place had become one of those hubs where people with similar interests gather.

Apart from my brother, I had never met anyone who was interested in the same sorts of things, so being surrounded by people close to my age with similar interests was a great joy.

Working there at an age when I should by rights have been in high school, I was the youngest.

My fellow part-timers didn't take me very seriously, but I was proud to have become a member of this group so young.

Perhaps because of the sheer number of part-timers, there was the kind of wall around people's hearts that is particular to cities, and at first they were hard to approach, but as time passed we gradually grew closer and I came to know what each of them was like.

The late-night crew had a lot of oddballs, perhaps because of the hour, and encountering types of people I had never met before left me excited.

The people I had met in my life up to then were either the sort who got hooked on religion or the sort living their lives on the inertia of the environment they were born and raised in.

Meeting a whole crowd of people who were doing what they wanted to do of their own free will was a first for me, and it was an enormous jolt.

Everyone on the late-night shift had their own distinct personality, but a few of them had one that stood out especially.

# N

N was a twenty-six-year-old who called himself a cultural aristocrat.

Shaved head, like me.

His knowledge of film ran extremely deep, so the entire late-night crew, all of them film fanatics, held him in high regard.

He was one of the foremost film fanatics in Japan and yet put that ability to work for around a thousand yen an hour (about eight dollars), which made him prized by the store manager too, and gave him real clout.

His charisma carried great influence within the late-night crew, and whatever he called good and did himself, everyone naturally followed.

When he bought a Macintosh and started editing photographs, everyone bought Macs; when he became a huge fan of Ryoko Hirosue, everyone became fans of Ryoko Hirosue; when he wandered around Southeast Asia, everyone wandered around Southeast Asia.

He was a great admirer of the director Yasujiro Ozu, and he valued the wabi-sabi sensibility particular to Japan.

Naturally the whole late-night crew, myself included, became fans of Yasujiro Ozu, discussed the world of Ozu's films together, and shared a certain sense of solidarity.

# M

M was twenty-two and aiming to be a filmmaker.

On film he ranked a step below N, but he was well versed in music and in culture generally, and the two of them were the "two great charismatic pillars" of the late-night shift.

![Loose coils of perforated 35mm film strip against a black background](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog30-2.jpg)

M and N ran the store's film showcase section between them, and carried weight among all the part-timers.

M knew the counterculture of the sixties especially deeply, and I was greatly influenced by the films, music, and ideas he introduced me to.

Above all he stood out in the realm of ideas, and I admired the way he held a philosophy of his own and stuck to his way of living.

# The Rest of the Lineup

There were other people overflowing with personality too: the guy in charge of comedy and partying, a pair of stoic band members, a techno musician, an athletic type nicknamed after a sporting goods store, a kindhearted idiot of a rich boy.

To this lineup I added one more color: a high school dropout, "an unimpressive brat."

The standard routine was that work ended at two in the morning, we all ate beef bowls together, and then split up.

Some days Yoshinoya, some days Matsuya.

Running down the other factions of part-timers, holding forth on film — it was not rare for us to still be in the beef bowl place at around four in the morning.

Being a freeter with no full-time job, all I had to do afterward was sleep at my leisure, but the rest of the lineup were mostly university students, so there must have been many days when they got only a few hours of sleep.

The fact that they spent the time with us anyway means they must have been enjoying themselves quite a lot.
