## Working Conditions

Work inside Japanese society, and you're bound by all kinds of legal regulations.

Those rules exist to protect workers, and also to keep them boxed in.

On the protective side, there's the Labor Standards Act, limits on working hours, the minimum wage, and so on.

But frighteningly, none of those laws applied to work at a mountain hut.

In the mountains, unexpected emergencies can happen.

When climbers are lost, you can't refuse to go out and search for them because of a limit on working hours.

Nor can you turn away climbers who show up, just because every room is full.

Hut staff had to be ready to handle whatever came up.

For those reasons, the hut was exempt from limits on working hours, so the question was never how many hours you worked in a day, only whether you worked that day or had it off.

On a workday, you did nothing but work.

## Pay

Being paid by the day instead of by the hour had the worst possible effect on the staff.

No matter how many hours you put in, the pay was the same.

Because a mountain hut was such a special case, legal regulations had nothing to do with it.

Hut jobs were popular, so people kept coming even when the pay was low.

Put all that together, and depending on who ran the place, a hut could become a breeding ground for ruthless exploitation.

And this big hut was a textbook case.

Our daily pay was Nagano Prefecture's hourly minimum wage plus a few hundred yen.

For me, though, the low pay wasn't that big a problem.

Room and board were free, and with no way to spend money up there, it was the perfect setup for saving up to travel abroad.

## Working Hours

What bothered me was the hours.

At this hut, we were on duty an average of fifteen hours a day.

And of those fifteen, about thirteen were actual work.

Even with the Labor Standards Act not applying, it seemed to go far beyond any reasonable limit.

The absurd hours made me angry, but once I'd set foot on this trail, there was nothing to do but walk it to the end.
