# Sinking

Since coming to Calcutta I had started meeting all kinds of odd travelers, but there can hardly be many as odd as K.

He was twenty-two, a student at Kyoto University, and had taken a leave of absence to travel around Asia.

Kyoto University, one of Japan's most elite schools, is known for turning out students who are bright, individual, and more than a little strange, and he was a fine representative of the type.

Among travelers there is a word: "sinking."

It means a traveler bound for somewhere else who by some fluke takes a liking to one town and settles in for a long stay, changing everything he had planned after that in order to do it.

Most of the time, "sinking" means you have found a town you want to stay in badly enough to change your plans for it, and so, for all the destructive impulse behind wrecking a schedule, it is usually a welcome and happy thing.

K was doing his "sinking" here in Calcutta.

Of all the places in the world to sink, this was a district with no tourist sights, among the poorest and filthiest anywhere — and sunk in it was a Kyoto University student with a world-class mind and a guaranteed future.

He was in deep, too: this was well past the few-week mark, and he had been sinking for nine months already.

An Indian visa is good for six months, and when it runs out you have to leave the country and get a new one.

He had flown in from Bangkok, Thailand, and for the whole six months until the visa expired he stayed on in Calcutta, apart from one or two other Indian cities he went to see.

![Two dark red passports on a yellow surface with a wooden-handled rubber stamp resting across them](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog89-1.jpg)

After it expired he flew back to Bangkok, got a second Indian visa, returned to Calcutta, and had been sinking there for three months since.

# Action

Not that he had spent those nine months the way the other travelers did, drifting through days that were stimulating and slack at the same time.

He had activities of his own, worked out with that brain of his.

At the guesthouse next door plenty of people were volunteering at Mother Teresa's facilities, but the convent atmosphere of it must not have appealed to him.

Still, in his own way, I think he wanted to be involved with the desperately poor people of the place.

His way of expressing that was to soak himself in the neighborhood and build friendships with the street children.

He had several things going, apparently, and one of them was running a shaved-ice stand on the street.

![A clear plastic cup of bright green shaved ice with a straw, standing on a concrete kerb](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog89-2.jpg)

Not that this is a place where every household has a freezer.

He had found a local ice wholesaler and hauled the huge blocks over on a handcart to run the stand.

Actually earning money on the street must have made it easy to connect with the people who lived on it.

He was expressing a love of his own, the kind that volunteer work at a convent has no way to express.

Curious, I asked him what he did in India about food hygiene, or a street-vending license, or anything of that sort.

His answer was simple: in India, none of that matters.

The ice he used was the same ice that comes in the Coke you order at a restaurant — no effect at all on Indians or long-term travelers, and guaranteed to wreck the stomach of any traveler just arrived from a developed country.

That was covered by the same simple answer: in India, none of that matters.

And precisely because licenses count for so little in India, there was one thing he did with a license that counted.

K had used those nine months to get an Indian driver's license.

Staying in India for more than four months, apparently, qualifies you to get one.

And then you can convert it into an international permit and drive in Japan too, or maybe you can't.

The way he operated ran so far outside the frame of what a traveler is that it succeeded in shaking the frame I was carrying around inside me.
