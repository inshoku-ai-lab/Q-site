# Anger, Repressed

I was angry at everything.

There was no particular object of the anger; it was simply there.

That anger never came out into the open, staying inside, repressed.

Repressed anger built up pressure, squeezing my soul tighter and tighter.

What I was angry at, I did not understand at all.

Looking back now, I think the number one cause of that anger was that my sense of self had begun to wake up.

Late-night TV, standing in bookstores reading, watching movies — through these my mind matured, and I became aware of myself.

Noticing that I existed as a self, I saw how unnatural and unreasonable a life I was living.

Aware of it, yet unable to do anything — that was me.

The boredom of high school life was another cause of the anger.

The academic level at that high school was quite low — about second-year junior high.

Many of my classmates were childish, and privately I treated them as idiots.

Boring days.

The one way out lay in the direction of culture, but that alone could not save my soul.

One morning, waking up, I noticed that an enormous anger had filled my heart.

Perhaps I had been dreaming something; the cause was unclear, but the anger was huge, huge.

![Hands in a dark suit snapping a pencil in half over a desk, splinters flying into the air](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog14-1.jpg)

As it happened, nobody was home that day, so half in tears I punched my pillow over and over.

Expressing anger outward for the first time in my life.

Sixteen years of accumulated anger.

Anger with no clear cause, anger with nowhere to go.

Not really understanding what I was angry at, I just kept punching the pillow, on and on.

This, it seems, was the first thing to push its way out before I released the anger.

# The Great Hanshin Earthquake

Right after winter break of my first year of high school ended, the Great Hanshin Earthquake struck.

I slept on the top bunk, so the shaking was tremendous.

Everything in the house toppled over and scattered — quite a disaster.

I turned on the TV, and there was news of a massive earthquake.

School had apparently been cancelled too.

Outside the house, some of the buildings had cracks in them.

More than joy at my life being spared, more than the terror of the earthquake, more than worry over the damage, what made me happy from the bottom of my heart was that school had been cancelled.

And at the same time, I found myself realizing that I hated school from the bottom of my heart.

What I wished for was not that the earthquake would spare the town and its people from damage, but that the earthquake would crush the school so I wouldn't have to go.

But unfortunately, the earthquake came only once and did not destroy the world for me.

Realizing I truly did not want to go to high school, I kept going anyway.

The fact that I could quit of my own accord underscored the pain of not quitting and continuing to go.

A few months later the sarin incident happened as well, and all I did was wish for the world to be destroyed.

Unable to think about anything properly, I got through the days with the eyes of a dead fish.

# My Sister's Marriage

Right around this time, my sister's Spanish studies as a Jehovah's Witness had progressed, and through that activity she got to know a Peruvian man who had come to Japan to work; they decided to marry.

![Two hands wearing wedding rings resting one over the other on yellow and orange flowers](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog14-2.jpg)

My sister marrying a foreigner was a major event, and the whole extended family was in an uproar.

Maybe these are the moments when true feelings show through — my aunt astonished everyone with a full-on racist remark about how mixing foreign blood into our family line would defile it.

My mother did not seem to mind much.

If the husband was a Jehovah's Witness and would keep studying the Bible from then on, anything was fine — that seemed to be her thinking.

I did not mind at all either; honestly, I couldn't have cared less.

I was desperate just to survive my own life.

For my sister, it seems, it was quite hard.

Back then, contact between foreigners and Japanese was far thinner than it is now, and there were no other cases like it.

For our family it was uncharted territory, and naturally, for my sister it was a great adventure of a choice.

But my sister did not let fear stop her, and she followed through on what she believed in.

My sister's connection to a foreign country, and her strength to overcome fear and act, influenced me without my knowing it, and some years later I would come to recognize that influence again.

This was the wellspring of the current that became my life of travel.
