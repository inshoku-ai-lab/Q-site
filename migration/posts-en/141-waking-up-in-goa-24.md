## The Wooden Floor

I went straight for the dance floor without hesitating, staked out a spot in front of the speakers, and let my whole body show how far my energy had already risen.

The sun sinking into the horizon was beautiful.

Watching it go down, I kept dancing, and it felt good.

The wooden floor was far easier to dance on than any of the floors I had danced on until then — beach, dirt, concrete.

My feet didn't sink the way they did in sand, it wasn't uneven and it didn't throw up dust the way dirt did, and there was none of the impact that concrete drives back up through your legs.

How easy that floor was to dance on showed in the party itself, and everyone was swinging their whole body into it.

## Hibernation

Music, LSD, and dancing together took me into a trance, and I did nothing but keep dancing.

As the LSD came up, the speed of my body and the speed of my mind both kept rising.

A few hours of that, and I hit my limit.

Not the limit of my stamina — the limit of how far my body could keep up with the speed of my mind.

My mind ran past what it could process and went on overloading.

My body could no longer catch that speed and would only move sluggishly.

Of course I knew this state was coming when I took the half tab of LSD.

A traveler's curiosity — the wish to see the limit that lay deeper still inside consciousness — far outweighed any fear of the risk.

Reeling with hallucinations, I made my way back to the chai stall.

I lay down right there on the straw mat at the chai stall and waited for the express train of my mind to pass.

Even lying there the LSD kept getting stronger, hallucinations came surging in, sights and sounds both, and I was thrown into a sea of sensation and inspiration.

My mind went hazy, and reality began to dance along with it.

## Fire

Through half-open eyes I was staring at a chai stall some distance away, where the woman who ran it — a chai mama, as they're called — was trying to light her kerosene stove.

The flame she lit leapt up and threw light over everything around it.

Startled by the blaze that had shot up out of nowhere, the chai mama arched back and wrestled with the fire.

The flames were big, more than she could control, and it looked as if the place was about to burn.

Woozy as I was, I wondered whether this was all right, sat up and looked again — and something was off.

I strained my eyes, but the line between reality and hallucination wouldn't come clear.

What was clear was that nobody around her was rattled; they were sitting there drinking chai.

The flame really had shot up, but in fact it was well within normal, and the assumption inside my own head had projected the rest: a chai mama flustered by a fire about to break out.

I caught that at the speed my mind was running, and I understood how readily the human brain manufactures its own assumptions and stages whole dramas out of getting things wrong.
