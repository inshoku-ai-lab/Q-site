# The Chinese Man

Walking through the garden inside the ashram, I ran into an East Asian man.

Apart from the two of us there were no other East Asians around, and out of plain curiosity we started talking.

In English there was always the language barrier, and the conversation kept stalling, but we still managed to get across who we each were.

He hated the repression in China, he told me, so he had fled through Tibet into Nepal and gone into exile, and from there had come on to India and settled in Pune.

Somewhere along the way he married a Nepali woman, and in Pune he made his living as an acupuncturist while training in yoga at Osho's ashram.

I told him I had come from Japan and stopped in Pune on the way to Goa, but that the prices here were more than my budget could take, so I was thinking of moving on soon.

At that, he generously offered: "There's a spare space at my clinic, so you can stay a few days if you like."

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog97-1.jpg)

In a city this expensive, a free place to sleep was an enormous help.

Foreigners who stay in Pune for any length of time apparently rent apartments, and over the long term it does not cost much to live there.

Normally, being put up by a man I had just met would be cause for suspicion, but he gave off such an obviously gentle air that I warmed to him, trusted him, and took him up on it.

# The Clinic

Before going to his clinic, I bought a ticket for a train leaving the following evening.

Free bed or not, there was still food to pay for, and the entrance fee for the ashram.

The longer I stayed in Pune, the shorter my journey would be; once I understood that, I decided to leave.

His clinic was a room in a high-rise apartment building, and life there was not much different from life in Japan.

Patients were coming to the clinic the next afternoon, so I would have to clear the room out; he said I could come back just for the nights, but I told him my ticket to Goa was already booked.

For dinner, he cooked me Chinese food from scratch.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog97-2.jpg)

Pune is a city, he said, so there are shops selling imported goods where you can buy some Chinese ingredients.

After days of nothing but curry, simply having the option of Chinese food was almost too good.

When you have spent time somewhere like India, where the choices are so few, the sheer range of an everyday choice becomes absurdly pleasing.

That day we talked about Japan and about China in our broken English, and turned in early.

The next morning he made me breakfast, and we spent the few hours before my train drinking tea and taking our time.

## Chewing Tobacco

After his tea, he put some Indian chewing tobacco in his mouth.

It is common enough in India and Nepal — damp, bright red tobacco that you tuck between your gum and your lip.

People tend to spit it straight out into the street, so the walls at stations and along the roads are streaked and crusted with bright red liquid.

The first time you see it you think it is dried blood, and it gives you a jolt. A classic India moment.

It was the first time I had watched someone I actually knew use the stuff, so out of curiosity I asked him to let me try.

It cost about five yen — four cents — and there was nothing dangerous about it, so he let me have some without a second thought.

I took a small amount and tucked it against my gum.

It tasted strange, and my mouth went numb.

While I was still wondering why anyone would put this in their mouth, the tobacco took hold and my head began to cloud over.

I had evidently taken a fairly large amount, and it was hitting hard.

Then, on top of that, my chest turned over and my stomach began to churn.

Before long it came on all at once, and I ran for the toilet.

I had taken far too much, and my body was overreacting.

It was the same mistake as the cannabis cake in Thailand, and the same symptoms came with it.

Breakfast came all the way back up, and after it the stomach acid.

Everything spun, and there was nothing to do but be thrown around by the sickness.

I must have kept throwing up for two or three hours; after a while it began to ease.

Even so, the dizziness stayed, and the headache with it.

The time I had to be out of the apartment was coming up, and I could not leave him with the trouble.

There was nothing to be done, so I had him call an auto-rickshaw and take me to the station.

Even a backpack with next to nothing in it was heavy on a body that unsteady, and I barely made it to the station.

The night train to Goa came in on time.

I found my berth fast, lay down at once, and fell asleep.

The vomiting had emptied me out; there was nothing else I could do.

I ate nothing on the train, and slept like the dead from evening until morning, which was how I got through it.
