# Ganden Monastery

As luck would have it, my stay lined up with a great Tibetan Buddhist festival held once every twelve years.

(That was what I was told at the time; when I looked it up later, it seems to happen every year. I'm writing it here the way it felt to me then.)

It takes place at Ganden Monastery, a place like the Potala Palace a few hours and a couple of bus changes out of Lhasa, where they hang an enormous cloth down the wall.

The cloth is called a thangka, painted with Tibetan Buddhist imagery.

What makes the event extraordinary is the size of it — tens of meters (over a hundred feet) on a side.

I got up early and set out for Ganden with my friends, changing buses along the way.

It was a big festival, and the buses were already packed.

With no seats left, we stood through a few hours of bad road and got shaken the whole way.

By the time we reached Ganden I was worn out, but the excitement of the festival lifted me right back up.

For an event that came only once in twelve years, the crowd was staggering.

People filled the ground around the monastery, and the hillside opposite, where you could look down on the whole place, was packed solid too.

Almost everyone there was a devout Tibetan family or a monk, and foreign tourists were a tiny fraction of the total.

We staked out a spot on the slope ourselves and settled in to wait for the thangka.

Foreigners were a rare sight for Tibetans who had come in from the countryside, and we were met with enormous smiles.

A family sitting near us waved us over to eat lunch with them.

The home cooking a local Tibetan mother had put her whole effort into for the big festival was so far beyond Tibetan food in a restaurant that there was no comparison, and I fell for Tibetan cooking on the spot.

# Groped by a Tibetan Monk

After we ate, while we waited for the thangka to come down, I tried to talk with the Tibetans, but my Chinese-speaking exchange-student friends had already gone back to Beijing, and we had no language in common.

We were getting by without words when two Tibetan monks pointed at my legs, laughed, and reached out to touch them.

My original plan had been to head south from Shanghai, so I only had clothes for warm places, and that day I was in knee-length shorts.

They pinched and stroked the hair on my bare inner thigh, looking puzzled.

For a second I had no idea what was happening.

Maybe my near-shaved head appeals to gay men — back when I worked at Tsutaya, customers who seemed that way would now and then hit on me.

So I braced myself, thinking a gay Tibetan monk was groping me, but it did not feel like that at all.

It was the middle of the day, the middle of a crowd at a religious event, families sitting all around.

Not a place where anyone could grope someone under cover of the crowd.

After a bit more of that, the monk pulled up his robe and showed me his own leg.

The Tibetans around us were laughing.

It finally got through to me: Tibetans, apparently, don't grow hair on their legs.

His leg was as smooth as a woman's after waxing.

Hairy inner thighs looked strange to them, and they had been touching me out of pure curiosity.

# The Thangka

After a while the crowd began to stir.

The famous thangka was finally coming down.

We were sitting several hundred meters (a thousand feet or so) from the monastery, and the monks looked like ants.

Just as I registered the ants swarming along the top of the building, the thangka dropped all at once.

Even from that distance the design was perfectly clear.

A Buddha at the center, with meditating saints painted around him.

I wanted a closer look, but the crush of people was too much and I gave up on getting near.

There must have been tens of thousands of them.

# The Way Back

Riding the bus again would have been rough, so we went back by shared taxi.

It cost a little more, but I had no interest in going through that bus twice in one day.

On the way, as we came up on a big road junction, one of the Japanese travelers with us announced he was getting out here.

There was nothing at the junction but road and open wasteland.

Cars would pass, since it was a festival day, but right then there was not another vehicle in sight.

He was going to hitchhike from there to another town.

The town was closed to foreigners, so getting there by public transport was apparently difficult.

It was not my problem, and still I could not stop worrying.

As far as I could see there was no shop, no house, not even a passing car.

This was country at 4,000 meters (13,000 feet), where freezing to death after dark would be no surprise.

But he paid no attention to any of that, shouldered his small backpack, and got out of the taxi.

We left him there and the taxi pulled away toward Lhasa.

The shape of him shrinking into the middle of the Himalayan wasteland looked completely self-sufficient, and impossibly cool.
