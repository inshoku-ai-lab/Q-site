## Magic Mushrooms

Through the rainy season in Pokhara, my days repeated themselves: smoke cannabis, and read manga while eating Japanese food.

Then one day Shiva, the small-time crook I'd met, came over to talk.

This time it wasn't cannabis — he had magic mushrooms, he said.

"Heh heh heh, got something nice in, boss." That sort of pitch.

Magic mushrooms growing wild here is one of the reasons travelers started gathering in Pokhara in the first place.

Ever since the hippie-traveler era of the sixties, so the story goes, Western travelers have come to Pokhara to eat mushrooms and play around on the lake.

When the rainy season comes, the mushrooms apparently push up out of cow dung.

The locals go around collecting them and pass them on to travelers.

## The Session

Shiva the crook offered to put on a session for me — take the mushrooms out in the countryside, out in nature.

By then I was in no condition to resist curiosity about anything at all, so, shady as Shiva was, I agreed.

The two of us rode the motorcycle into the forest, and I ate the mushroom omelet he had ready.

The taste was neither good nor bad.

Not that it tasted bad, exactly, but not something you would go out of your way to eat.

Which is precisely why they mix them into a good omelet.

About thirty minutes in, the effects began to come on, little by little.

A genuine psychedelic experience had finally begun.

The first time, on Koh Phangan, I ate too much cannabis cake and went down hard; the second, with the mushrooms I grew at home, I fell asleep; the third, in Kathmandu, I was sold fake LSD.

Fourth time lucky — this time I could feel the mushrooms for certain.

A slight queasiness around my stomach, my eyes going out of focus.

A restless feeling welling up in my chest, and my heart starting to race.

About an hour in, the effects came on clearly.

A high ringing started up in my ears, my senses turned abnormally sharp, and I felt strange.

Honestly, I had no idea what was supposed to be interesting about the sensation, but just feeling something I had never felt before made me happy.

## Communication

I tried to talk with Shiva, who had brought me all the way out here, but our few words of broken English did not get us far.

With my head fogged by the mushrooms, talking was harder still.

Understanding each other only vaguely, we got onto Japan, and from there to him asking to see Japanese money.

I kept my valuables on me at all times, so I had a ten-thousand-yen note right there — about eighty dollars.

I handed the note over and he looked it over with interest.

The talk moved on to something else. He was still gripping the note.

When I told him to give it back, he hid it and slipped it into his pocket.

What? What are you doing?

That's not right — but I had no idea how to get that across.

I tried to take it back by force, and he dodged and deflected every time.

In the end it turned into: aren't you just too far gone on the mushrooms to know what's going on? (That's what I guessed he was saying.)

In the state I was in I couldn't go to the police, and being left out here in the mountains could turn into a matter of life and death.

I wanted to press him hard, but I couldn't put it into words.

It brought back being bullied in my school years.

And in the middle of this worst possible situation, the mushroom high kept getting deeper.

Less and less of it made sense, and it was nothing like fun, nothing like feeling good.

I wanted to deck him and take it back, but being raised a Jehovah's Witness had wired me so that hitting a person was, reflexively, not something I could do.

My pacifism had backfired.

In the end, pathetically, I did nothing at all, and he drove me home on the back of his motorcycle.
