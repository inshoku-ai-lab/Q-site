# Quentin Tarantino

My parents divorced when I was around three, and my brother and I were raised apart, but because of that there was no strange family drama or entanglement between us — he was more like a friend I happened to share blood with.

Around the time I started high school, I began seeing a lot of him.

The reason was that he worked part-time at a video rental shop and could lend me videos for free.

He played in a band and was a subculture obsessive, and it apparently made him happy that I was starting to take an interest in film and music.

Once a month the local movie theater had a day when admission was a thousand yen (about nine dollars), and every time it came around he took me to a film.

Me, my brother, and my brother's wife — a date for three.

We saw all sorts of films, but my brother was a huge fan of Quentin Tarantino, and he took me to see every one of Tarantino's films without fail.

When we went to see the first one, *Reservoir Dogs*, it just struck me as an ordinarily enjoyable film, but the second one, *Pulp Fiction*, came as an enormous shock.

![Two men in black suits with blood-spattered white shirts standing side by side beside a sunlit window](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog15-1.jpg)

It ran on a completely different sensibility from the Hollywood action films I had mainly been watching until then, and the way it was shot, the way the story unfolded, the way the music was used — every part of it blew my mind.

That shock was tremendous, and I fell in love on the spot.

My brother introduced me to related films, and I watched all of them.

In the course of that I naturally turned into a film obsessive, graduated from my old taste for Hollywood action movies, and came to like films with a bit more substance in them.

Before I knew it, I had started thinking that in the future I wanted a job connected to film.

That one film, *Pulp Fiction*, changed the course of my life.

In truth it was not the film that changed me — the film was the trigger for someone who had already reached the point of changing — but its influence was large.

# My Seventeenth Birthday

Over summer vacation in my second year of high school, I turned seventeen.

Going from sixteen to seventeen is a change of only one digit, but inside me that one carried something like the weight of ten.

Up to sixteen I was, to my mind, still a child, but seventeen was to me the entrance to adulthood, close to twenty.

Until then I had never once thought about the future.

It was less that I had no interest than that I had too little hope for the future, and had, it seems, been unconsciously avoiding thinking about what was ahead.

But as my age went up by one, the entrance to adulthood came into view, and the words "the future" crept into my subconscious.

Having time on my hands over summer vacation, I sank quietly into thought.

When I thought about the future, nothing rose up but a pitch-black hellscape, and it made my already brooding mood heavier still.

A life that had been suppressed without pause to begin with now sank deeper still, as self-consciousness, a growing ego, and the ability to think about the future came into bloom.

It was not falling from a height, but the bottom dropping out and collapsing from under something that had been crawling along the bottom.

I arrived at the very bottom of the bottom of life.

Dark, dark daily existence.

And then the realization that, if things stayed as they were, this dark daily existence would go on for the rest of my life.

Arriving at that bottom of the bottom where there was no longer a single thing left to lose, I managed, at long last, to face reality.

# Light Breaks into the Darkness

My mother had always declared openly that if I ever quit Jehovah's Witnesses, she would throw me out of the house.

If I were thrown out of the house, I would have no way to live, so I reluctantly did what my mother said.

Then an option came to me — living without depending on my mother.

Whether my birthday came first or seeing *Pulp Fiction* came first, I do not remember, but I think it was around the same time.

![A man in a black suit beside a woman with a black bob haircut holding a lit cigarette, large lettering on the wall behind](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog15-2.jpg)

The film is driven mainly by a story about gangsters, and through my admiration for that gangster kind of cool, it dawned on me that living by committing crimes was an available option.

Realizing the possibility of living by committing crimes must have knocked the restraints of common sense off my thinking.

I began to consider living as a beggar as well.

In the worst case, a person can go on living — even if it means committing crimes, even if it means becoming a beggar.

With that resolve, I could be free of Jehovah's Witnesses then and there.

That realization was hope for my life, and light.

Having seen nothing in the pitch darkness, having done nothing but writhe and suffer, I began to move toward the light that had broken into that darkness.
