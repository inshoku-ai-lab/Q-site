## The New Place

After about two weeks of N putting us up, we moved into the new place.

Not that a backpacker's move amounts to much — you stuff your things into your backpack and carry them over.

Thankfully, the place we moved into came furnished with everything we needed.

Dishes, pots, beds, bedding — every last thing was already there.

For a traveler who had just moved in, nothing could have been more welcome.

At the new house, father and daughter were both away at school during the day, so on weekdays we effectively had the place to ourselves.

By the time they came home in the evening, I was leaving for work.

And by the time I got back from work they were asleep, so we hardly ever crossed paths with our landlord's family.

I had been looking forward to spending time with the Stevie Wonder family, and I had thought it might help my English along, so that was a disappointment, but the days were easy and comfortable.

In my free time I wandered the neighborhood and learned where to buy cheap food and where to find secondhand clothes and bicycles.

Little by little, I was settling into life in England.

## A Small Incident

Around the time I was starting to settle in, there was a small incident involving money.

I-chan had asked me to go into town and take money out of the bank account she used for traveling abroad.

I stepped into the little room where the ATM was, put the card in, and drew out the cash.

What I took out was the last of the money in the account, leaving only about 5,000 yen — forty dollars or so — behind.

I had the money in hand and was reaching for the card when someone tapped me on the shoulder from behind.

I turned around to find a man pointing at the ground and saying something.

My English was almost nonexistent, so I had no idea what he was telling me, but there at the end of his finger lay a twenty-pound note.

It was definitely not mine, but the man kept pointing at the note and would not stop talking at me.

I have nothing against picking up money, so I did as I was told, picked up the twenty pounds, and put it in my pocket.

That seemed to put him at ease, and he walked out of the little ATM room.

When I glanced back at the machine, the cash card I was supposed to have taken out was gone.

Maybe I had put it away myself already, I thought, and checked my valuables pouch; it was not in there either.

While I was busy picking up the twenty pounds, the man had pulled the card out of the machine and walked off with it!

By the time I realized I had been robbed, he had long since run off.

I thought about calling the card company, but the card with the contact number printed on it was exactly what had just been taken.

And with the English I had, calling a British ATM company was out of the question.

With no other option, I let it stay stolen.

The saving grace, I suppose, was that for the 5,000 yen stolen from me I had come away with twenty pounds, close to thirty dollars.

In the end the damage came to just 1,500 yen.

What stung was my own uselessness — that in a situation like that, I could not do a single thing.

On top of that, when I got home, I-chan laid into me.

There was nothing I could say in my defense, and nothing to do but feel terrible.
