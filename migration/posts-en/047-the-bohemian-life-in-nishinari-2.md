# A Shift in Values

After Thailand, and especially after all that unhurried time on the southern islands, I began to want a slightly slower life.

The travelers I met on Koh Phangan were loose and unhurried about everything.

I wanted to drift along the way they did, and hippie thinking started seeping into the punk thinking I had been running on.

The Tsutaya job let me borrow all the videos and CDs I wanted, so I meant to keep it, but the work itself was relentless and I wanted to cut back my days.

Five days a week down to two or three, with the rest replaced by an easier job — that was the ideal.

Was there work out there that was easy and free and asked nothing of me? Looking for that entirely self-serving arrangement, I went through the job listings and asked my older brother about it.

I kept at it for a while, and then, miraculously, the ideal job turned up.

# The Video Store

A video rental store where a former bandmate of my brother's worked was hiring.

Another video store, I thought. Apparently that was the fate assigned to me.

As it happened, the place stood just three hundred meters (a thousand feet) from the biggest Tsutaya in western Japan, where I already worked.

The floor was maybe ten tatami mats, sixteen square meters (175 square feet), a cramped little video store of the old downtown kind.

Business had been good once, but the big Tsutaya had opened and taken the customers, and now the place was close to going under.

From what I heard, the monthly take went almost entirely to keeping the store open and paying the part-timers.

The owner had a number of different shops, and the only reason he kept this one alive, they said, was to keep the part-timers fed.

An owner who looked after his part-timers. Nothing to feel but grateful.

The store was called Forum — a meeting room, a place for public debate — and true to the name, it did its part: the part-timers gathered there and made a culture of their own.

# Hired

G, my brother's old bandmate, put in a word with the part-timer who acted as stand-in manager, and I was taken on at Forum.

One condition: my job at Tsutaya stayed a secret, so as not to upset the owner.

And of course, my job at Forum was a secret from my bosses at Tsutaya.

And so began a nonsensical double-agent life: at one job, the biggest video rental store in western Japan; at the other, the video store that store was putting out of business.

The setup had a spy's crookedness to it, but for my own life it was exactly the arrangement I had wanted.

# Working at Forum

The work at Forum was easy beyond anything I had imagined.

At Tsutaya, once you got put on register, the busy stretches meant hours on your feet ringing people up, unable to slip out even to the bathroom until your relief arrived — a brutal kind of busy.

At Forum, a customer came in maybe once every thirty minutes, if that.

Mostly you sat in a chair listening to whatever music you liked, reading whatever books you liked, watching whatever movies you liked, and when somebody did come in, you served them — which was, precisely, the job I had been imagining.

There was no manager, just part-timers running the place together as equals, and nobody to defer to.

The stand-in manager looked after the store as a kind of volunteer, since nobody else would take responsibility for it.

The rest of us simply leaned on his goodwill.

The owner came by about once a month, said nothing in particular, said hello, and that was that.

Even so, the freedom and the terms for part-timers were excellent.

The hourly wage was only about eighty percent of what Tsutaya paid, but the workload was less than a tenth, so there was nothing to complain about.

The bento shop a minute's walk away sold us boxed lunches at half price on the days we were working.

The freedom was superb: no uniform, and if what you wore fell somewhat outside what the world considered normal, nobody cared.

The customers, in turn, were the sort of people naturally drawn to exactly that slack atmosphere.

Local old men and old women who couldn't stand the young-crowd energy of the big stores, local yakuza, shy young guys too embarrassed to rent adult videos under bright lights — our regulars were the people who felt out of place at a big chain like Tsutaya.

A small store had a demand all its own.
