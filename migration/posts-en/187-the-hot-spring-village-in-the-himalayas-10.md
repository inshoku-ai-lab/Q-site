## Friends

After I arrived in Vashisht, several friends from Goa started showing up.

It wasn't as if we'd promised to meet again in Manali.

Goa had simply been too much fun, and with my closest friends up in Manali having a good time, being the only one not there was more than I could stand.

That's how good Goa had been, and in each of our lives those friendships had become something none of us could do without.

## Travelers and Society

Everyone had gone back to Japan after Goa, taken some job or other, scraped together money as hard as they could, and come back to India.

The ones who already had savings, or who had been through the cycle of traveling and going home to Japan to work many times over, seemed to take it in stride, but people like me — who had enjoyed India to the last drop on a first trip and then gone home to Japan to save money with everything they had — had all, without exception, been through a deep kind of suffering.

Hardly any company wants to hire someone who comes back from a long trip, works for a short stretch, and then heads overseas again.

The work you could get was simple physical labor that only needed a body, or a job in the nightlife business.

The kind of job that interests you, that's actually fun, that makes you want to do it — a traveler who's going to quit in a few months almost never lands one.

And when someone who has tasted freedom and let their own character loose tries to fit back into society, all it produces is friction — painful for the one coming in and painful for the ones taking them in.

## The Pattern of Suffering

I found out a few years later that this is something nearly every traveler goes through: almost everyone who takes a first long trip abroad, burns through their savings, goes home, and starts working so they can travel again feels a gap open between themselves and the family and friends and society they'd always known, and feels isolated, and feels the particular pain of not being understood.

Plenty of them set out abroad again just to escape that pain.

The interesting part is that it seems to have almost nothing to do with where you traveled or where you're from, and everything to do with how much you enjoyed being away.

And the stronger the enjoyment was, and the further the experience was from anything you'd known before, the more it seems to hurt when you go back to your own society.

The country doesn't have to be a foreign one, either — it holds just as well for someone from Tokyo who had a wonderful time in Hokkaido, or someone from Osaka who had a wonderful time in Okinawa.

You leave the reality you've known, live an ideal life inside a new one, let out the self that had been held down all along, and then you go back to the old reality.

But once you've had that new experience, there's no going back to who you were.
