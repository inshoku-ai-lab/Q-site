# Moving On

We decided to go to Ilam, a "tea town" in eastern Nepal.

Ilam sits on the other side of the border from Darjeeling, and the tea there is supposed to be good.

The original plan was to drop straight south from Pokhara to Goa, my final destination, but I wasn't ready yet to head into India on my own.

India is a special place for travelers: most of them want to go at least once, and at the same time it's a hard country to take that first step into.

I decided to get a little more used to the feel of things in Nepal, and ease into India slowly.

From Pokhara to Kathmandu, and from Kathmandu to Ilam.

It's a poor showing for someone writing a travelogue, but I have almost no memory of the town of Ilam.

What's left is that the mountain road was rough, the tea was good, and I-san and G were all over each other — about that much.

Coming to this town was where I said goodbye to the friends I had traveled with since Tibet.

There must have been a moving farewell, but none of it stayed with me.

People are coldhearted.

# The Road to Darjeeling

I crossed Nepal's eastern border into India.

I can't say I felt ready, but my resolve was solid.

Darjeeling is up in the mountains, so I spent a night in a town at the foot of them before going up.

The next morning I got into a shared taxi bound for Darjeeling, and the endless hairpin curves gave me a brutal case of carsickness.

There can't be many travelers who get as carsick as I do, but I suppose that's fate too.

The carsickness that day was bad, but I didn't throw up.

Instead, the nausea carried me into a deep meditative state.

Strangely, by tasting that carsick misery all the way to the bottom, I came to feel for the suffering my mother had been through in her life.

What carsickness has to do with my mother's suffering, I can't say, but I understood that she had suffered in her life.

And that was why she'd had no choice but to run to something as extreme as Jehovah's Witnesses.

Extreme suffering, I learned, needs an extreme faith to heal it.

Understanding that, I could forgive my mother completely.

I had resented her for raising me as a child of Jehovah's Witnesses.

How many times as a kid did I wish for an ordinary life.

But understanding what she had suffered in her life let me accept all of it.

It isn't something I can explain easily in words, but that experience was the dividing line: I put down my anger at my mother and stood on my own as a grown adult.

# The Town of Darjeeling

Starting my first step into India from Darjeeling was, I think, the right call.

These are mountain people, and the temperament matches — mild, settled.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog85-2.jpg)

None of the this-and-that I'd pictured as uniquely Indian before coming ever happened, and I entered India as if Nepal had simply continued.

In this town I met a Japanese traveler, a man who had an experience there that could only have happened in India, and I want to pass it on.

Some readers may feel the story makes fun of Indians, and others may wonder what the problem is supposed to be, but I'd like you to take it as one of those things that happen in India and enjoy it without thinking too hard.

He had come from Nepal to India, and the poor sanitation there wrecked his stomach.

He was plagued by amoebic stomach pains, and one day, in the middle of walking through town, he shit himself without warning.

The pants could have been washed and used again, but he was too sick to have the strength for laundry, so he threw them in the trash at his guesthouse.

A few days later he saw an older guy from the neighborhood wearing the colorful pants he had thrown out, washed and on his legs.

The contrast between the old guy's drab clothes and those loud pants was so outlandish that it amounted to a real taste of India's surreal humor — and that's the story...
