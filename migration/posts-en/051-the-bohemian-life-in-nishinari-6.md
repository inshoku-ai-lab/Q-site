# Ideology

The idea of the music unit with my brother kept evolving, and it settled into a form: experimental musicians working in tape collage, with an anti-copyright ideology behind everything they made.

We didn't really hold any serious ideology, but we enjoyed setting ourselves up that way.

In 1997, copyright's grip on the media industry was at its peak — the rights to all music, all film, all books were strictly managed, and you couldn't get near any of it without paying a lot of money.

The internet was still undeveloped, and it was eight years before YouTube existed, so there was next to no way to see anything on video for free.

The only exceptions were libraries and video rental shops, which is why I worked part-time at a video store and spent my days at the library.

Fed up with a world where the copyright societies controlled everything and you couldn't get at art freely, we wanted to make people see how ridiculous copyright law was.

We believed that by making songs out of nothing but copyrighted music, we could draw out the essence of music itself — whatever lies beyond the human tangle of who made it and whose profit it is.

What are the limits of music?

Where is the line between this is music and this is not music?

All kinds of music influenced us, but nothing more than the thinking of the experimental composer John Cage.

His most famous piece is called 4′33″, and it is a recording of four minutes and thirty-three seconds of silence.

(Video) [https://youtu.be/JTEFKFiXSx4](https://youtu.be/JTEFKFiXSx4)

By presenting pieces of total silence like that, he had succeeded in destroying the concept of music, and for us, under his influence, every sound and every silence was music.

# Style

Once our thinking was free of the concept of music, our own work got freer too.

To push at the limits of music, we had fun making things that broke existing values apart — dance music focused on not letting anyone dance, or music built out of completely unmusical sounds like the back-and-forth on TV variety shows.

As stupid and pointless as it could be, and still with some kind of passion hidden inside it. That was the incomprehensible thing we were aiming for.

Even with escape from existing values as our goal, we did have our own tastes, and while we took in every style there was, we built our music on funk and electro, both of them Black music.

The combination seemed like it would be interesting — the experimental ground of tape collage against the groove of funk and electro, music rooted in the body.

An anti-copyright experimental funk-electro-tape-collage unit. That was exactly what we were.
