# F's Life as a Beggar

S, the homeless Iranian R had been looking after, was no beggar — home-free, nomad, those were the words that suited him, a traveler carried a little further along the same line.

But F was a different picture: a long-haul traveler who had held the line, just barely, without going over to the dark side, an enlightened man with no ambition to speak of.

There is a demon in Goa named Freedom, and it hands every traveler who arrives a serious test.

The ones who taste that much freedom and learn to handle it come away with possibilities in themselves they never knew were there.

The ones who can't handle it and get taken down by it often end up in ruin.

In F's case, the freedom element and the can't-be-bothered element lined up neatly, for better and for worse.

Not so much a home-free man living without a house, he carried a heavy dose of the beggar in him: slack, caring about nothing at all.

# F's Philosophy of Life

His motto was spent entirely on a single question: how to get a lot while taking it easy.

What I want you to notice is that this is a world away from the greedy way of living, where you focus on getting a lot and spare no effort doing it.

His first objective was how to take it easy.

And on top of that, how to get a lot.

For a little gain he wouldn't make the slightest effort; only for a large gain would he make the very smallest one.

He had pushed that self-centeredness through to the end, so cleanly it was almost admirable, and he had no doubts about it.

At the same time he carried a Japanese sort of politeness, so there was nothing tiresome or irritating about him.

F was in fact highly experienced and highly capable — he could do most things and he knew a great deal, and if he had gotten serious he could easily have earned real money and lived at his leisure in Goa — but he had been infected by the can't-be-bothered philosophy, and he took no action of any kind.

He seemed to be flying an absolute flag of revolt against the social value that praises effort.

For whatever time remained until death, just living out the days as they came.

He was a homeless man in the mold of a komusō, one of those wandering monks who walk the country with a woven basket over the head.
