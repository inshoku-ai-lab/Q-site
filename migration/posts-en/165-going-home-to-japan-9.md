## Scaffolding

The scaffolding job went better than I had expected.

Having grown used to the dirt and earthiness of India, I found the coarseness and roughness particular to manual laborers comfortable instead, and the friendliness of my coworkers — they were yankees, Japan's delinquent kids — helped me settle back into Japan.

It was a strange thing, the kid who had been bullied all through school now getting on well with yankees, but somehow I took to it.

The pay was ten thousand yen a day — about eighty dollars — and looking back, that seems a little cheap for putting your life on the line, but I had never made ten thousand yen in a single day before, and I was more than happy with it.

Measured by my Indian sense of money, three days of work bought a whole month of living for the fun of it.

Every week on the job, I told myself, had earned me two months of vacation, and I was very pleased with myself about it.

## The Boss

The man who had interviewed me turned out to be the president of the company, and he was only twenty-seven.

His father had been a scaffolder to the bone, and he had gone into the trade himself straight out of junior high, he said.

He had lived for this trade and nothing else, and his confidence in his own work was absolute, so he wore no helmet and carried no insurance on himself at all.

Twelve years of working every single day, and not one accident, not one injury.

Working alongside him, I could catch a glimpse of where that confidence came from.

His mind was always clear; he understood exactly who should be doing what and how, and the instructions he gave were precise and perfect.

His pride in the trade ran deep, and it surprised me that a value system like that existed at all.

The point of it, apparently, is this: with nothing but your own body — reflexes, stamina, skill, experience, a feel for it — you take on the most dangerous work there is, and you are paid well for it.

The coolest of them all is said to be tekkotsu-tobi, steel erecting: taking the huge steel beams handed over from a crane truck and building the skeleton of a building out of them.

That work carries real danger, of course, and plenty of men are badly hurt and lose their lives.

The appeal is probably in exactly that risk.

And the pursuit of that aesthetic, I think, is what shows up in going without a helmet and without insurance.

## A Talking-To

A few days into the job, I got a talking-to.

I had been trusting the boss's perfect instructions and working while I waited for the next one, and he said that wasn't enough.

Make mistakes if you have to, but move on your own.

That one line was a shock to me.

Every part-time job I had ever had was robot work, carrying out the tasks I was handed, selling my hours off in slices and turning them into money, and it had never once occurred to me that my own judgment, my own initiative, might be something anyone valued.

If the boss said so, then fine — I took the plunge, started making my own calls, and went at the work without waiting to be told.

The approach paid off, and I learned to use my own head and move.

Once I was actually thinking and acting for myself, the work flowed more smoothly, and a sense that it was worth doing began to rise up in me.

Until then, work had meant nothing to me but repeating what I was told, flat and unfeeling; for the first time I could put my own will into a job, and it became far more interesting.
