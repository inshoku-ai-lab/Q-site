# Starting Our Own Label

Working at the biggest Tsutaya in western Japan turned out to be pretty stimulating.

A lot of the people there were doing interesting work in the arts, and I took all kinds of inspiration from them.

Each of them wanted to get something of their own moving, and I think a quiet heat burned in all of them.

The musicians on the late-night shift wanted to give their own ideas a shape too, and they were looking into starting an indie label.

Then an invitation came my way: did I want in on starting it?

It came from A-kun, the techno musician I had gone to Thailand with.

That same heat — the urge to get something moving — was in me as well, and starting a label of our own struck me as a genuinely interesting thing to do, so I said yes on the spot.

# The CD Recorder

CD recorders were only just appearing back then, and for a musician, being able to make a CD on your own computer, fairly easily, landed like a shock.

Until then, putting your music out meant sending your recordings to a label that was already running and getting them to approve of you.

Naturally, whatever you wanted to do got limited by whoever was on the other end, and if they judged it wouldn't sell, it never became a CD.

With an indie label, that part was up to us — which artist, which songs got made into a CD, we could choose freely.

In exchange, if nothing sold, we carried the financial risk ourselves.

To cut that risk, the more investors the better.

That was how the invitation reached me.

A CD recorder cost about ninety thousand yen back then — something like seven hundred dollars.

Access to that expensive machine worked out to thirty thousand yen a head, about two hundred and forty dollars.

# Running the Label

When it came time to start the label, we decided it needed a name and everyone threw out ideas, and the one I put forward,

FinderPop

was the one we adopted.

The name came from an extension for the Mac that I liked using.

An extension that popped up on the Finder screen — hence FinderPop.

For our label, the sense of it was closer to

"the ones who go and find their own kind of pop music."

Something along those lines.

Running it fell to the two-man band who had come up with the idea, and it drifted into a setup where they made the decisions in consultation with A-kun.

I had wanted to be deeply involved in running the label too, but I was kept in the position of investor and artist on the roster.

The first I heard of it, we were starting the thing together, and having things decided without me left me unhappy.

If I was unhappy, I should have said so, or talked it out, but the other label members had years on me — in age, at the job, and as musicians — and I couldn't bring myself to speak up.

# The CD Debut

By their decision, my album became the first release.

Why me first, I don't know, though part of it was probably that my songs were already finished.

I had been writing on ReBirth, a software synthesizer that ran on the computer and put a synth and a drum machine together.

Click "Export" in the menu and the track was done.

The others had to record with a band, or record analog synths, so it took them much longer.

My album was techno: a four-on-the-floor kick drum at the base, with the squelchy synth sound of a TB-303 riding on top.

As someone who kept writing music for years afterward, I find those tracks of mine deeply childish and rudimentary, but at the time I was thoroughly pleased with myself.
