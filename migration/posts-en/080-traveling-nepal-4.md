# Pokhara

A few days later my friends said they were heading to Pokhara after Kathmandu, so I went along with them.

Nepal was easy to be in and nothing about it worried me, but traveling alone was dull.

Pokhara is known for Phewa Lake, set down in a gap in the Himalayas, and it is a resort town where foreign travelers come to settle in along the water and take their time.

Plenty of well-off tourists come through, but it is popular with backpackers too.

Western backpackers stay along the northern shore, the stretch called Lakeside, while the southern shore, called Damside, is where the Japanese backpackers end up, or so I heard.

Six hours to Pokhara, rocking along a mountain road on a bus.

I had half expected it, and sure enough, motion sickness got me.

The road threw one curve after another, left then right without letup, and about a third of the bus sat hunched forward with a miserable look on their faces.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog80-1.jpg)

Once we reached Pokhara, we found a place to stay in Damside.

Four of us shared one room in a startlingly nice resort-style hotel, which brought the price down to almost nothing.

# The Japanese Restaurant

One reason so many Japanese wound up in Damside was a Japanese restaurant called Anil Momo.

It was no upscale, authentic Japanese place — a shack thrown together out of corrugated tin — but it had a reputation for being cheap and good.

It began as a small local restaurant, and the whole thing got started when a Japanese traveler taught them how to cook Japanese food.

Apart from the soy sauce and the miso, nothing about the seasoning was particularly Japanese, but the mere fact that you could eat something that tasted of soy sauce in Nepal was enough to pull Japanese travelers in.

Soy sauce is famous worldwide now, and you can pick up a bottle of Kikkoman in cities anywhere, but back then it was a precious thing and not easy to come by.

Anil Momo served that rare soy sauce cooking at the price of an ordinary Nepali restaurant, which made it popular with long-term Japanese travelers.

It turned into a hangout for Japanese travelers on its own, something like a loosely connected Japanese community.

Some of them went home to Japan and came back again, and they donated Japanese books, which made the place more of a gathering spot than ever.

One person taught them a wider range of dishes, another painted the sign or wrote out the menu, and between them everyone built the place up.

# The Regulars

Anil Momo was about a three-minute walk from our hotel, so we went every single day.

The taste of soy sauce, the first in the month and a half since leaving Japan, hit me hard, and confirmed just how Japanese I was.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog80-2.jpg)

It was not only the taste of home that drew me in; the Japanese manga sitting around the place kept me from leaving too.

It was the rainy season, and some days the rain came down from morning to night.

Cheap food from home on a day like that, with all the manga I could read, was exactly what I would have wished for.

What are you doing all the way out in Nepal, of all places?

Some people would ask that, I imagine.

But that is the short-term traveler's way of thinking, and for a long-term traveler with unlimited time, what matters is how to enjoy yourself without spending money.

Ticking off more sights held less appeal for me than having something real happen and feeling what it is to be a traveler.

We were only a group of Japanese hanging around together reading manga, but by my own values it was a very worthwhile way to spend the time.
