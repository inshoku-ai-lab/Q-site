## Surprises at Gusto

Gusto had other surprises for me, too.

One day I happened to catch sight of a sheet the manager kept on the cost of each item on the menu.

Every day the manager went over those costs line by line, trying to push sales up.

When I sneaked a look at it, the costs were so low they floored me.

A steak that sold for over 1,000 yen — about eight dollars — cost around 230 yen to make, under two dollars.

Beef steak, the very picture of luxury food, for a mere 230 yen.

Of course, the part-timers' wages and the store's rent got piled on top of that, so the real figure must have come out higher in the end, but the sheer gap between the cost and the price was a shock.

What surprised me even more was that the chicken steak, which sold for about 900 yen — around seven dollars — cost more to make than the beef steak selling for over 1,000.

Prices weren't high because costs were high.

In a capitalist world, that may be just how a business is run, but I had never given any of it a thought, and those costs caught me completely off guard.

## The Manager

The restaurant, near the center of Osaka, had a jaded feel to it in some ways.

Nobody took the work very seriously; everyone seemed focused on how to take it easy and let the hours slide by any old way.

The manager was married, but was apparently having an affair, slipping out between tasks for one secret rendezvous after another.

Among the part-timers it had become an open secret, and out of earshot they whispered about what the manager was up to.

When I'd worked at the pizza place, I fit in with the people around me and got along fine, but here I was the odd one out.

I didn't make a single friend, and I never talked openly about myself or my travels.

I was a strange, silent young guy with long hair and a tan burned nearly black — in early spring.

Remembering my days in the Moroccan desert and missing the honesty and sincerity of the Muslim people, I got through my shifts one day at a time.
