# Sandals

Right after my brother and his friend arrived, K — a Japanese woman living out at Calangute Beach who was soon to marry a local man — invited all of us over for a meal.

A kind offer: she wanted to treat the two of them, who had come all the way from Japan, to real local Goan food.

We all piled into a taxi and rode out to their house.

We were fed excellent Goan food, talked about all sorts of things, and passed a fine evening.

Then, just as we were saying we ought to head back to Anjuna where we lived, we noticed that my brother's sandals were gone.

They were an unusual design, bought specially for this trip by a sneaker obsessive with a collection of more than a hundred pairs.

Nothing you would ever see in India.

By Indian logic the fault lay in wearing sandals that strange in the first place, and in leaving them out where people could see them, but for us, who wanted the two of them to have a good time, it felt pretty lousy.

The house sat in a stand of trees at the back of a lane nobody walked down, which left us suspecting people close to us, and that was the sad part.

Someone had probably taken them to sell, and there was no one to complain to, so that day he went home in a borrowed pair of K's sandals.

# Stealing

Small thefts like this happen constantly in India, and every one of them drags the mood down.

The sense of theft in India is very different from the Japanese one — less that the person who steals is in the wrong, more that the person who gets robbed is.

They are people of enormous goodwill, and at the same time their feeling for wrongdoing is nothing like Japan's.

It was a shame this happened at the very start of their ten days in India, but my brother had come with a frightening picture of the country in his head, braced for having all his luggage stolen, so he didn't seem all that troubled.

We swam in the sea, shopped the markets, ate at good restaurants, and made the most of our days in Goa.

At the markets I handled the haggling in English and helped them buy as cheap as they could.

A small thing, but it felt good to be of use to a brother who had done nothing but look after me.

The best part of running around with him was that he paid for everything.

He held firmly to the role of older brother toward me, seven years younger, and he always covered the whole bill.

When he was short of money he would say he was short of money, and buy me something cheap.

He never once told me to go halves. No discussion — he just paid.

It's a ridiculous thing to admit, but back then I was the youngest wherever I went, and I took being treated as my due.

The way a child thinks on entering the world of adults.

Y complained about it more than once: "You use a child's privileges and then demand the same rights as an adult."

But that streak of stand-up generosity in my brother worked on me without my noticing, and years later, paying for other people came naturally.

Over their ten days the two of them squeezed out every drop of it.

I'd never imagined myself working as a tour guide in India, not being able to speak English, but next to people with no experience of the country at all, it was obvious how much they could lean on me, and that became a real source of confidence.

On the day they flew home, I saw the two of them off at the airport.

Apart from the stolen sandals there had been no losses, no accidents, no injuries, and it made me happy to send them back to Japan safe.

It seems the inspiration to write a letter and invite them to India hadn't been a mistake.
