## Lowland Sickness

On top of the misery of the carsickness, there was the oxygen problem.

When people who live in the lowlands suddenly go up to an altitude of 2,800 meters (9,200 feet), they get altitude sickness.

The air is thin, so breathing is hard, and less oxygen reaches the brain.

That brings on headaches and nausea, and in bad cases, things like seizures.

Those symptoms come from moving from low ground to high ground, but going the other way, from high ground down to low, also does something strange to the body.

There's no actual illness called "lowland sickness," but that's what the hut staff call it among themselves.

The symptoms come from suddenly finding yourself with too much oxygen: you're overcome by an unnatural drowsiness, your head goes foggy, you get heartburn, that sort of thing.

And all of it makes motion sickness worse.

## Motion Sickness

Since I'd last gone up the mountain, I'd spent about four and a half months straight on top of a 2,800-meter peak.

Day after day of a very simple, frugal life.

No being jostled around in a car, no breathing in exhaust fumes.

Going straight from that to driving the mountain roads down in the lowlands and then through town, I was hit by violent motion sickness.

Once every few years, I get a truly violent bout of motion sickness, and this was one of those times.

Again and again I had them stop the car along the way, and I threw up everything in my stomach, and once it was empty, I kept throwing up yellow fluid.

The others in the car had to wait on me, but there was nothing I could do about it.

I suffered through every last bit of it and finally made it to the dorm in town.

Following the most incredible experience in the mountains with a brutally miserable bout of motion sickness seemed like a kind of balance, but it also seemed to mean something.
