# The Bus to Tibet

We got ourselves ready and boarded the bus for Tibet.

We had spent about three days in Golmud, so our bodies should have been used to the high altitude by now.

Just to be safe, we had picked up some Chinese herbal medicine for altitude sickness.

This time we were not in soft seat or hard sleeper but in soft sleeper.

A sleeper car with soft beds, in other words.

Narrow, but you could stretch your legs out and sleep.

The bus was due to reach Lhasa, the Tibetan capital, in three days and two nights.

It rolled steadily on through dry wasteland.

We were starting at 2,800 meters (9,200 feet) and heading for 5,300 (17,400 feet).

# Manners

Cultural differences turn up in all kinds of places, and the ideas about cleanliness gave me another dose of culture shock.

The bus was a sleeper, with an aisle down the middle, and since everyone's luggage was packed into that aisle, you walked half on top of the bags to reach your bed.

That alone was a shock, but the real surprise came once the bus pulled out.

As soon as we were moving, everyone opened the bags of peanuts they had brought and started eating.

And every one of them threw the empty shells onto the luggage in the aisle.

True, the shells never went in anyone's mouth, so there was no saliva on them and probably no bacteria either.

Still, throwing what I registered as garbage on top of other people's bags was a shock.

Not unhygienic, but it felt dirty.

In Chinese culture, though, this was apparently ordinary — everyone did it and nobody minded.

Maybe the feeling is like setting your bag down on hay?

Dried peanut shells instead of dried grass, and the bag underneath instead of on top.

The only one bothered by it seemed to be me, the one who was not used to China, while everyone else smiled and enjoyed the ride.

For now I decided to go by the old proverb and do as the locals do.

# Motion Sickness

I get motion sick easily and long mountain roads are the worst thing for me, but since this bus was a sleeper, I never got badly carsick.

As we climbed, breathing got harder and a headache slowly set in.

Every few hours there was a rest stop, and talking with my friends, it turned out we all had the same symptoms.

We talked it over and decided to take the altitude medicine before the symptoms got bad.

Once in Peru I had taken altitude medicine together with cold medicine and paid for it badly, so this time I swallowed it nervously.

It did not make the symptoms any lighter, but it may have kept them from getting much worse.

Near the top of the pass, somewhere around 5,000 meters (16,400 feet), the headache turned sharper.

Breathing got harder too, and a little motion sickness came in on top of it.

# Tanggula Pass

After a long stretch of that misery, we finally reached Tanggula Pass, the seventh-highest pass in the world, boasting an elevation of 5,231 meters (17,162 feet).

You could feel in your body exactly how high up this place was.

The air was thin, my head ached, and dizziness set in on top of that.

There was so little oxygen that a few steps left me out of breath.

Unsteady on my feet, I headed for the public toilet to relieve myself.

And once again a Chinese public toilet delivered a shock.

This was not merely deep in the mountains, it was extremely deep in the mountains, and probably nobody ever came up here to clean.

A Chinese public toilet in the far deep mountains that would never be cleaned by anyone, ever.

It was filthy enough to deserve an adults-only rating.

Too filthy to actually use, it did nothing but make travelers gag.

Travelers who came over to relieve themselves would look inside, wear a look of despair, and disappear off into the distant fields.

Going outdoors made many times more sense.

# The View

The bus looked to be stopping at the top of the pass for a while, so my friends and I went for a short walk around.

After walking a little way, I saw the view on the other side of the pass, and it was another shock, a big one.

From the top of the pass a gentle slope ran on and on toward the horizon, until it disappeared.

The slope was covered in lush green grass, like something out of Nausicaä, the Miyazaki anime, like the grasslands of Mongolia, a view unlike anything I had ever seen going on and on.

I had seen my share of beautiful scenery before, but this was the kind that knocks the wind out of you.

I was reeling from altitude sickness, but the moment I saw it my eyes went wide and a sigh escaped me at the beauty of it.

It was an overwhelming experience, as if my soul had been let loose and was gliding down that green slope on a hang glider.
