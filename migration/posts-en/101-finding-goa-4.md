# M the Gypsy

With the motorcycle rented, we went over to Anjuna, the next village, to see M — the German working at a restaurant there.

Sure enough, he really did work there: when we turned up he was waiting tables.

There didn't seem to be any wage, just all the food he could eat.

When I asked where he slept, he said "Here," and pointed at the restaurant floor.

Hard to believe on the spot, but that was really how it was.

He came of true Gypsy blood, he said, raised by a Gypsy family in Germany, drifting and drifting until he washed up in Goa, where he simply settled.

# The History of the Gypsies

I didn't really understand any of this back then, but let me explain a little here about the Gypsies as a people.

Gypsy is the collective name for certain peoples who live by wandering, and the details of their history are wrapped in mystery.

The wandering people of Rajasthan in India are the famous ones, though the fortune-tellers said to come out of Egypt are known as well.

In the end no one knows who they really are, but from around the fourteenth century they seem to have been living on the move all across Europe.

They work mainly in circuses, street performance, and music, and plenty of them are thieves, beggars, and prostitutes as well.

They hold a culture entirely their own while refusing, absolutely, to be civilized by the West, and they live stubbornly outside the system.

That attitude has made them hated by whoever holds power, and in the Nazi era they were massacred in great numbers, out of sight of the world.

Their refusal of the system is not only a thing of the past but goes on today: they belong to no school system, no medical system, no welfare system, living a life that depends on society and stays independent of it at the same time.

# M's Life

He came from a family of wandering Gypsies, had never been educated, and belonged to no system.

Hippies who have been on the road for years will sometimes call themselves Gypsies, but M was the genuine article.

Maybe it was all those years of wandering, but there was something of the enlightened about him, and he found his satisfaction in the simple passing of the days.

Apart from a little waiting at lunch and dinner, he sat at one of the restaurant tables, looking out at the sea and smoking hand-rolled cigarettes.

A house, money, status, a comfortable life, a girlfriend — none of it seemed to mean much to him.

Because he worked there, his foreign friends came around and spent money at the restaurant, so as far as the place was concerned he seemed well worth having.

He had been there some eight years already, and seemed to be enjoying the peace and quiet.

Which would mean not just no visa but, in all likelihood, no passport.

When I asked whether tourists just didn't come to Goa anymore, he said that wasn't it at all: high season started the next month, before Christmas.

So the reason Goa was empty now was simply that the rains had only just ended.

He preferred the off-season in the rains, he said, when the country is full of green and beautiful, and there are few people and it is quiet.

## M's Income

M worked at the restaurant for nothing.

With free meals and a place to sleep, and almost no desire for possessions, he had little need for money.

He wasn't going back to Germany, he didn't see doctors, and savings and luxuries had nothing to do with him.

Even so, he liked to go into town now and then for a good curry, so a little cash was welcome.

Which is why M bought hash cheap from one friend, added his margin, and sold it to another.

We had no real grasp of the situation, which made us convenient customers for him as well.

The convenience ran both ways: buying from M meant paying a little extra but getting good quality without being cheated.

M rolled and smoked joints from morning until night.

The way he rolled a joint had become an art — two small cigarettes broken open and loosened, the hash resin they call charas worked in, the whole thing rolled up into one big joint.

He'd smoke the joint, drink his chai, and look out at the sea from the restaurant up on the hill.

Apart from the little bit of work at midday and in the evening, he played backgammon and chess with his friends.

It wasn't that I wanted to copy it, and I probably couldn't have, but the discovery that a life like this existed was a huge culture shock.

And this was only the opening, because the shock Goa had in store for me went far beyond it.
