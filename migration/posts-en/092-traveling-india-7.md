# Chai

The chai boy, in a low voice worn far past his years, only bellowed "Chaaaiii— chaaaiii—"; with Indians, apparently, he never shook a foot to wake anyone.

Special treatment for foreigners only, it seemed.

Up on the top berth of the sleeper car, drinking my chai and sinking into the feeling of being on the road, I watched the people in the seats below start waking to the boy's call.

Everyone was buying chai.

For the boy, this had to be the big selling hour of the day.

He worked through them with quick hands.

Chai on the street in India has the finest atmosphere there is, but it doesn't taste as good as the chai at an Indian restaurant in Japan.

Cheap tea leaves boiled down past all reason until the water takes color, then thin milk and a mountain of sugar, and basically no spices at all.

Real chai, the kind with spices in it, you won't meet unless you go somewhere fairly upscale.

Nearly every dish, every snack, even the fruit salad gets spices on it, and yet chai, the most basic thing there is, doesn't — a strange business, but it's a fact, so there's nothing to be done.

# Time with an Indian Family

Climbing down from the top bunk and coming back from the toilet, I was stopped by a family using the lower berths.

"We have snacks. Why don't you eat with us?"

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog92-1.jpg)

They wanted a bit of contact with the odd-looking Asian, apparently.

I wanted the contact too, but with almost no English there was no conversation to be had.

Still, not wanting to brush off the gesture, I sat down on the lower seat and ate the snacks with them.

Indian snacks come in two main kinds, and to a Japanese palate the two kinds are extremely hot and extremely sweet.

Travelers say Indians have been eating chili in staggering quantities since childhood, until the cells that taste it go numb.

This time I was given the extremely hot kind.

Something like okaki, the Japanese rice cracker: fried sticks of wheat flour coated in an assortment of spices.

There is a cheap Japanese candy-store snack called Aji Curry — imagine that with about three times the spice and the Ajinomoto, the MSG, stripped out.

One of the good things about India is that the flavors are so violent that there would be no point in using MSG.

The food colorings and the pesticides can be something else, but you almost never find MSG.

Back then I had no thought of avoiding MSG or chemicals, but without knowing it I was reaping the benefit of food made without chemical seasoning.

# Questions

The questions started as soon as I sat down.

Where are you from, and where are you going?

Are you married?

Why aren't you married?

How many brothers and sisters do you have?

Are your parents well?

Aren't you lonely, so far from home?

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog92-2.jpg)

They were basic questions in simple English, so even I could follow them, but "Why aren't you married?" and "Aren't you lonely, so far from home?" were beyond what my English could answer.

It wasn't the first time; the same questions had come at me in the same sort of situation more than once.

In Japan the equivalent would be:

The weather today is such-and-such, isn't it.

It certainly is.

Off somewhere today?

Oh, just down the road.

That, I think, is the category these questions fall into.

But with Indians it isn't reserve, or a move toward harmony, the way a pleasantry is — it's more direct, and it comes from plain curiosity.

And since every one of them is curious about the same things, it ends up looking like a pleasantry anyway.

The longer I spent in Indian culture the clearer it became: they really do want to know the answers to those questions, and in their society the answers really do matter.

You can see from that how much Indians care about family.

For someone who came out of a wrecked family, it was a bit of a nuisance...
