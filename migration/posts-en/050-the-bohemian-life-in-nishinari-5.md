# The Duo with My Brother

I was picking up all kinds of musical experience, and the duo I had with my brother was evolving in a direction of its own.

We decided to call ourselves Senboku Community.

The name came from a free local magazine that was handed out on a regular schedule around Senboku, the suburb where we grew up, and its homey, small-town feel struck us as surreal and cool for exactly that reason — a good description of two mutations that had come out of Senboku.

My brother's upbringing after our parents' divorce had been chaotic and full of upheaval: our father, his new wife, her three children from before, and a new little sister.

Suddenly sharing a cramped house with strangers, right in the middle of adolescence — that must have left its own cracks in him.

At our mother's urging he had studied with Jehovah's Witnesses for a while; at the same time, he had the rebellious streak of a rock kid, playing in bands since around junior high.

We knew our upbringing had been one of a kind and our personalities with it, so we believed we could make music that only we could make.

Neither of us had that much musical talent, but we thought that if we played up what made us different, we might be able to make some waves in the music industry.

These days every musical possibility is already out there, and if you widen your own possibilities too far, you lose sight of yourself.

So we flipped it around: we would start from the other end, by shutting our possibilities down as far as they would go.

# The Sampling Rules

We figured that by policing the range of our music as strictly as possible, we might arrive at a sound unlike anything before it, so we drew up a few rules.

Rule 1: Don't play.

The sampler — the machine that records real sounds and makes music out of them — was our strength, we decided, so we would quit live playing entirely.

My brother was a bassist. He didn't play.

I had been learning to program drums on the computer and to work tones out of a synthesizer. That was out too.

We would build our tracks out of nothing but what other people had played and recorded.

Rule 2: Copyrighted recordings only.

The rules escalated. We might as well compose using only recordings under copyright, we decided.

And we would use them only in a form where the original artist, hearing it, would know it was theirs.

In hip-hop and so on, people sample somebody's kick drum and build a beat out of it. We took the whole beat instead of a single sound, and used it whole.

By then it had spilled outside the category of sampling and become something like the musical technique of tape collage, reproduced electronically.

A beat some band had played, with a bassline someone else had played laid over it, then a piano tone somebody had played, or audio recorded straight off the television. Experimental tape-collage music.

Tape collage in its original form is a severely experimental sound, but we were trying to make ours as pop as we could.

Rule 3: Only samples from music nobody respects.

The rules escalated further, and we added one more: no sampling from musicians who were cool in the ordinary way.

What we were allowed to sample was either artists so obscure that nobody knew them, or records so famous that everybody did.

Anyone can make a cool track out of a cool musician's sound.

Making a cool one out of a song nobody knows, or an uncool song, or a song that has been musically underrated — that was where we figured our skill would show.
