## Cut Short

The party was rolling along nicely, and then out of nowhere it had to be called off.

It was at that farmhouse deep in the mountains, and someone had blundered into the vegetable garden without realizing and wrecked it. The owner was furious, and that was the end of it.

The organizers had roped the garden off to keep people out, but the rope was not strong enough, and apparently somewhere in the middle of the night a wrecked dancer had crashed into it and brought it down.

I could see exactly why the owner was angry.

That one was entirely on the organizers.

There was no point in complaining about people who were putting the whole thing on for free, but a party that had been as good as they come was over.

Compared to Goa, being up in the mountains meant the ground had some slope to it, and nobody could honestly call it easy to dance on — but the parties in Manali had more wildness in them.

For the connection with nature, and for the sense of the mystical, Manali had the edge.

What interested me most was the crowd itself, and how undiluted it was.

Goa had a certain amount of tourist mixed into it, but in Manali it was travelers and nobody else.

It made for a world with a very strong flavor.

## The Tibetan Restaurant

We were still deep in the LSD, and we came down off the mountain still tripping.

With the senses sharpened the way they were, the connection with nature felt more real as well.

After an expedition into another dimension with your friends, hiking down while everyone talks at top speed is a kind of fun I can't put into words.

We kept going on foot, all the way into the town of Manali.

By the time we reached town the LSD had worn off and we were getting a little hungry.

"There's a Tibetan restaurant that's cheap and good — want to stop in?" the Section Chief asked, in exactly the tone of a section chief rounding up the office at the end of the workday.

The block behind the bus stop where his regular place stood was a settlement of Tibetan refugees who had moved in and built a neighborhood of their own.

Every shop was decked out in Tibetan style, with momos — Tibetan dumplings, essentially — laid out under the eaves for sale.

His place was inside a long, narrow building, where they cooked on the ground floor and carried the food up to the seating above.

The ground floor was kitchen, storeroom, and cash register all at once: an open kitchen if you wanted to be generous, a filthy mess if you didn't.

You squeezed through the gaps in it and climbed to the floor above.

That unmistakable feeling of a place the locals actually use works on a traveler every time.

The menu ran to only a few dishes, which was another point in its favor for anyone in the know.

I ordered Tibetan noodles and Tibetan dumplings, and here too, they tasted far better than what I had eaten in Tibet itself.
