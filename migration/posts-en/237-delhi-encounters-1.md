## The Veteran Japanese Hippie

It was a guesthouse travelers passed the word about, and it lived up to the talk — all kinds of interesting people were in it, and of all of them the one who hit us hardest was an older Japanese man on the road.

The small older man, who gave his name as J, was plain, unpretentious, casual in the way he carried himself, and yet something like an aura came off him in waves, and he had the weight and the charisma that only someone who has come through years of hard travel can have.

In Goa there had been an older Japanese hippie too, but for presence and charisma, J was in a different class from any other traveler.

Without J saying a word, U — wild, nothing but rebellion — was won over in an instant and started speaking to him in keigo, which is something only the real thing can do.

J had been on the road for something like thirty years, and his stories were like kintaro candy — cut the stick anywhere and you get the same face — good no matter where he started. We were hooked immediately.

## Money on the Road

There were all kinds of good stories, but what caught my interest was how he had a sharp eye for chances to make money wherever he went, pulling in big money without much effort and living for the fun of it.

It felt like my eyes had been opened to what was possible for a traveler.

The secret, according to J, was to let go of your fixations and do whatever that place and that moment allowed, and to do it smartly.

What he did most often was join one of the huge festivals held around the world, make a serious pile there, and then take the money to a country where everything was cheap and live at an easy pace.

When we asked about his biggest haul, he told us about selling sunglasses at the Love Parade, a techno event in Germany that draws a million people.

White people's eyes don't have much tolerance for sunlight, apparently, so under the strong June sun they suffer without a pair.

He timed it for that, bought cheap sunglasses in bulk in Thailand, and unloaded the lot on a million tourists.

In three days J made five hundred thousand yen — about four thousand dollars — and then, he said, took the money to India and spent a year there living for the fun of it.

That there was a way of traveling like that floored me, and meeting someone who had actually pulled it off was a shock of its own.

It made me see how small my own view of traveling had been, and it reinforced the feeling that I could do anything.
