## Anchovy Tagine

The anchovy tagine the waiter had recommended arrived.

The moment the lid came off the clay pot, the kind made just for tagine, the smell of vegetables, fish and olive oil all mingled together, and it was impossible not to get hungry.

Needless to say, it was astonishingly good.

By now I was sure of it: in Morocco, the food is pretty good wherever you eat.

It's either good or really good.

So far I hadn't come across anything bad, or anything just okay.

The secret, it seemed, was the olive oil.

The dry, sun-baked climate is apparently ideal for growing olives, so the finest fresh olive oil can be had for very little.

Eating plenty of oil also seems to work it into your skin, which helps protect you from the dry climate.

We came away slick with olive oil, yet it never sat heavy in our stomachs, and we finished the meal happy and content.

## Mint Tea

After the meal comes mint tea.

In Morocco, when people say tea, they mean mint tea.

Just as India has chai and Italy has espresso, Morocco has mint tea.

But this mint tea is a little different from the mint tea you drink in other countries.

It isn't some herbal tea with a faint scent of mint; it's steeped so strong you can almost taste the bitterness, then sweetened with an outrageous amount of sugar.

By Japanese standards of sweetness, it might be more accurate to call it a dessert you drink than an after-dinner tea.

It's that strong, and that sweet.

The tea is poured into a cup from a pot that looks like Aladdin's magic lamp.

There's a particular way to pour it: you start close to the cup, and as you pour, you gradually lift the pot up and away from it.

That makes the stream come down harder and harder, which apparently cools the tea to a drinkable temperature and mixes the sugar in evenly.

It's a Moroccan magic ritual, and it makes you feel as if you're drinking some very special tea.
