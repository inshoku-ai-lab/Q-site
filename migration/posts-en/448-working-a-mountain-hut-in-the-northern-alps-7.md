## A History of the Hut

As the days at the hut went by, I got friendly with the other part-timers, and all sorts of stories started to leak out.

The rumors about the company president in particular were often strange.

The president's grandfather had been a man who lived for his passions.

He loved nature, and he loved the mountains with everything he had.

That love grew until he built a small mountain hut, and so the hut business began.

His son, the second president, was also a man who lived for his passions.

He had a taste for art and loved nature.

Having seen how hard the first president had it, he knew the value of work in the mountains.

He looked out for his employees and built up the huts together with them.

## The Third Generation

The son of that artist president was the current president, the third generation.

Third-generation heads are often a difficult sort.

Third-generation presidents, like third-generation feudal lords, never knew the hardships their predecessors went through, so they tend to head off in a direction completely different from what those predecessors intended.

On top of that, being raised from childhood as the president's son, with all the material comforts that come with it, can leave them unable to truly trust anyone.

## The God of Management

The president of this hut may have gone down a similar path.

Maybe the responsibility and pressure of being president left him in need of strong guidance.

He became a devoted follower of a man called Sadamu Ichikura, known as "the god of management."

His creed held that the president earning as much as he possibly could was what led to the happiness of the company and its employees, and the third president pushed that idea to the extreme, until the whole operation ran on exploiting the staff's labor.

That management creed led to the recitation every morning, to the floor-polishing of "environmental upkeep," and to working days that averaged fifteen hours.

It was a harsh working environment, and it asked far too much of people, but the creed did its brainwashing so beautifully that no one ever complained.

The first president had left the city longing for the freedom of the mountains and nature, but as it passed to the second generation and then the third, his vision had turned into a tool for making money.

For the exploited staff it was more than anyone should have to put up with, but for the president's family, who made a fortune from it, and for the guests, who got a comfortable stay, it paid off handsomely.
