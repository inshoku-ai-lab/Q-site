## Departure

The savings had piled up, so I quit the job and became a free man again.

The thought of the adventure waiting ahead of me made my heart leap.

I told my family, my relatives and my friends that I was heading out again, and got the goodbyes out of the way.

Parting from my girlfriend I-chan for a while was hard, but in a few months we would see each other again.

Excitement about the journey had me in a far stronger grip than the pain of parting.

And greater still than the anticipation of adventure was the joy of escaping the closed-in feeling of Japan.

I was leaving behind the self that had played a role for a purpose and going back to being simply, plainly myself.

That sense of release felt like a solid expression of what my soul is at its core.

## The Flight

On the day I left, I-chan came to see me off.

She was open about how lonely it would be not to see me for a while, but my mind had long since flown ahead to India.

As behavior from someone in a relationship it was extremely rude, but at that moment nothing mattered to me as much as traveling in India.

We said goodbye on a promise to see each other again in five months.

The flight ran from Kansai Airport to Delhi by way of Bangkok.

I thought about stopping in Thailand to play around a little before going on to India, but more than that I wanted to be back in India without losing a moment.

For me India came before anything else, no matter what.

## The Smell

The flight ran from Kansai Airport to Delhi by way of Bangkok.

I came off the plane, and the moment I stepped inside the Delhi airport I felt India in my body.

It wasn't from seeing women in saris — the traditional dress — or men in turbans.

I felt it through the smell that belongs to India and nowhere else.

They say the first thing that hits the nose of a foreigner arriving in Japan is the smell of dashi broth and soy sauce; what I took in on arriving in India was the smell of curry mixed with sewage.

Not plain curry and not plain sewage but a smell all its own, the two mixed and fused into one, and into that came the disinfectant smell peculiar to India that my nose already knew — and together they made up the presence of India.

Breathing in this smell — a thing better described as a stench, if anything — I felt the strength go out of my legs.

The strength hadn't gone out of me because of the stench. The tension that had set hard in me back in Japan came undone, and it went out of me because I had relaxed.

I was back in India.

There was no more need to play a part and fit myself into Japanese society; I could be myself and go where my soul went.

Being myself was also, for me, a return to the home my soul came from.

That I would ever feel at home in a smell of curry mixed with sewage had never once crossed my mind, but my soul answered that stench beautifully.
