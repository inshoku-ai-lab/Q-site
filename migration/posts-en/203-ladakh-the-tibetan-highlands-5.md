## The Town of Leh

As the bus drew closer to Leh, farmhouses and fields began to show up here and there.

Coming in from country where nothing at all was man-made, the sight of civilization felt fresh.

And that civilization was nothing like the India I had seen up to then.

Ladakh is part of the Tibetan cultural world, and on the hills in the distance stood temples of the kind I had seen in Tibet.

As the town came closer it wasn't only the temples: almost every house was built in the Tibetan style, and the people living in them had Tibetan faces.

Leh was effectively a province of Tibet, where the Tibetan culture the Chinese Communist Party had destroyed in Tibet itself remained undestroyed.

Or rather than remained — it felt more as though a culture with the same roots as Tibet's had been preserved under India, a generous country, and had come into magnificent bloom.

When we reached town we got off the bus, split ourselves among auto-rickshaws, and rode to the neighborhood where most of the travelers were staying.

Most big Indian cities have an area like that, where travelers gather and you meet interesting people.

We found a cheap, comfortable guesthouse and decided to take two rooms and split up between them.

## The Travelers

Crossing a Himalayan pass with friends I had known a while and got along with, then staying in a new town together and going out to see it, was a joy.

Every one of us was an open-ended backpacker, wanting to see the whole world while spending as little as possible, so our rhythms fell into step easily and it was fun to knock around together.

Among us, traveling with no end date was the baseline, and travelers who had to be back in their country by a certain day ran on a different sense of time — getting close to them was hard — so having people who shared that sense with me was something I was truly grateful for.

A relationship where each of us could accept the other exactly as he was.

Out in the town, the streets were overflowing with travelers.

Leh's tourist season runs about three months, June through August, and August is the peak.

Walking around town, what struck me was that Leh had a cleanliness and order that were astonishing, and not at all Indian.

Maybe that came from the Tibetan people, maybe from the high altitude and the dry air, but the place was overwhelmingly clean and admirably well ordered.

And in proportion to that, it seemed, prices were more than double what they were anywhere else in India.

In a sense you could call it the Switzerland of India.

For open-ended travelers like us this was a life-or-death matter, since it meant that the longer we stayed in Leh, the more the time we had left to travel was cut in half.
