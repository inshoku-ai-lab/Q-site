## Opium

It was coming up on three months since I arrived in Vashisht, and through all of it R, my closest friend since Goa, had stayed hooked on brown sugar.

The other travelers were all dabbling in opium for fun; R alone was a real addict.

His days and nights were reversed, as always: in the middle of the night he would lay brown sugar on aluminum foil, heat it, breathe in the smoke, and play the same role-playing game on a handheld console over and over.

The levels of his characters had long since passed the point of sanity, and I suspect they may have been the highest levels anyone in human history has ever reached.

Nobody grinds levels eight hours a day for months on end.

## Cavities

Back when R was living alone downstream from Vashisht, Kit Kat bars had been his staple food.

Smoking opium made every last thing feel like too much trouble, so he passed his days without brushing his teeth, and the cavities set in.

Once he started living with his girlfriend T he gave up the Kit Kats, but the bacteria had no intention of stopping, and they kept eating away at his teeth.

The pain got to him, so he began brushing seriously, and handled the rest by taking opium to keep from feeling it.

Anyone can see, of course, that killing the pain does not cure a cavity, but that had nothing to do with R, in whom a cranky, stubborn, childish streak had begun to surface.

## The Dentist

With his Indian visa running out, R made up his mind to see a dentist in the town of Manali before he left the country.

Once he was out of India, there would be no more opium to smoke against the pain.

I don't know how many readers have any picture of what India was like at the time, but in 1999, going to a dentist there was as good as suicide.

R said he had no confidence he could sit through the treatment and wanted us with him, so T came along and the three of us headed for a dentist in a back alley of Manali.

The office was inside an old wooden building that by no stretch of the imagination could be called state-of-the-art; it felt more like a neighborhood dentist in Japan right after the war.

Inside there was nothing but an old chair that creaked and an elderly doctor of about seventy.

The room was thick with the smell of disinfectant, so there was no sense of filth about it and no fear of germs, but I could not begin to imagine that decent treatment was possible there.
