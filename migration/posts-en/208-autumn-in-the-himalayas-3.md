## The Chillum Dojo

The Tibetan restaurant next door to the guesthouse had become the place where everyone gathered to socialize and smoke cannabis, and the Tibetan restaurant on the ground floor of the guesthouse had turned into much the same thing.

The two owners had been close friends ever since they came from Tibet to India as refugees, and in their younger days the two of them had run wild with foreign hippies, wrecked on LSD.

This one went by the nickname of the Chillum Dojo, and the people who came here took the chillum more seriously, smoking the way the wandering monks of Shiva did.

The owner of the Chillum Dojo had the hardness and the stillness of an ascetic monk about him, so people who liked that kind of air collected there.

## Travelers of Shiva

Among the people who had traveled India for years, many were drawn deep into sadhu culture — the culture of the wandering holy men — and had built something distinct out of it, a foreigners' sadhu culture, you could call it.

Most of them wore dreadlocks, the Bob Marley hairstyle, and never shaved their beards at all.

It meant accepting yourself exactly as you were, nature itself; and by way of the Rastafarian thinking in reggae, it also carried the idea of harming no living thing — never letting a blade touch anything above the neck.

The length of the hair was a direct measure of the years you had put in, and anyone with long dreads hanging down to his waist got respect without question.

The Chillum Dojo drew the harder sort of traveler, and the chillum went around in a quiet room.

There was a whole etiquette to passing it, and at first the old hands would correct you on it.

I couldn't stand that kind of stiffness, so I didn't go to the Chillum Dojo much, but staying in Vashisht and avoiding chillum culture was close to impossible, and I grew into it naturally enough.

## Chillum Etiquette

There were several rules of etiquette for smoking a chillum, and one of the absolute ones was that you never put your mouth directly on it.

You took a cotton cloth called a safi — the word means clean — wet it with water, pressed it against the fired clay tube of the chillum, closed your fingers over the safi, put your mouth to your fingers, and pulled the smoke through.

Maybe it came from the same Indian idea that anything another person's mouth has touched is unclean, and maybe that idea was religious to begin with.

The chillum itself stands for Shiva's penis, so part of it is probably that you must not touch that, and part of it is probably a custom that grew out of ordinary hygiene in an India crawling with every kind of germ.
