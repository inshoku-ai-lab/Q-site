# R's House

Y and I were staying in small single rooms, but R had rented a whole house with a garden.

The rent was steep, but by paying three months up front he had apparently managed to knock quite a lot off the price.

Between the size of the place, the garden, and the location, it turned into our hangout as a matter of course.

He seemed to have decided on a long stay before he ever arrived in Goa, and he had already put together a full set of household necessities.

R's knowledge of how to live in India was something else: how to run a kerosene stove without wasting kerosene, how to fix it yourself when it broke.

How to split a raw coconut so it opened cleanly and the meat inside tasted good, how to draw out the character of each spice and make a good curry -- all of it seriously practical knowledge.

That kind of local grounding gave R a personality all his own, with plenty of room in him for other people.

He was kind to the local kids, of course, but also to travelers who were a little broken.

# S

Y and I weren't the only ones who came around to R's house; there was also an Iranian traveler named S, somewhere around thirty.

She had come to Goa on a trip a few years earlier, and the moment she fell for the place the freedom of it cracked her open, and she stopped wanting to go home to her own country.

Once a woman who grew up in the harsh conditions of a Muslim country like Iran experiences a free place like Goa, she probably cannot go back, even if she wants to.

She was a kind of freedom refugee.

She never went home after that first trip, just stayed on in Goa, working in a restaurant now and then, living what amounted to a half-homeless life.

Homeless, though, in a way completely unlike the homeless of a city, who have no choice but to live at the bottom of society -- words like home-free or nomad, living without a house and living free, probably fit her better.

Every so often she would drop by R's house, get a meal out of it, wash the dishes as thanks, and then sleep on the balcony that night without asking anyone.

That seemed to be how she went, drifting from one friend's place to the next.

In most countries that way of living is not particularly welcome, but Goa had room enough in it to take her in without a second thought.

## Dancing at the Bar

High on LSD, I dove into the bar with the music blasting out of it.

The bar had only a back wall and a roof -- no walls on the sides or the front -- and it faced the sea and the beach.

The sun had already gone down and it was dark outside, but the inside of the bar was lit by black light, the ultraviolet kind that makes certain things glow.

Inside was pitch dark, but everything that answered the black light was glowing, so moving around was no problem at all.

I went straight to the front of the speakers and started dancing full-out.

LSD sharpens the senses, so you catch every detail in the sound and connect with the music at a far greater depth.

The LSD also makes it feel like you can concentrate on the music even harder.

That concentration syncs your body to the music more tightly still, and the pleasure of dancing along with it doubles.

I emptied my mind and kept dancing.

Back in Japan I had gone to clubs every weekend and danced to techno all night long, but what happened in this bar was on a completely different plane.

My senses were already sharpened by the LSD, and now came a flood of sound and light aimed straight at them.

My body and senses were focused on the dance while my thinking stayed drunk, and along with the confusion came all sorts of realizations.

The hallucinations from the LSD came in too, and the burly, shaggy-bearded guy dancing in front of me started to look like a dancing gorilla.

Once you start thinking that way you see it that much more strongly, which happens plenty when you are sober too, but under LSD the power of suggestion is multiplied many times over, until you cannot see it as anything else.

That power of suggestion is one of the things that calls the hallucinations up.
