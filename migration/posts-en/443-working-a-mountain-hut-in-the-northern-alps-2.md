## The Relay Hut

When we reached the cable-car relay hut, we were met by M, a cheerful man with a mustache, somewhere between "big brother" and "uncle" in age.

Grinning from ear to ear, he talked to us warmly.

He was the kind of easygoing guy who makes you think you'd like to work alongside someone like that.

M had apparently been running the relay hut for years.

He'd already heard that I-chan and I had been traveling, and he asked us about our trips.

No surprise there: M was a serious traveler himself, a veteran of the road who for more than ten years had spent his summers working in the mountains and his winters traveling the world.

When I told him about places I'd been, he seemed to know most of them, and he just smirked.

That look told me he'd done plenty of traveling himself.

## The Real Climb

After M treated us to lunch at the relay hut, we started climbing again.

Up to this point, the cable car had carried our bags, but from here on we had to carry them on our own backs.

The moment I shouldered my pack and started up, I found out what the climb was really like.

Climbing with a load and climbing without one were different by a factor of several.

And it wasn't only the weight: now that we were halfway up the mountain, the air was getting thinner.

On top of that, there was still snow on the mountain that hadn't melted yet.

An hour earlier I'd been thinking this might be pretty easy, but that idea was turned completely on its head.

A heavy pack, thin air, and slippery snow underfoot, on legs that weren't used to mountain walking.

The elderly hikers on the tour were old hands at climbing, and their packs had been small to begin with, holding only the bare minimum.

Even so, they had proper gear for the snow, crampons and the like.

In the first half of the climb I'd been acting like it was nothing, but in the second half, the tables had turned completely.

I cursed the weight of my pack.

## The Mountain Hut

Hauling that heavy pack, soaked in sweat, on the edge of blacking out, I made it up the snowy slope, and there at the top was the mountain hut.

I have never been so grateful for something man-made out in the wild.

For now, at least, I was done climbing.

The pack had been heavier than I'd ever imagined and I was completely spent, but one way or another, I'd made it to the top.

That was the first wall behind me.
