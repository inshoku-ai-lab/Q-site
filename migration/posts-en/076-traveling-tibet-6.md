# To Nepal

The time to cut out of Tibet and head for Nepal was coming.

Legally, Tibet is an autonomous region inside China, and the Chinese visa is what applies there.

I had spent about ten days in China and another ten in Tibet.

The Chinese visa was good for a month, so I had only about ten days left.

The road from Tibet to Nepal carries overwhelmingly less traffic than the road from China into Tibet, so it was unpaved, and every heavy rain tore it apart.

Whether a bus could even set out within those ten days was doubtful, people said.

Rumor had it that the recent rains had collapsed the road and that nothing would be moving by bus for another two weeks.

Foreign travelers with their visas running out had no choice but to make the trip by Land Cruiser.

The Land Cruiser is Toyota's big four-wheel-drive, and it had become the standard way to move along Tibet's rough mountain roads.

Money was one reason and the sleeper bus was another: I had wanted to travel by bus, but that was not going to happen.

So I decided to stay in Lhasa until my visa was down to the wire, then head for Nepal by Land Cruiser.

Sharing the road to Nepal with me were an American traveler, plus a Tibetan couple and a businessman.

Being the only Japanese there gave me a trace of unease, but after nearly a month of traveling in China it was not all that frightening.

# Departure

We gathered at the guesthouse before dawn and set out on the road to Nepal.

There were two drivers, apparently taking turns so the car could keep running day and night.

The road toward Nepal was paved for a while, but once it climbed into the deep mountains it was exactly as the rumors had it — wrecked by the rain.

The damage was not in one place; it was in several.

In some spots the roadbed had caved in, in others a landslide had buried it.

No road for a full-size bus, that much was obvious.

Our Land Cruiser had serious four-wheel drive, and it steered around the broken stretches and ran along the mountain slope beside the road.

At one point an instant river left by the rains ran straight across the road, but even with a meter (three feet) of the body under water it did not care.

If it had been a bus, wouldn't it have broken down the moment it went under?

Among Tibetans the Land Cruiser was rated so highly that they would tell you nothing but a Toyota Land Cruiser can get through Tibet.

And in fact no other kind of vehicle was out there. Land Cruiser or nothing.

Getting into Tibet from China we had crossed a pass over five thousand meters (16,000 feet), and reaching the Nepalese side meant crossing another pass over five thousand.

This time, though, after nearly two weeks up at that altitude, I got over it easily, with no altitude sickness.

# The Border

We spent two nights in the car and reached the Nepalese border on the morning of the third day.

We said goodbye to the drivers, and the two travelers, the American and I, went on ahead.

At the immigration office set up at the border, we finished the exit paperwork.

It was simple, and we were outside China without trouble.

From there we walked across the bridge to the Nepalese side.

On the far side of the bridge was Nepal's own immigration office, and that is where we did the entry paperwork.

The Nepalese visa system was simple: you paid cash at the office and got a one-month visa on the spot.

When I heard the road had been washed out I had wondered what would become of us, but here we were in Nepal, in one piece.

We headed for a village called Tatopani, about a thirty-minute walk from the border.

Unlike the wastelands of China and Tibet, the Nepalese side overflowed with green.

The road ran deep in a valley, with the mountain slopes rising sheer on both sides.

Spring water seeped down those slopes and wove the moss into a landscape all its own.

Far off, a waterfall came down from high on a mountain to join the river at the bottom of the valley.

Nothing like the harsh country we had been in: water, green, steam, warm air, no wind, a gentle mood hanging over everything.

A little while after we started walking, the American with me turned around and shouted at the top of his lungs.

"FUCK YOU, CHINA!!!"

Middle finger up, no attempt at all to hide the anger.

Even I, who can read kanji, had a fairly hard time of it, so I imagine what he went through was something else — white, and a traveler at a glance.

I couldn't speak even broken English, so I couldn't ask him for the details of what he was angry about, but there must have been all kinds of outrageous travel stories in it.

Having shouted and gotten the anger out of his system, he was saying something, laughing with an innocent grin.

I had no idea what it was, but the joy of getting out of China and the joy of reaching Nepal were the same.

I found myself smiling, and, making no sense of any of it, we laughed out loud together.
