## Because It's a Mountain Hut

The grime and inconvenience of mountain huts have historical roots, and in this respect huts are nothing like ordinary hotels.

Historically, a mountain hut was a place near the summit where you could take shelter from the elements and get enough food and water not to starve.

An emergency facility for staying alive so you could keep climbing.

Sitting that close to the summit, a hut has no valley springs nearby, so water is always short.

The only food available is a limited range of things that keep.

If you can eat something hot, that alone is enough.

## Inconvenience

Because the whole thing is built on the inconvenience of such extreme places as its baseline, climbers accept it without complaint.

If anything, climbers take some pride in being tough enough not to mind the inconvenience and the grime.

That mindset has something in common with traveler culture.

Even now, some small huts deep in the backcountry have no electricity and look after their climbers with oil lamps and meals cooked over firewood.

The oil and food are carried up on foot, and the firewood is cut from the surrounding forest.

This very simple way of running a hut, close to nature, has passionate supporters among some climbers.

## Convenience

At the same time, though, in the modern age, supplies come up by helicopter, water is pumped up, electricity comes from generators and solar power, food is kept in freezers, and some huts even have satellite internet.

At the big hut I'd just come from, water is pumped up from the valley twenty-four hours a day, and the generator runs around the clock too.

The cooking runs on gas and freezers, and the food is brought up by helicopter.

All this extreme convenience was the doing of the president, a devotee of "the god of management," and climbers welcomed it gladly.

But people who remembered the good old mountain huts often criticized it as far too much material comfort.

Of course, the president had far too much nerve to let something like that bother him.
