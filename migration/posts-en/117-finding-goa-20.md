# Friends

As December and Christmas came closer, the number of travelers kept growing.

O, a Japanese woman, turned up around that time.

Y, the man I had been running around with ever since I got to Goa, was a former bōsōzoku and a current fighter, a film-industry man who worked as an assistant director, and a player.

A single woman showing up must have made him happy, because he made his move on her in the blink of an eye.

Raised in a strict religious household and slow with women because of it, I was floored by how fast he worked.

Y's looks scored low enough that he admitted it himself: "the one weak point in this perfect man is my face."

Even being generous about it, his was not a face that drew women.

A banchō since childhood, with stamina and sex drive to spare, he had faced down both his hunger to be wanted and the handicap of his own face, and had spent his whole life honing every other ability that might get him women.

That drive gave him consideration for others, a sense of responsibility, and a code about cutting the strong down to size and standing up for the weak — the values of a hero out of Shonen Jump, the boys' manga magazine.

It figures that the hero of his teenage years was Matchy, the idol singer Masahiko Kondo.

He was running flat out down his own idea of the hero's road, and that road seems to be what carried him to the bōsōzoku, to fighting, and to the assistant director's chair.

O was a city girl and a nerd for film music, and the shared taste made us friends right away.

### The Day of the Full Moon

December 4, 1998 — a date I have never forgotten.

There was a full moon that night.

December had finally arrived, and Goa's party season was starting.

We had been enjoying parties at a beach bar, fifty or so people at a time, but those were supervised parties, when it came down to it.

What a real party was, I had no idea until that day.

For days beforehand the local motorcycle-taxi drivers had been telling us, with obvious pleasure, that there would be a party at Anjuna on the night of the full moon.

A party is payday for them too, which must be where the pleasure came from.

We had N, a seasoned Japanese traveler we had just met, get hold of LSD for us, and made ready for the full moon party.

It was fresh LSD that a European had brought over from Amsterdam, and we were told we could trust it to work.

You might wonder what freshness has to do with a chemical, but it matters a great deal — LSD breaks down on contact with air and light.

The best way to store it is plastic wrap, then aluminum foil, then the freezer, but in India in those days few houses had a refrigerator at all, so well-kept LSD basically did not exist.

Which is exactly why LSD freshly smuggled in from Europe was so prized.

LSD is built mainly around a compound called LSD-25 — the twenty-fifth success in the run of experiments that produced it.

A piece of trivia I picked up later: at the ashram of Aum Shinrikyo, the cult behind the Tokyo subway gas attack, they kept the research going and completed an LSD-28 under the brand name Shiva.

Something like Tetsujin 28, the giant robot from the old cartoon.

They used it to brainwash their followers, apparently.

The LSD of that era was an assortment of chemicals mixed together and soaked into little printed squares of paper, about seven millimeters (a quarter inch) on a side.

The one we got hold of that time went by the name Guevara.

The paper carried a portrait of Che Guevara, the man who led the Cuban Revolution.

Many years later I realized that the picture had carried a meaning that mattered, but at the time I gave it no thought at all.

Back when I was living in Osaka, I went to an event put on by Ninja Tune, the British hip-hop and trip-hop label, and came away with a Che Guevara T-shirt that one of the bands had thrown into the crowd.

That was how I came to know about Che Guevara, and I read a biography and came away deeply impressed.

He was a symbol of revolution.

Through this LSD called Guevara, I would go through a major revolution in my own consciousness.
