## The Military Uniform Factory

I went to see T, a friend I'd met in India, at the place he was living.

It turned out to be one of those places they call a squat house — a style of communal living, with a whole crowd of people sharing it.

The way they told it, the building had originally been a factory that made military uniforms.

Then a change in policy left it unused, and after it had sat abandoned for a while, a few years back somebody slipped inside and started living there without asking anyone — and that was how the squat house began.

After that, their friends came and stayed, and then friends of friends did the same.

By then about thirty people were living together in the two residential buildings, or so I was told.

## Two Camps

The residents fell neatly into two types, and the building T lived in was mostly home to artists and travelers.

Painters, filmmakers, musicians, DJs, travelers, hippies, meditators.

The mood in that building was bright.

The other building was the opposite, heavy with a dark mood, a hangout for drunks, junkies, and the homeless.

The two sets of residents were too different to be friendly, but that did not mean they fought; grudgingly, they coexisted in peace.

A free place to live is worth something to anyone, and they had learned to share.

## Dumpsters

Hardly anyone living here worked for money.

Food still costs money, though, doesn't it? They had a trick of their own for that too.

It's called dumpster diving: you go to where the supermarket dumps its trash, bring home the food that has been thrown out, and eat it.

By ordinary standards, taking food out of the garbage is what a beggar does, not what a person living a decent life does — that would be the general opinion.

Back then I thought the same thing.

It was nearly ten years after this that I found out what dumpster diving actually brings home: fresh vegetables and fruit that were on the store shelf half an hour ago, and some of it not even past the best-by date.

Say a store clears out expired stock once a week — then something with six days still left on it goes out with the rest.

The same logic throws out fruit that isn't even ripe yet, potatoes that have started to sprout, a six-pack of beer cans with a broken carrying handle.

The people in this squat used the free housing and the free food, lived at no real cost, and threw themselves into making art.

The ones in the other building threw themselves into getting drunk.
