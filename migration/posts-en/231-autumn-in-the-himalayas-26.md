## The Treatment

Sure enough, a dentist in India was India through and through.

Maybe the concept of treatment does not exist there to begin with.

One look at R's teeth, and the doctor decided on the spot to pull them.

Whether any option other than pulling even existed is doubtful.

He drove a fat needle of anesthetic into R and waited for it to take hold.

After a while the work began, and the extraction went ahead to strange, explosive noises coming out of R that never quite became a voice.

Three of them, apparently, were rotted all the way down to the root.

Teeth that far gone were probably weak at the root already, and I imagine the doctor was well practiced at pulling them, too.

He had all three out in no time.

The anesthetic apparently had not done much, and between the adrenaline of that much pain, the tears and the tension, R came out of the treatment room wobbling and only half conscious.

We had thought about stopping in town before heading back, but he was in no shape for it.

T went back to the guesthouse with R, and I stayed in town to do the shopping — theirs as well as mine — and went back after.

## Moving Out

My roommate K's visa was running out, so he left Vashisht, and at exactly that moment another friend, O, asked me to share a room with him.

Winter was coming on, and with it the time to leave Vashisht, so moving once and living a different kind of life for a while before moving on did not seem like a bad idea.

O had been traveling the world for about two years already, had all kinds of experience behind him and a good eye for art, and sharing a room with him looked like it would be inspiring.

The guesthouse I had been staying in until then was a two-minute walk from the hot springs, well placed, and the concrete building was solid, with a steady sort of comfort to it.

The new room was the opposite in every way, a fifteen-minute climb up the mountain from the springs.

Nothing around it, no buildings, no shops — just a sweeping view.

A traditional Himalayan house of mud walls, a stone roof and wooden posts, weathered enough to look as though it had already been standing fifty years.

It had none of the solidity of concrete, but inside there was an iron stove they call a tandoor, and you could light a fire and cook right there in the room.

The floor creaked with every step, the ceiling was low and the room a little dim, but the days spent cooking and living around that stove were something completely new.
