## The Other Side of Freedom

Goa's freedom drew travelers from all over the world, but in exchange for that freedom it also produced no end of trouble.

I have already written about the corruption of the police, and tied in with it there is the problem of thieves.

Some of my friends had been robbed, and after watching how often the police and the thieves struck, Y started putting forward a conspiracy theory about the police, the thieves, and the landlords.

Back then I told him it was ridiculous, that he was overthinking it, but now that I know the underside of the world well, I can no longer say flatly that the story was a lie.

I think it was probably true.

The area around Anjuna Beach, where we were, was known in particular for drawing novice Japanese travelers like us, and the bad characters who wanted to shake money out of foreigners came there looking for easy prey.

People like that are connected behind the scenes, feeding each other information and squeezing travelers dry.

How far up the connections actually run is anyone's guess, but it would be no surprise if all of it were connected.

## How Travelers Get Squeezed

The first to arrive is a police officer.

He knocks on the door and goes around asking whether anything suspicious has been going on.

The official line is concern for the traveler, but if there is anything at all he can pin on you — a motorcycle without an international license, an expired visa, drugs of any kind, however trivial — he will threaten you with arrest without mercy.

An arrest is not what he is after, the bribe is, so he bargains with the traveler over not making the arrest and walks off with the money.

While he is there, he takes stock of the layout of the house, who is staying in it, how many of them, and the rhythm of their days.

Next, a few days later, a thief who has heard all of it from the officer comes to the same house.

The thief knows how many people are in the house and what hours they are out, so he can lift the tiles off the roof and get in safely.

And the traveler, robbed of everything he owns, loses heart and goes home.

The landlord knows about the problem and leaves the roof, easy to get in through, unrepaired.

Probably the landlord is in on it as well.

Then the next traveler arrives, and in short order the police come, the thief comes, and the traveler goes home.

Kind-hearted restaurant owners and the like will warn you that you would do better not to stay at that guesthouse.

They never spell out the reason — they have their neighbors to think of — but they must know a great deal.

## The Real Party Season

Once mid-December had passed and Christmas was coming up, Goa began moving into its real party season.

The number of travelers climbed exponentially.

The next party was not in a bar or a restaurant but out in the forest.

A few days before this party, R had run his pants through the wash with the LSD still in the pocket, wrapped in plastic, and what he had ready was the slightly damp, faintly detergent-scented, it's-probably-fine sort of LSD.

What I had ready was a brand of LSD called Simpsons.

On a scrap of paper about a centimeter (half an inch) square was the face of Bart Simpson from the cartoon, and the LSD had a reputation for being barely cut, pure, and good.

Along with it, to keep from going into a bad trip, I had a small amount of ecstasy ready.

Y was saying that one tab wasn't enough for him and he might eat two.

Around this time we began to notice that how hard the LSD hit depended on a person's constitution.

R and N were about average, but T — thin, no stamina, the artistic type — and I got hit far more easily than the others.

Y, on the other hand, was a fighter packed with muscle, and his liver was trained up too, so one tab of LSD left him completely unfazed.

Working it out by trial and error, we got ourselves set up to enjoy the night as much as possible, and headed into the outdoor party.
