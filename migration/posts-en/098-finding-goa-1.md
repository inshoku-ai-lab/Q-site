# Goa

The train ran all night and pulled into Goa in the morning.

Goa was not just the next stop on the road but the final destination of this whole journey.

It had taken three and a half months since leaving Japan, by sea and over land, to get this far.

I couldn't help expecting a lot of it.

Out of the station, onto a bus, and into town.

Goa being a Christian state, churches showed up here and there as we went.

Unlike Pune, a modernized city, this place brimmed with everyday life.

Even so, it had none of the poverty or filth of Varanasi, and what came through instead was the abundance of the tropics.

The guidebook said you could live relatively cheaply at Calangute Beach, so that was where I headed.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog98-1.jpg)

A few minutes' walk from the beach I found a cheap room, about 150 yen a night, something like a dollar twenty.

A narrow room with one single bed, a shared toilet and cold-water shower, the bare minimum, but there was a wide garden with bright flowers blooming all over it.

It was not a garden anyone kept up carefully, yet the tropical plants growing thick and unruly in every inch of it brought a pleasant sense of ease.

From what I had heard, hippie travelers gathered in this town from all over the world, but so far I had not met a single foreigner.

# Cooking for Myself

Since I was planning to stay in Goa a while, I bought a kerosene stove, a portable burner that runs on lamp oil, along with some cooking gear.

Cooking for myself instead of eating in restaurants ought to save a great deal.

The market had familiar ingredients laid out, and ingredients I had never seen in my life.

Being a town on the sea, there was plenty of fish and shellfish, and being a Christian town, beef and pork were out on the counters as well.

That abundance of ingredients is probably one of the reasons foreign travelers favor Goa.

In India alcohol is generally shunned, and in a religious city like Varanasi people apparently buy it out of sight, but in Goa it is sold like anything else.

Compared with other Indian towns, the place looked freer in all sorts of ways.

For my first meal cooked by my own hand, I decided on fish.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog98-2.jpg)

The first fish since day one of the journey, three and a half months back.

The seasoning was simple, just salt and spices, but it had the kind of flavor that clings to a Japanese tongue.

A hundred yen or so of ingredients had bought considerable luxury, and at this rate a long stay would be easy, and I was thoroughly pleased with myself.

# Anjuna

A few days went by eating well and swimming in the sea, but with no friends anywhere I started to get bored.

Wondering whether anything interesting was going on, I changed buses and went to look at Anjuna, the next town over.

In a restaurant on the way from the bus stop down to the beach, there was a Japanese traveler.

With not one foreign traveler anywhere in sight, I was glad to run into someone who spoke my own language.

He had been bored too, with no travelers around.

An older Western guy was sitting at the table as well.

Invited over, I took a seat and ordered a chai.

They had been playing backgammon, it seemed.

I could feel the boredom coming off them.

The Japanese man was called Y, and in Japan he had worked as an assistant film director.

An industry guy.

Through a friend's connection he had heard a rumor that something interesting was happening in Goa, and he had come all the way here, only to find no people and nothing going on, and he was feeling low about it.

The older Western guy was called M, a Gypsy who had come from Germany, and he lived at this restaurant, working there and sleeping there.

Huh?

A Gypsy?

Working at a restaurant and living there?

I could not follow what he was telling me, but in time I would come to understand what it meant.
