# A Restaurant in Shanghai

Three days of seasickness and not a bite of food had left me wrung out, but inside I was lit up.

I set off into the streets of Shanghai in high spirits.

The seasickness had settled, and I wanted something, anything, to eat, so I walked around looking for a restaurant.

A sign with characters that looked like they had to do with food hung on the second floor of a building.

Up the stairs and through the door, and sure enough, it was a restaurant.

It had a homey feel to it, but it was tidy and clean in a way that felt good.

The place was small, seats for about twenty, and a few customers were sitting at them.

It was around three in the afternoon when I came in, which is probably why it wasn't busy.

I held out the guidebook in my hand, pointed at the characters for ordering a dish, and had them read it.

Getting by in writing did seem to work; they understood right away.

If this was how it went, I'd manage for the rest of the trip.

After a while, out came a large platter of Chinese-style fried fish.

The fish had been dredged in batter and deep-fried, with a sweet-and-sour thickened sauce poured over it.

A plate of rice and hot tea came with it, on the house.

As for the taste — this being the homeland of Chinese cooking — not one complaint.

I had just been setting out to travel plain and simple, and here I was eating the best fish I had ever had, thoroughly satisfied.

# The Bill

The trouble came when it was time to pay.

It came to about fifteen hundred yen, twelve dollars or so, which for China was outrageously expensive.

Not knowing how to order, I never asked the price, and that was another thing I got wrong, I suppose.

Or rather: I didn't know how to check a price, and I didn't know how to order.

With no idea what to do, I paid what I was told to pay, had no idea how to complain either, and said only xie xie — thank you.

Had I been ripped off? Was it a high-end dish? Or was that just the going rate?

Coming right after a meal that good and that satisfying, it left me with a feeling that wouldn't quite settle, and I walked out telling myself to be more careful from here on.

What the problem had been, I worked out later.

The Chinese in the guidebook said: Please serve me the most delicious dish in this restaurant.

The staff had simply done what the Japanese customer asked, paid no attention to the price, and brought out the most delicious dish they had. That was all it was.

# A Room for the Night

With a good meal in my stomach, the only thing left to do that day was find somewhere to sleep.

The guidebook listed a few hotels, but they cost a fair amount.

For someone who wanted to keep costs down and travel on a shoestring, that was an option to avoid if I could.

Walking along the street toward the center of the city, I spotted a sign with characters that looked like they meant lodging.

The building was dated and the windows didn't look particularly clean, which made me think a night there might come pretty cheap, so I went in through the entrance.

A price list hung on the wall.

Far cheaper than anything in the guidebook.

Relieved, I let the older guy at the front desk know I wanted to stay the night.

Whether it got through to him or not I couldn't tell, but he was making a gesture that meant no.

Full, maybe?

I gave up and headed for another place.

This one was cheap too, and looked fine.

I said I wanted a room, and for some reason I was turned down again.

After about five refusals like that, I finally found somewhere that would take me.

After blowing money at the restaurant, getting a cheap room felt good.

The room was a simple thing — four and a half tatami mats of space, about seven square meters (eighty square feet), with a plain bed and a small desk and nothing else.

A curtain hung over the window, and looking out, I could see the building next door.

On the desk sat a thermos, filled to the top with boiling water.

For now, that was enough to relax.

I had made it here from Japan in one piece, my stomach was full, and I had found a place where I could sleep without worrying.

That night I wrote in the notebook I had brought along, then went to bed.
