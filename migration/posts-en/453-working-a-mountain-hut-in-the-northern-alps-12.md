## The Move

Just as I was starting to get used to the work at the big hut, the day came to move on to the next one.

I had come to work in the mountains so I could spend time with my girlfriend, I-chan, while we both worked, but as it turned out, we hardly spent any time together, and now I was being sent on to the next hut.

The hut I was headed to was far smaller than the one I was working at, with a staff of just three.

The manager and two helpers, and that was it.

Two more would join them at the height of summer, I was told, but even then it would be less than a third of the staff at the current hut.

Word among the staff was that the small hut was a good place to work, and it was the envy of everyone.

All I'd heard were good things, so my hopes were running high.

## Helicopter

The walk from the big hut to the small one took about eight hours, but since a lot of cargo had to go up for opening the hut for the season, I was allowed to catch a ride on a helicopter.

It would, of course, be the first helicopter ride of my life.

I couldn't even begin to picture what riding in one would be like.

The day I was leaving was also the day of the helicopter supply lift, so helicopter after helicopter would be coming up by way of the cable-car relay hut.

That day everyone got up at four in the morning to brace for a ridiculously busy day.

The interesting part was that the helicopters didn't come in from above; they rose up from below our feet.

A helicopter far off down below would make its way toward us, its roar echoing all through the mountains.

Having appeared from below, it kept climbing until it hung for a moment in the air above the hut's helipad.

Then a staff member well practiced at working with the helicopters would guide it in and get it to set its load down on the helipad.

The helicopter never stopped on the helipad; it did a literal *tonbo-gaeri* — the Japanese word for turning straight back around, which literally means a somersault — and went right back down to the relay hut for the next load.

I said a literal *tonbo-gaeri* there (written with the character for "flip," not the one for "go home"), and I really do mean literal.

With some acrobatic piloting, the helicopter would drop away downward in what was very nearly a backflip.

I was told that the helicopter pilots who work in the mountains have some of the best flying skills in all of Japan.
