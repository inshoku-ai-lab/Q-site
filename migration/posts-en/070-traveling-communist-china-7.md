# The Temple in Golmud

While we were staying in Golmud to let our bodies adjust to the altitude, we went out to see a temple.

It did not seem to be a famous one, but apparently plenty of foreign tourists stop in the way we had, while waiting out their acclimatization in Golmud.

Being a tourist spot, the local Chinese had gotten a little jaded, and they were not easy to deal with.

Overcharging you was simply the default, and if you let your guard down you would be handed an outrageous bill.

I was trying to buy a steamed pork bun and being quoted some enormous price when one of my Chinese-speaking friends stepped in and rescued me.

I am not sure "rip-off" is quite the right word for it, but the ticket into the temple also came at two prices, one for Chinese and one for foreigners.

The gap was about three to one, and foreigners got squeezed quite aggressively.

The shopkeepers around there were probably collecting triple from foreigners too.

But the people I was traveling with were Japanese exchange students studying in Beijing.

They had survived life in China by passing as Chinese, and here too they passed as student tourists visiting from Beijing, which got us into the temple at the Chinese price.

The temple was an old-fashioned Chinese building with a great historical figure enshrined inside.

Less a Buddhist temple than a shrine — or maybe just a small wayside shrine.

The old traditional architecture was beautiful.

The garden was neatly kept, and you could feel a moment of calm there.

Getting in at the Chinese price was satisfying all the same, but nothing about the place particularly interested me.

### Public Toilets

What interested me was not the temple but the public toilet.

In today's modernized China it must be completely different, but this was 1998.

Whether it was standard for China, or only for that town, or something Mongolian, I have no idea — but to my astonishment the public toilets in Golmud were communal, and not only for urinating but for defecating as well.

Inside the men's or the women's, a single trench ran down the middle, divided up by boards.

You straddle the trench to do your business, and since there are no doors you are in full view from the side.

The boards spared me the sight of the backside of the man ahead of me, but what he produced came flowing down the trench.

Then mine joined it and flowed on, into the view of the man behind me.

I had never seen another person's fresh excrement from that close, and the shock was enormous.

I had come to China wanting an experience that would smash everything I took for granted, and what smashed it was a public toilet.

When I told the exchange students about it, they set me straight: China is simply like this, and you do not carry Japanese common sense into the country.

Live in Beijing a year and you apparently get used to most things.

# Touring Tibet

The Chinese government does not allow foreigners to travel to Tibet on their own.

If a foreigner wants to enter Tibet officially, the only way is to book a tour through a government-approved travel agency and see the place on the itinerary they set.

The tour covers not only the bus to Tibet but the hotels and the tickets and buses to the sights.

The other way is to negotiate with one of the truck drivers running back and forth between China and Tibet, ride in the bed of his truck, and cross in illegally.

We had Asian faces and Chinese on our side, so sneaking in did cross our minds.

But the exchange students said that if we were caught they could have their student visas revoked and be deported, so we let the idea go.

We went to a travel agency in Golmud and signed up for a tour.

It cost about twenty thousand yen, a hundred and sixty dollars or so — almost exactly what the ferry from Kobe to Shanghai had cost.

With hotels and the buses to the sights included, that was not a bad price.

I was full of anticipation for whatever kind of journey through Tibet was waiting.
