# Setting Sail

I was lying on my bunk flipping through the guidebook when the others started coming in, one at a time, and by the time the room was about half full the ship pulled out of port.

Everyone in the room was a Chinese migrant worker, and talking with them didn't look likely.

They all kept quiet on their own bunks.

A few hours out, around the time we reached open ocean, things started to go wrong.

The ship rolled harder than I had expected.

I had been on ferries several times before, but always on the inland sea, and nothing had ever rolled like this.

# Seasick

The rolling was worse than expected, and, exactly as expected, I got seasick.

As I wrote back in the story about the mountain roads in Peru, I get motion sick very easily, and it has put me through a rough time more than once.

For a few hours I rode out the rolling and kept the nausea down, but the limit was coming, so I headed for the toilet to get ready to throw up.

The toilet was luckily only about three meters (ten feet) away, but walking those three meters was close to impossible.

Maybe it was being on my feet — a meter in, the ship threw me around and the nausea hit its peak.

The last two meters I covered at something close to a jump, and I just barely made it: the sink, not the corridor.

Done throwing up and feeling a little better, I rested on my bunk.

A few hours later the nausea rose again and I dove for the toilet.

I repeated this process again and again, through the night and all the next day.

I am used to the nausea rush of motion sickness, but that doesn't make throwing up any easier.

After enough rounds of it there was nothing left to bring up, so I lay down on my bunk, and still the sickness would not let up at all.

Uneasy about when the nausea would ever stop, I went on waiting for the time to pass in rhythm with the roll of the ship.

How long I had been throwing up, whether I had rested, whether I had slept at all — I had no idea, and while I lay there in that state, the announcement came that we were arriving in Shanghai.

In the end the three-day, two-night voyage was over, and I had done nothing on it but be seasick.

It really had been nothing but the bunk and the toilet, back and forth.

Forget the ping-pong table — in those three days I did not eat a single meal.

That the great adventure I had been so full of hope for should open with the worst seasickness of my life was a real letdown, but I knew this weakness of mine well, and I decided to take it as one of those things.

# Shanghai

The ship rolled hard, but it carried me to Shanghai without a single problem.

At long last the rolling was over and I had my feet on land.

Inside me, the waves were still rolling.

The port was flooded with Chinese characters, just as I had pictured.

Plenty of them were not used in Japan, but luckily I could understand what most of them meant.

I could work with this.

I went to customs and showed my passport.

Somewhere in the exchange I used the word passport, and it did not get through at all.

The man I was talking to was a customs officer.

An international port in one of the biggest cities in the world, and the English word passport meant nothing.

Unbelievable!

I had landed in the country of real chaos.

Being unable to make myself understood at customs was about as inconvenient as a situation gets, but I had hit my goal of experiencing real chaos right out of the gate, and even though seasickness and an empty stomach had worn me down to nothing, I was elated.
