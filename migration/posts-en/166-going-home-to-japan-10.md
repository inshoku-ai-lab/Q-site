## The Body

The first days were rough, but by the time a week was up, my twenty-year-old body had adapted easily.

It grew stronger by the day, and there was real pleasure in finding I could shoulder heavier and heavier loads, and pleasure too in building something alongside the boss and the older hands.

Until I left on my travels, my body had done no physical activity of any kind beyond dancing at a club on weekends.

I had swallowed whole the notion that I was no good at anything athletic, and had lived keeping my distance from exercise of every kind.

Rather than face being hopeless at sports and put in the practice, I chose to escape the shame of putting that hopelessness on display, by convincing myself that I hated exercise.

My school years were the go-home club, playing cards and video games with friends or hitting the arcade — geek days, all of them.

There had been no chance at all for my body to grow.

And now — eight hours a day of punishing physical labor.

Carrying forty-kilo (ninety-pound) bars of rebar on my shoulder up and down stairs had become routine, and my body was growing fast.

## The Food Bill

In proportion to how my body grew, the amount I ate went up dramatically.

The volume was so staggering that my mother teased me for eating "like the food had killed my parents."

If you know the reference, picture Kinnikuman, the wrestler from the manga, putting away a bowl of gyudon, beef over rice.

I just ate and ate, in quantities that surprised even me.

Part of it was fuel for building a body, part of it was hunger for the Japanese food I hadn't been able to eat in seven and a half months on the road, and part of it, probably, was the instinct of a traveler who had survived India.

On the road, depending on when you had to move, there were times you couldn't get a proper meal, and it was a nerve-grinding, brutal environment where you traveled threading your way between thieves — that, I think, is where the animal instinct in me woke up.

My food bill in that period must have run to several times what my mother normally spent.

The work had steadied and I was earning properly, so I decided to start paying into the household.

The rent my mother paid on our unit in the prefectural housing block was next to nothing, but the food bill had gone way up because of me.

Paying rent while living under my mother's roof had never once crossed my mind, but handing money over was how I wanted to confirm that I stood on my own.

It was at the same time a ritual of cutting the cord, a declaration of independence, and a way of taking full responsibility for myself.

And more than anything else, it was my own way of squaring the account with myself for having decided to live exactly as I pleased, selfish to the last.

Out of gratitude to my mother, who had raised me and cooked for me, I decided on fifty thousand yen a month — about four hundred dollars — starting with my first paycheck.

My take-home pay was a hundred and seventy thousand yen, some thirteen hundred dollars, so even after handing over the fifty thousand I could put away a hundred and twenty thousand every month.

As long as I was working there was no time to go out anyway, and for the sake of getting back to India I could put up with anything.

Dreaming of a triumphant return to India, I worked day after day and put the money away bit by bit.
