## How to Walk

The other thing they taught me was how to walk on the way down.

People tend to think going down a mountain is easy, since you just keep stepping and let gravity do the work, but the truth is the opposite: most accidents happen on the descent.

The reason is that when you step down, your whole body weight plus the weight of your pack, sped up by gravity, comes down on one foot alone.

Land that foot on a loose stone and you twist your ankle, and if you twist it at the edge of the trail, you go off the trail.

And if what's past the edge is a cliff, it could cost you your life.

That's mountaineering: the smallest thing can kill you.

The way to avoid it, I was taught, is to shorten your stride, whatever else you do.

Short steps take the strain off your legs and hips, and if you do happen to step on a loose stone, it's easier to recover.

But shorten your stride and nothing else, and you'll never get off the mountain, so the short steps have to be quick.

Hurrying along in quick little steps under a big load makes you look ridiculous, like a penguin, but apparently real mountain men walk like penguins.

One more tip: when there's a big drop in the trail, taking it in two or three steps eases the strain.

## Into Town

I carried my pack the way they'd taught me, took short steps, and made it to the foot of the mountain without much trouble.

Our ride was already there, and we set off for town right away.

In town there was a dorm that staff from the company's huts could use, and people on their time off stayed there.

The plan was to rest there for a few days and then go back up to work at the big hut.

For me, though, the biggest ordeal was this car ride down.

I've always been prone to motion sickness, so I avoid getting around by car, and mountain roads, as much as I can, but coming down from a mountain hut there's no way around it.

Those winding, snaking mountain roads might as well have been built to make you carsick.
