## The Police Raid

The Tibetan organizers plunged into the forest and tore straight down through the trees without once looking back.

Sure enough, a group of police came out from the back and announced that the party was over.

That was the moment to run down through the forest with the Tibetans, but I had no real sense of danger and stayed on at the party.

I wasn't carrying any drugs, and simply being at a party was not in itself a crime, so I figured that once the necessary formalities were out of the way, that would be the end of it.

The police came into the courtyard, lined the travelers up, and sorted them into two groups.

One group with ID, the other without.

For better or worse I had my passport on me, so I was in the clear, but for the ones without ID it looked like things were going to get a lot more complicated.

Y was nowhere in sight by then, and I hoped he was safe.

## The Walk

We waited a while for it all to settle, but with Indian inefficiency nothing moved at all, and in the end the procedure came down to this: everyone, ID or no ID, would walk into town to the police station, sign a form, and be released.

About fifty of us, the partygoers who hadn't gotten away in time, formed a line and headed down the asphalt mountain road.

Old Manali to Manali is about a thirty-minute walk.

Travelers in full psychedelic getup, wrecked on LSD, walking that road with a police escort — it was a pretty strange sight.

Everyone was wrecked, but everyone also understood that this was not going to turn into anything serious, so the mood stayed easygoing as we walked.

For the first stretch it was a pleasant enough walk, but partway down I noticed something.

Our numbers were steadily shrinking.

The Europeans and Israelis who had been in the line with us were breaking away one at a time and disappearing.

I wanted to make a run for it myself, but never quite found the right moment, and walked all the way into town.

By the time we reached the police station, most of the fifty or so walkers had vanished, leaving one Israeli couple and about ten Japanese.

The Tibetans were gone first, the Europeans were gone already, and nearly all the Israelis were gone too.

The only ones left were the Japanese, the ones with the thinnest sense of danger — a stupid and thoroughly uncool way to end up.
