## Life in Goa

Life in Goa was a tropical paradise, with delicious ingredients piled high everywhere and prices that were startlingly cheap.

The market overflowed with just-caught seafood, and in town you could get even beef and pork, which are quite rare in India.

We made the most of it and spent our days eating well.

In town, beef ran about a hundred yen a kilo (2.2 pounds) — around eighty cents.

The meat was lean and tough and hard to eat, but hammering away at it with a stick softened it up, so we lived on steak.

The seafood was fresh too, and we had a fine time with it: ikameshi, squid stuffed with rice; oyster curry; fish barbecued out in the yard.

Shopping trips turned into riding the motorcycle all over town, getting lost in the jungle, and exploring old ruins.

And of course, since the village sat along the beach, we swam in the sea, and we swam in a lake out in the jungle as well.

Every other day or so we would go over to a friend's house to cook together, or head out to a restaurant, or ride the motorcycles a long way out to swim at some country beach. Days in a tropical paradise.

They were fun, rich days, but in a way it's a commonplace story, not particularly worth putting in a travelogue.

## Goa's Traveler Culture

Still, if I'm going to write a travelogue, what I want to write about is the traveler's world, the part of it that is nothing like ordinary life.

Goa's traveler culture really is peculiar and fascinating, and there is nothing else like it anywhere.

There is nothing to compare it to, which makes it hard to get across to anyone who has not actually lived it — but getting it across is the reason this travelogue exists, so I'll do my best to put it into words.

The Goa traveler culture I mean here is that of the twenty thousand or so travelers (the number as of 2000) who gathered on about fourteen kilometers (nine miles) of beach — a tiny sliver of the state of Goa, which itself ranks among the smallest of India's many states.

Goa has long been called the last paradise a traveler arrives at, and in the 1960s the ones who came over from America and the ones who had crossed the Eurasian landmass from Europe gathered there and built a paradise of their own.

Even after they leave Goa and travel elsewhere, in the end they come back to Goa.

That's because, for a traveler, India is something like a home you return to again and again, and Goa above all is one of the few homes where travelers who have fallen clean out of society can be themselves.

Once a traveler has known Goa deeply, his consciousness has expanded to match the degree of freedom there, and after that, anywhere outside Goa feels as cramped as being inside a prison.
