## A Multinational Household

Our communal life began with a nice round number: ten people.

The largest group, a proud 30 percent of the total, was our Japanese team: me, I-chan, and Y.

Tied for second, with two members each, were the Moroccans, F and A-kun, and the English, Gandalf and R.

That left one French, one German, and one Australian.

## Nationality

Gandalf's nationality had been a mystery all along, but now, at last, I found out what it was.

Among seasoned, hippie-ish travelers, there's an unspoken rule of manners: you don't ask people what country they're from.

Most of the time you can tell someone's race from their looks or their accent anyway, but by choosing not to ask, you show that you want to connect as one human being to another, whatever your race.

That was why, until now, I had never known Gandalf's nationality.

## The Language Barrier

The official languages of Morocco are Moroccan and French, but among us, English was the common language.

Three of us were native English speakers, and F from Morocco, Y from Japan, and M from Germany all spoke it fluently.

A-kun, the other Moroccan, had the English of a Japanese elementary school kid, though, and the French member, C, and I were at about the level of Japanese junior high students.

I-chan spoke high-school-level English, which put her miles ahead of the rest of our can't-speak-English crowd.

She was sociable by nature and loved to talk, and with English as the only way to talk to anyone, her English was getting better by the day.

She had actually studied in school and had gone to an English conversation school, so she had solid basics, and that let her English take off.

C, A-kun, and I had no such basics, so our language skills were slow to improve.

We still very much wanted to communicate, though, so we learned to do it with gestures and facial expressions.

The native speakers, for their part, learned to talk slowly and in simple words so they would be understood.

Care for one another is what finds the middle ground.
