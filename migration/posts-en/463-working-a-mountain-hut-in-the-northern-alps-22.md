## Professionalism

J's professionalism went all the way down.

Part of that came from his serious training as a chef, and part of it, it seemed, came from his own sense of aesthetics.

That sense of aesthetics carried over into the way he ran the hut.

Most people picture a mountain hut as a rough, spartan place to sleep in harsh surroundings, but ours was different.

Before dinner, everyone was poured a glass of white wine as an aperitif, and the meal began with some breezy banter from J, the manager.

The food was nothing like the spartan mountain-hut image. What came out was on par with a full-course French dinner.

But serving a full French course near a summit 2,800 meters (9,200 feet) up takes no ordinary effort.

That was where J's professionalism made up the difference.

## Going the Extra Mile

When supplies come up only once every two weeks, a mountain hut can only do so much to give climbers fresh ingredients.

It also has to get by on a limited budget.

So J raised the quality of the meals with clever use of frozen food and a cook's bag of tricks.

He went over every ingredient, chose with care, and turned out the best food possible.

That was J's creed in action: "Making the best food in the most limited conditions is what makes you the best chef."

All that extra effort got the hut talked about among climbers. Guests brought more guests, repeat visitors multiplied, and plenty of climbers came back up every year.

## A Virtuous Circle

As the hut's image went up dramatically, the image of the company's huts as a whole went up with it.

That put this hut at the very top of the company's image strategy.

The president, a management buff, started giving the hut a bigger budget as a matter of business strategy, and the food got even better.

Climbers longed to stay there, and people who came to work in the mountains dreamed of working there.

And I had been dropped into a place like that simply because it suited the staffing plans. I must have been pretty lucky.
