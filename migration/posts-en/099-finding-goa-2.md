# Living Together

When I described my cheap-room setup to Y, the man I had met at the restaurant, the price of 150 yen a day — about a dollar twenty — won him over, and by the next day he was moving in.

Neither of us had anything to do, so this was going to be fun.

Y traveled as light as I did, and he settled into the room next door.

That was the beginning of what became a "long-lasting friendship," and of a stretch of days spent doing nothing but having fun.

We swam in the sea and cooked our own meals and had a good time of it, but what took up the most time at that guesthouse was cards.

We both fancied ourselves good at cards and worked through all sorts of games, but Y almost never beat me.

It is not for me to say, but my head has always been quick, and I rarely lose at cards or games of that kind.

On top of that, Daifugō — the Japanese card game — and poker were what I had spent nearly all my free time on in middle school. We weren't in the same league.

Y was a former banchō, the tough kid who ran his school, and a former biker-gang member, these days training in a fighting style called Pancrase: a hard-looking man from the film business, with a firm principle of meeting any contest head-on.

Unable to win at Daifugō no matter what he tried, he grew frustrated and challenged me in every situation imaginable, but nothing he did could touch my years at the game, and his frustration kept building.

He was twenty-nine, a muscle-packed industry guy, a former banchō with all kinds of life behind him; I was a brat with no experience of the world at all, a skinny, formerly bullied movie geek — I think that gap made it sting that much more.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog99-1.jpg)

We kept playing for fun, with very small sums riding on the games, but Y hated losing and kept coming back at me, and it came out looking like I was fleecing him — in the end he covered two weeks of my rent.

For him, with money to spare, it was nothing, but for me, traveling on almost nothing, two weeks of rent was a lot, and it converted straight into two weeks of vacation.

# Goans

One day, wandering through the street-stall market that had gone up in the village along the beach, I met a Japanese woman, K.

She had come to Goa the year before to have a good time, met a local Goan man, started seeing him, and planned to marry him before long.

She was living now in a quiet patch of woods near Calangute beach, and she invited us to come by if we felt like it.

The next day we went over to her place.

Y, the older one with plenty of social experience, brought a small gift along.

Y, seeing me empty-handed, set about teaching me the basic courtesy of dealing with people, and I explained that I came with nothing because, the way I saw it, showing up and letting people enjoy my company was gift enough.

Now that I'm somewhat more grown up, showing up empty-handed in a situation like that would be close to unthinkable, but back then I was a sheltered youngest child, unconsciously drawing on the privilege of being the youngest person in any room.

Maximum use of two sayings: travel while you're young, and shame on the road is shame you leave behind.

At K's place, besides her fiancé, there was a Japanese friend of hers and, on top of that, the friend's Goan fiancé.

She invites a friend out to her house in Goa, and the friend goes and finds a husband of her own.

K was entertaining to listen to, and though she was only twenty-three she had already lived an interesting life; she had the presence of a mother running a big household.

She had been born into the household of the Shinto priest of the one shrine on an island in Kyushu, an island where surfers gather.

Maybe it was the way she grew up, but she started surfing young and spent much of her time around surfers.

Sharing the culture of surfers who lived free, she started traveling young, met her husband in Goa the year before, and would be married soon.

"That's impressive, at your age," I said, younger still myself.

"There are decisions you can only make because you're young," said Y.

Y was no match for me at cards, but he had a wealth of life experience, and I learned a great deal from him.

# A Marriage Without Love?

A little rude to say, a little worrying, and just a little funny: T, the friend who had come to visit and found herself a husband.

You could see the love between K and her husband, but between T and her Indian man there was no deep love and no trust, and that was visible even to me, who had never been in a relationship.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog99-2.jpg)

Both Y and I were thinking (oh, this one's no good), but with things already as far along as marriage, there was no butting in.

Back in Japan she had been a girl devoted to following a British band around, and she probably had almost no experience with romance.

Hearing that her friend had met an Indian husband, she may have figured she could meet someone wonderful too.

The same went for the man she was marrying: hearing that K's husband had met a Japanese woman, he thought, I imagine, that he could marry a Japanese woman himself and get an easy, wealthy life out of it.

Then T showed up, and the two sets of needs matched up beautifully.

It was plain that the man had no interest in T, plain that there was no love between them, but things had already developed to a point where there was no turning back.

An awkward situation any way you looked at it.

Back at the guesthouse afterward, Y and I talked it over in low voices: that one was bad.

We agreed with each other not to poke at this any further.
