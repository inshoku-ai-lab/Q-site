# Arriving in Varanasi

The train pulled into Varanasi a little behind schedule.

I had heard that Indian trains can run enormously late, so getting off with only a small delay was something to be grateful for.

Getting off the train and coming into the station, I found the same scene I had watched in Calcutta playing out all over again.

The one difference was that the moment I stepped down, the baggage carriers they call porters came swarming in.

They make their living hauling travelers' luggage for tips.

All I had was a small backpack, so I turned them down and started walking.

The next thing to come at me, from the instant I left the station, was the taxi drivers' battle for customers.

Five or so older guys pushing hard — where are you going, I'll make it cheap.

None of them actually grabbed my arm, but at that rate I could easily have been abducted.

![A green and yellow auto-rickshaw parked at a kerb with a passenger's bare legs stretched out of the side](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog93-1.jpg)

Getting into one of those taxis looked likely to end badly, so I decided to go on my own feet.

I had slept well in the sleeper car, my pack was not that heavy, I had the stamina for a walk, and the sky was gray but showed no sign of rain.

# Walking Varanasi

Varanasi, also called Benares, has been revered since ancient times as one of the greatest holy places in India.

Why it is holy, though, is not settled; there are several theories.

The usual one is that the sacred Ganges runs from northwest to southeast along its whole length, and only at Varanasi does it bend around and head from south to north.

Why a bend in a river should be holy is beyond me, but since everyone, without exception, worships the place, no one disputes that Varanasi is a holy city.

The poverty in Calcutta had shocked me, but Varanasi's was equal to it, or beyond it.

Taking the city as a whole, Varanasi is probably the poorer of the two.

Unlike Calcutta, where Western city culture had made its way in, Varanasi's India-ness ran much higher.

For the amount of ground it covers, there were several times more sacred cows, which may be the truest measure of how holy Varanasi is.

# Beggars

Relative to the size of the city, the number of beggars also looked higher than in Calcutta.

They would chase after me, the foreigner, shouting "baksheesh, baksheesh."

Baksheesh means alms.

The thinking in Indian culture is that the existence of beggars is what lets you perform the good deed of giving alms to the poor, and through that build up merit and live a happier life in the next one.

So they never thank you for the money or the food; the arrangement is that the giver is the one who ought to be grateful.

![A person sitting with bandaged feet and cupped hands beside a cardboard sign reading ONCE I WAS LIKE YOU](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog93-2.jpg)

Most of them are born into the beggar caste — a rank within the hereditary status system — and as long as they stay Hindu, apparently, there is no getting out of it.

In practice they could walk away at any time, of course, but they believe they cannot, and so they go on begging for the rest of their lives.

To get out of those low castes a lot of people convert to Buddhism, it seems; at one point it grew into a large movement and a considerable number changed religion.

# The Conflict

Having grown up inside Japanese culture, I had a deep resistance to the idea of the caste system, and to the idea of begging for an entire lifetime, and I stubbornly refused to hand over money.

Instead, whenever I was eating something or had food on me, I made a point of giving food away.

But the beggars outnumbered whatever food I was carrying by an overwhelming margin, and giving to everyone who came would have left me stripped bare in under ten minutes.

For a traveler coming to India from a developed country, this is the first trial everyone passes through: how to accept being on the rich side of an overwhelming gap between rich and poor.

People who decide to travel in India tend, to one degree or another, to be skeptical of Western civilization and materialism, and plenty of them hold a grudge against the rich.

And now they, and I, are the ones standing in the position of the overwhelmingly rich, the top one percent.

Some people end up facing the contradictions they carry, and the ones their society carries, and are plagued by a powerful inner conflict.

Most who travel India come through that conflict, and each of them finds, in their own way, where they stand.

That intensity is part of what makes traveling in India interesting.
