# Earliest Memory

My earliest memory is from when the six of us lived together: my father, my mother, my paternal grandmother, my older brother, my older sister, and me, the youngest.

It was a two-story house, and I remember the plaster walls used to flake off easily.

I was very attached to my grandmother, doing everything together with her.

The exception was at night — unless I was with my mother, I got too anxious to sleep.

Peaceful days.

I'd go for walks with my grandmother, or play house with a girl from the neighborhood.

Then one day, out of nowhere, it was decided that my mother and I would move out, just the two of us.

Too young to understand anything, I was simply happy about moving — the first time such a thing had happened in my life.

But after that move, the whole family never lived together again.

Too young to find it strange that my family had disappeared and my life had changed, I simply accepted it as it was, and the days went by.

# Family Drama

Unbeknownst to my oblivious younger self, a family drama had been unfolding.

My story in this life begins in a household that was lower working class through and through — both my father and mother had grown up in a doya-gai, a day-laborers' slum, in Osaka.

Apparently my father's whole family and my mother's whole family lived in the same neighborhood, and everyone knew each other well.

My parents happened to run into each other again as adults, fell in love, started dating, and got married.

After that they lived together with my father's mother, had three children, and built a happy household.

But the happy marriage lasted only the first few years, it seems, before it gradually fell apart.

My father got pulled into gambling — an entirely ordinary pastime in the environment he'd grown up in.

Horse racing, keirin (track cycling), boat racing, mahjong.

He drove a taxi for a living, which gave him plenty of freedom with his schedule. Taking advantage of the fact that my mother wouldn't find out, he threw his work aside and sank all the way into gambling, until eventually he was buried in debt, or so I'm told.

And my grandmother, the kind woman I loved so much, was apparently quite hard on my mother, her daughter-in-law.

The usual mother-in-law and daughter-in-law trouble.

Worn down by her husband's gambling addiction and her mother-in-law's bullying, my mother went looking for somewhere to escape to.

What she found was the Jehovah's Witnesses, the religion her older sister was a devoted believer in, and my mother let herself be drawn in, becoming a believer herself.

A gambler husband buried in debt, a mother-in-law who doted on her son and was hard on her daughter-in-law, and a wife devoted to a shady cult — there was no way that combination was going to work out, and in the end they got divorced.

That was the move I mentioned above.

# Kindergarten

After the divorce, my mother needed to work, so she sent me to kindergarten.

I still remember the first day I went.

Just like with the move, my mother may not have explained anything to me about kindergarten beforehand.

Shocked at being separated from every single member of my family for the first time in my life, I cried the whole day at kindergarten.

I think I cried like that every day for the first several days.

Looking back now, I sometimes wonder if it left me with real trauma.

At first my eyes were swollen from crying, but I settled in quickly, and I think my days there became happy ones.

Nothing else about my life as a kindergartner is worth mentioning in particular.

# Another Move, and Then Religion

After attending kindergarten for a while, we moved again.

The thinking was that my older sister, approaching adolescence, would be better off living with our mother, so she joined our household, and the three of us began living together in a small danchi, a public housing complex.

With this new life, the whole family started attending Jehovah's Witness meetings in earnest.

Every time we went to a meeting, my mother's religious fervor intensified.

Before long, whenever we had free time, we were out going door to door in the neighborhood doing field service, trying to bring people into the Jehovah's Witnesses.

A large part of our time came to be spent on religious activity, and little by little religion became the center of our lives.

We went to meetings that took about three hours round trip, three times a week, plus about three hours of field service twice a week.

On top of that there was preparation and review, so it wouldn't be an exaggeration to say that practically all of our time was spent on religion.

Of course, I wasn't doing any of it out of my own choice — I was just a small child tagging along after my mother.

This was probably around when I was five.

After that, I would go on with the religious activity for twelve whole years, until I left the Jehovah's Witnesses at seventeen.
