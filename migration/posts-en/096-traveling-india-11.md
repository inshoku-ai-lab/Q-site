# The Developed World

Coming from Varanasi, the first thing that surprised me about Pune was how developed the city was.

It was about as modern as Japan.

Along paved roads, concrete high-rise housing blocks stood in rows.

The streets weren't organic and self-generated the way Varanasi's were; they felt built out along a city plan drawn up by the government.

There were none of the hand-pulled rickshaws of Calcutta, none of the bicycle-powered cycle rickshaws of Varanasi; what Pune had were auto-rickshaws, run on motors and gasoline.

There was no cheap way to get anywhere, as if the city had no poor people in it.

I went looking for a room, but there seemed to be no cheap-hotel quarter here, and every hotel cost real money.

The unmistakable India-ness I had seen in Calcutta and Varanasi was already gone, and maybe cheap rooms simply didn't exist here.

With no other option, I paid several times what a room in Varanasi cost and stayed at a hotel near the station.

# The Osho Ashram

Thinking that unless I found a cheap room I couldn't stay long in this city, I headed for a place that taught yoga.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog96-1.jpg)

It turned out to be an ashram — a place set up for spiritual practice — founded by a man called Osho, and apparently they taught yoga and ran all sorts of workshops there.

To get inside, you had to buy a maroon-colored garment and wear it.

On top of that, they said you couldn't get in unless you took a blood test and proved you were negative for AIDS.

Why any of that was necessary in order to learn yoga I had no idea, but I hadn't come this far to turn back, so I did as I was told.

They drew blood with a needle and tested it, and I put on the brand-new maroon robe they issued me.

Looking in the mirror at myself as a shaven-headed young Asian man, I did not look entirely unlike a Tibetan monk in training.

A day there cost about a thousand yen — around eight dollars — and you had to buy a ticket for a minimum three-day stay.

The cost of the robe, the cost of the needle, the daily entry fee.

Once inside, apparently there were meal charges and fees for the special workshops on top of that.

A single day in Pune burned through what several days in Varanasi had cost.

# Paradise

Inside the ashram it was another world from the one outside, and the view that opened up was something like paradise.

Beautiful gardens spread out, the flowers in full bloom.

There was a small pond, and a garden lined with trees, and people were meditating at the water's edge or relaxing in the shade.

Neat, bright-eyed Westerners and Indians were laughing together, having a good time.

In the restaurant you ate fresh salad from the buffet and drank fresh juice.

The exact opposite of the brutal poverty and filth I had been through in Calcutta and Varanasi — it was precisely what I wanted right then.

I'd had my fill of Varanasi's thick India-ness.

The time I spent in the ashram was pleasant, but every single thing cost a decent amount of money, and traveling as broke as I was, I could never quite relax.

Barely arrived in Pune, I was already thinking about the next destination.

Measured against prices in the developed world, the ashram gave you high-quality service for far less, so for anyone with money to spare I think it's a good choice.

# Music and Meditation

I wanted to learn yoga, but there was no way I could afford it, so I gave up.

One workshop cost about as much as two weeks of living in Varanasi.

Instead I joined a free workshop where you meditated while dancing to music.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog96-2.jpg)

In a hall like a small gymnasium, about ten of us sat in a circle, and the workshop began there, eyes closed.

If you felt like moving or dancing, you were free to move however the mood took you.

A group of hippie-looking men and women, around sixty, started playing their instruments live, and the music felt good.

They were acoustic instruments, keeping a slow but easy rhythm, and with my eyes closed, swaying my body along with the sound, I felt like I was being carried somewhere.

The music started slow and quiet and gradually built, and by about thirty minutes in everyone was swaying hard and dancing.

They played well, well enough to call to mind the seventies German rock scene I love.

Just getting to hear this music made me think that maybe coming here had been worth it.

Swaying to music in a meditative state meant something completely different from dancing hard all night at a techno club in the city, and I was glad to be feeling something I had never felt before.
