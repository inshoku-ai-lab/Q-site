## Escaping the Rainy Season

In India the whole summer is the rainy season.

The timing shifts a little from region to region, but in summer the rain is hard to get away from.

All of that holds except for one place: the Leh–Ladakh region, at the very top of India.

Ladakh sits on the north side of the Himalayas, out of reach of the monsoon that covers the rest of Asia.

Rain almost never falls there, but it lies at an altitude of 3,500 meters (11,500 feet) and runs about twenty degrees Celsius colder than the plains — a gap of some thirty-five degrees Fahrenheit — so as a rule you can only go in summer.

From fall through spring, the pass roads, which climb above 5,000 meters (16,400 feet), are closed by heavy snow.

There are only two main overland routes into Ladakh: one that comes up from Manali in the south and crosses the Himalayas, and one that goes around them and passes through Kashmir, a conflict zone.

Not many people, obviously, want to travel through a conflict zone, so almost everyone goes to Ladakh by way of Manali, in the summer.

## Setting Out

Several of us in the same circle of friends were planning to dodge the rainy season and go to Ladakh, and it turned into: we may as well all go together.

We booked a bus, five friends bound for Ladakh.

A long-distance bus trip, two days and one night.

Not a two-day round trip — two solid days of moving, on a bus.

The part where we would cross a 5,000-meter pass brought back the trip to Tibet.

We claimed the back row of the big bus and packed ourselves in together.

That was when N — the Section Chief, our senior on the road — helped himself to the most comfortable seat, as though it were the most natural thing in the world.

I was young then, and watching him I decided that a traveler needed to be exactly that selfish, that greedy, so I went and staked out another of the open seats.

The one who paid for it was C, who had the soul of a samurai.

He took it meekly, without a word of complaint.

Dead center of the back row — the least stable seat there is, with nothing to grab when the bus lurched — was where he would spend three days.

I felt bad about it, and chose to stay greedy anyway.

The influence an older traveler has on a young one is a frightening thing.

I met all kinds of interesting people and took a lot of good from them, and I think I took about as much bad.

At twenty, I had no way of telling one from the other.

Nothing but to take it all in exactly as it came.
