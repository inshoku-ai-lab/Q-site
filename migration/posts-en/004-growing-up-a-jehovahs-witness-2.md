# A Rare Experience

This is a firsthand report on twelve years of growing up as a Jehovah's Witness child, an experience rare enough to rival the various adventures I would go on as an adult, so I want to write about it in a little more detail.

Please be careful not to get caught up in criticism or rejection, and read it from an anthropological angle, the way you would read a report by someone who infiltrated some primitive tribe.

If the details of the Jehovah's Witnesses do not interest you, feel free to skip ahead to the next entry, about facing the harshness of society in my elementary school years.

# The Way of Life of the Jehovah's Witnesses

I do not know how it is now, but around thirty years ago, when I was living as a Jehovah's Witness, the membership was said to be a hundred thousand across all of Japan.

The number was probably inflated, but even so, I think it is a fact that a fairly large number of people were in it without anyone noticing.

It was a worldwide organization, with branches in each country and each region, and the regions were subdivided very finely.

The smallest unit was called a "group," about fifteen people.

Several groups together made up a "congregation" of around a hundred.

# Tuesday Meeting

Every Tuesday night (the day and time varied by congregation), we gathered as a "group" at a private home a few minutes' walk away and held a "meeting."

There were times when our own house was the meeting place.

Before going to a meeting, you changed into dress clothes, a white dress shirt and slacks, and headed out.

It began with a few minutes of "prayer," and for an hour we studied the publications of the "Society" (not "the church").

Questions and answers went back and forth, and you raised your hand and answered with what you had prepared in advance. That act was called giving "comments."

Children were encouraged to raise their hands and give "comments," and were praised when they got it right.

At the end there was another "prayer," and the meeting was closed.

Very occasionally, after the meeting, there was socializing with the other "brothers and sisters," called "fellowship."

Drinking tea, eating sweets.

# Thursday Meeting

Every Thursday, all hundred or so members of the "congregation" gathered at the "Kingdom Hall," about ten minutes from my house by bicycle, and held a "meeting."

Thursdays began with a "song of praise."

Songs with lovely melodies and lyrics meant to stir faith.

The songs were the same all over the world, with each country given a fitting translation.

We studied the Society's publications for about two hours, then sang another "song of praise" at the end and closed with prayer.

After that came about thirty minutes of "fellowship" with the "brothers and sisters" of the "congregation," and then home.

The Sunday daytime meeting each week was much the same as the Thursday one.

Among the Jehovah's Witnesses, attending these meetings was treated as the highest priority, and plans like travel were made only so far as they would not interfere with them.

My family was dirt poor, so there was no such thing as going on a trip, but with a meeting every Sunday, I never had any sense of enjoying a weekend in the first place.

# Field Service

Apart from these meetings, there was also a house-to-house visiting activity called "field service," which was pursued vigorously.

For us, as "obedient servants of God" sent to save the "lost sheep" (ordinary citizens) who had been deceived by the Devil, "field service" was one of the important acts.

If we do not convey God's truth, our neighbors will go on being deceived by the Devil and will lose their chance to live forever in "the earthly Paradise."

Out of love for our neighbors, let us save them, for their own good...

This "field service" was every day, three hours, from nine in the morning until noon.

Not once a week; every day.

Several of us gathered at a specific meeting point within the territory and visited each house based on a detailed map.

Detailed information was shared about who had shown interest, who had gotten angry, which houses had been visited often.

We gathered in dress clothes, walked from house to house, and conveyed the truth as we went.

In practical terms, people who believed the visitors' words and became Jehovah's Witnesses were extremely rare, about two a year in our congregation, so the thousands of hours the servants spent visiting were in vain.

Or perhaps it is a great love, the idea that it is enough to save one person even if it takes thousands of hours.

With these meetings and field service at its core, the activity of the Jehovah's Witnesses moved forward.

People who did it seriously filled every free hour of daily life with meetings, field service, and the schedule of preparation for them, so there was almost no time left to spend on anything else.

You could call it quite an ascetic discipline.
