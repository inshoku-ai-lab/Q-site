# A Birthday Party

The guesthouse we were staying in held a fair number of travelers.

Fifty or so, all told.

About thirty percent of them were Japanese, which made us the biggest group in the place.

There were all kinds of Japanese travelers with all kinds of personalities, and the conversations were lively.

By the time everyone had gotten friendly and we were going out sightseeing and eating together, my twentieth birthday came around.

I had told them I wanted to travel before I turned twenty — the age Japan counts you as an adult — and so, one thing leading to another, they decided to celebrate it for me.

I had grown up in a Jehovah's Witness household, where the rule was that nothing but Jehovah God gets celebrated, and I had never once celebrated my own birthday or gone to anyone else's birthday party.

Having a birthday celebrated for the first time in my life left me shy and a little embarrassed, and glad.

The plan was to gather that night in our dormitory room — the shared one — and hold the party there.

It was meant to be a few people at first, but the guests called in more guests, and in the end fifteen Japanese plus the German and the Mexican staying in the same room — seventeen of us — crowded in, and it turned into quite a party.

# The Same Age

Everyone brought something: Lhasa Beer, brewed right there in the city, and momos, the local dumplings.

We picked at the momos and drank.

Tibet sits high enough that the drink hits fast, and it takes very little to get drunk.

One of the people who came was a boy exactly my age.

He was another exchange student in China, apparently, over on summer break with Japanese friends from his university.

The older friends had brought him along, or he had tagged after them, and he was terrified of this unfamiliar, foreign world.

He seemed unable to stop worrying about how little confidence he had in his Chinese, and he had settled completely into the role of the group's timid little brother.

They teased him about it by holding me up beside him, the same age, and it left me with a strange discomfort I could not name.

# The Line

The altitude did its work on the beer, and the talk came easily.

A few hours into the party, with everything really picking up, one of my travel companions, a Beijing exchange student, crossed a line.

His ancestors were some daimyo or other, one of the old feudal lords, and the drink carried him into a long me-me-me boast.

The endless speech wore out a burly literature major who lived in Okinawa, and when he told him that was enough, the two drunks started arguing.

That it was my birthday party made no difference to the argument, which went on until there was nothing to be done, and the party broke up in an awkward mood.

It ended badly, but I was glad to have had the first birthday party of my life somewhere as unusual as Tibet, and I came away having met other travelers and made connections.

# Travel Companions

My travel companions, the Beijing students, were going back to Beijing in a few days, but I wanted to keep going as I was, on to India.

Traveling alone made me a little uneasy, though.

There were other travelers with the same feeling, meaning to take the same route, and the birthday party was where I met them.

The roads out of Tibet toward Nepal were in terrible shape and nothing about them was certain, so there was no telling who would end up traveling with whom, but knowing there were other Japanese headed the same way was a comfort.

My new travel companions were not on a student's summer vacation; they had time to spare, and their sense of what traveling meant matched mine.

After my Beijing student friends went home, I started spending my time with the new ones.
