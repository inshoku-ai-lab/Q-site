# The Gray City

I checked out of my cheap room in Shanghai and set off on foot toward the center of the city.

The plan was to get a train ticket and head inland.

As I got closer to the high-rise district downtown, I noticed that almost every building was bare concrete.

Until the day before I had been down near the port, where rows of bare concrete buildings had seemed unremarkable enough, but among the towers of the busy downtown the strangeness of it exploded.

A good number of people were out striding the streets — no question this was a big city.

I had no idea how close to the center I actually was, but eighty percent of the buildings in front of me were still raw concrete.

Construction finished, construction abandoned halfway, or the intended design from the start?

Those blocks of concrete stood there as if that were the most ordinary thing in the world, and the sight shook me.

So this was what communism was?

I had never had the slightest interest in politics and barely understood what communism even meant, but the gray buildings kept shoving an absolute wrongness in my face.

A building works perfectly well without paint on its outer walls, true, but when almost an entire city is gray, that is a different order of problem.

Complaining would change nothing, but standing there, I felt the fear of communism.

# A Kind, Shady Man

On my way to the station to buy a ticket into the middle of the continent, a shady-looking man came up to me near the entrance.

He could apparently speak English.

Since I couldn't speak English, his speaking it didn't help much, but I could still make out words like "bus," "train," and "ticket."

I had no idea who he was, but he seemed to be either trying to help me or trying to con me.

Suspicious as I was, it felt good to be communicating with another person.

Either way I had no idea where anything was, so I decided to go along with him while staying wary, and to walk away the moment things started to smell wrong.

I showed him my map and got across that I wanted to head west and then go to Tibet.

He pointed at the map and told me to go to a city called Xi'an.

From there, apparently, you could get to Tibet.

He came with me all the way to the ticket window and helped me buy the ticket.

Reserved seats came in two kinds, hard seat and soft seat, and I took the soft seat, on the grounds that it sounded softer.

Nothing about it felt like a rip-off or a scam.

Afterward, to thank him, I took him to a street stall, where we ate together and drank beer and I paid for it.

Ordering on my own tended to produce strange results, so having help with it was a relief.

When we finished eating, he showed me which way to walk, and we parted.

In the end I think he may simply have been a kind person, or maybe he only wanted a beer, or maybe he had been working up to something worse — there is no way to know.

Looking back now, maybe the ticket seller had a marked-up ticket set aside in advance and sold me that one.

# The Department Store

There was still time before the train left, so I decided to wander around the city.

In China, free hot water is handed out in thermoses everywhere — guesthouses, trains, buses — so I decided to buy loose tea leaves and a bottle.

I stepped into an enormous department store of bare concrete.

Contrary to the plain exterior, the inside felt neat and tidy.

A beautiful young woman sat at the counter, slumped and visibly uninterested in doing anything at all.

For how big the place was, there was little variety to the goods, and the same item was piled up in mountains and sold that way.

Something like a supermarket was attached to it, so I decided to stock up on food to eat on the train.

Walking around looking for tea leaves, I came to a shelf of bottled tea.

Here too, one single kind of drink took up a huge amount of space, and there was little else.

The tea on sale was oolong.

China, of course — oolong in the front row, exactly as expected.

Looking closer, there seemed to be two kinds, and one of them carried the character 糖 — sugar.

So there was unsweetened oolong and sweet oolong with sugar in it.

I take sugar in my black tea myself, and yet somewhere in the back of my mind I had decided that putting sugar in oolong was wrong.

And here, in China, the home of oolong tea, sweetened oolong sat in the front row of the supermarket shelf.

A trivial thing, but culture shock of that kind left a big mark on me.
