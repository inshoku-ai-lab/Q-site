# House Party

A while after I arrived in Peru, my sister would take me along now and then to gatherings of the local Jehovah's Witnesses.

Not a religious thing — a house party.

The Peruvian Witnesses had gotten together, put on music, and were dancing.

By the standards of Japanese Jehovah's Witnesses, music carries too much worldly influence and is better left unheard, and dancing in front of other people is out of the question, so watching them dance was a bit of a culture shock.

At first I held back, watching from off to the side.

"You'd never dance if it weren't for something like this, so why not dance while you can?"

my sister said, and spurred on by her words, I decided to give it a try.

There had been a few times in gym class at school when we were made to dance, but this was the first time in my life that I danced to music of my own free will.

![Two figures in hats silhouetted against a low sun, one crouching with an arm outstretched](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog21-1.jpg)

It was a low-crouching, knee-swiveling sort of dance, something like the Twist, as I recall.

The one that was popular in Peru at the time.

Embarrassment made me stop after about ten minutes, but even so, getting over the shame of expressing myself in front of other people was a big step.

Back then I could not have imagined that dancing would ever be part of my life.

# Friends My Own Age

At this party, through my sister's introduction, I got to know two boys my own age.

Perhaps she had asked them to keep me company.

We could not understand each other's words, of course, but with gestures we managed to communicate somehow.

The Peruvian kids my age struck me as more unaffected, purer, warmer, and friendlier than my classmates back in Japan.

What made me envious was that in Peruvian society, being a Jehovah's Witness was nothing special.

With Christian culture firmly rooted there, and with all sorts of Christian offshoots that had merged with indigenous religion, perhaps the Jehovah's Witnesses were seen as just one more branch of Christianity.

There was no air of heresy or cultishness about them at all.

The Peruvian Witnesses also had far looser rules than the Japanese ones, and seemed woven into daily life.

In Japan the standard formal dress was a white dress shirt and a red tie for everyone, but here the shirts were more colorful, and the ties were up to the individual.

I met these two friends several more times after that, and little by little we opened up to each other.

One time they took me on a small adventure to a river nearby, and on the way back we ate fish at a restaurant.

For me, having only just escaped the darkness, healthy human relationships felt good.

# A Peruvian-Style Wedding

As the end of the year drew closer, so did the date of my sister's wedding.

She was busy with the preparations.

My mother and my aunt were coming from Japan right before the ceremony, so my sister had to arrange that as well, and at the same time settle into life in a new country and get along with her husband's family.

On top of all that she was looking after me while preparing a wedding in a country she barely knew, so of course she had her hands full.

Around the time the preparations were coming together, my mother and aunt arrived all the way from Japan.

My brother begged off, saying Peru was too far, and my uncle declined because he was afraid of planes.

For me it was a reunion with my mother after a long time, but nothing in particular had changed.

The wedding began a few days after they arrived.

Because my aunt's time was limited, the schedule was packed tight.

![A bride holding a pink and white bouquet as guests throw petals and rice around her](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog21-2.jpg)

My sister's wedding took place over several days.

Peruvian style, I suppose.

What Japan would call the reception was held at a different house each day.

Each reception was small and intimate, and every household served up the dish it was proud of.

One house slaughtered a turkey they had set aside for the day and made a magnificent feast out of it.

Getting from house to house meant about fifteen people standing in the back of a small flatbed truck and being hauled along, and my aunt grumbled, "In Japan this is how we transport pigs," but I was delighted by a wild experience I could never have had in Japan.

The days-long wedding went off without a hitch, and the two families were happily joined together.

Two people born into completely different countries and cultures getting married — that is no small thing.
