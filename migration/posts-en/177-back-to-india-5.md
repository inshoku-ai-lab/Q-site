## The Night Bus

I stayed only one night in Delhi, and on the evening of the second day I got on a night bus for Manali, a hill town in the Himalayas where people go to escape the heat, and the destination for this trip.

Vashisht, a village a little way out from Manali, had hot springs, and from what I heard a lot of travelers settled in there and stayed for a long time.

Several friends from my time in Goa had already gone up there, waving me on to hurry and come join them.

I had heard no end of good things — how wonderful the hot springs were, how kind the local people were, how beautiful the mountains, how good the water and the food — so I was looking forward to going.

In the evening I went to the long-distance bus station and climbed aboard.

What I really wanted was to travel by train, but Manali sits in the mountains with no rail line running to it, and the only way there is by road.

Nobody could have called the ride comfortable, and although the bus ran through the night, there were no sleeper berths.

It meant sitting upright in a cramped seat and getting through the night that way — punishing travel, and very Indian.

The bus ran out through a Delhi lit orange by its streetlights and headed north toward the Himalayan mountains.

## The Himalayas

Between the rough ride and the miserable seat there was no real sleep, and I woke at every toilet stop as we went on toward Manali.

This was July, the rainy season in India.

In Delhi the rain had been starting and stopping and the heat and humidity left everything steaming, but as we climbed into the mountains the air turned sharp, and stepping off the bus at night was cold.

The winding mountain road nearly made me carsick, but watching the mountain scenery brighten as the night gave way to morning got me through it.

In the morning the bus passed through towns in the Himalayas.

Middle-aged women in the traditional dress of the hill tribes sat out on the street selling vegetables.

While we were stopped at a bus stop in one town, a few boys came leaping onto the bus.

They had ice-cold bottles of water and bags of snacks in their hands, and each of them pushed to sell ahead of the others.

Next came an older guy with chai, and last of all an old woman with bananas.

The law didn't function all that well in India, so depending on where you stood in the caste system, child labor was an ordinary thing and retirement age meant nothing.

Everyone who could work worked for their keep, and within a family people held each other up.

India had a very simple law of living things.
