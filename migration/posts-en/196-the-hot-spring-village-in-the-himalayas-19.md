## All Sorts of Travelers

Vashisht was a hidden corner known only to those in the know, so interesting travelers came through on a regular basis.

Back then I couldn't speak a word of English, so the only travelers I met were Japanese, but even among them there were plenty of characters, and I took a lot from them.

K had been on the road longer than anyone I had met up to that point, and it opened my eyes — you could live like this too.

His style of travel was very simple: above all, to keep his days slow and settled.

Some years back he had worked a short, concentrated stretch in a factory in Japan and saved a large sum.

Then, as luck would have it, he changed all of it into dollars at a moment when the yen had shot up, and put that into traveler's checks.

Traveler's checks were the standard way travelers held their money in those days; people liked that they could be reissued if you lost them, and that the exchange rate on them ran a little better than cash.

With that money behind him, he lived a quiet, inwardly rich life deep in the mountains of India.

He had already been living that way for about five years, and the simplicity and the composure of it were something to see.

In one sense it was a shut-in's life, only one he took full responsibility for, but it also looked like a yogi's life of seclusion.

## Broke Travelers

Long-term backpackers, as a rule, put real effort into spending less.

Spending less meant more time living for the fun of it without working, which made it far more worthwhile than saving money while holding down a steady job.

I was the same type, and I thought of it almost as a job: living cheap and plain was the work available to someone who couldn't earn anything at the moment.

No income, so cut the spending instead.

Growing up poor meant that going without never felt like a hardship, and I had come to see that upbringing as one of my strengths as a traveler.

But K was a level above me when it came to frugality — he never ate out, never bought snacks.

He rarely moved on, staying in a dirt-cheap house deep in the mountains, building a wood fire and cooking for himself.

The sheer simplicity of the way he lived hit me hard and gave me all kinds of inspiration, but at the same time it left me with questions about a life that simple.

What is there to enjoy about spending your days alone and quiet that far up in the mountains?

The world is overflowing with every kind of adventure — isn't it a waste to go through life without trying any of them?

These days I can understand the wish for seclusion and the contentment of quiet days, but to me at twenty it seemed completely pointless.
