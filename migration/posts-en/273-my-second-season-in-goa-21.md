## Eccentric Friends

Basically everyone in Goa was an eccentric, but there were many different ways to be one — bright and dark, gentle and frightening, country and city, all kinds.

Out of all of them, maybe because I was a traveler with an otaku streak myself, the eccentric friends who collected around me were the ones with otaku streaks of their own.

N was a friend who had every trait of the Japanese subculture otaku in abundance, and we first met the summer before, in Vashisht.

He had been at a university in Kanto, but he gave up on the Japanese social system, dropped out, and went out on the road looking for a new world.

## The Plastic-Bagpacker

People who travel through Southeast Asia and India are usually called backpackers.

The reason is that they walk around with their belongings stuffed into a big backpack on their backs.

N, though, was a plastic-bagpacker.

He knew that there was no need to prepare by buying an expensive backpack in Japan, that almost every daily necessity could be had on the spot, so he stuffed a toothbrush and a change of clothes into a supermarket plastic bag and landed in Nepal.

He looked like a man on his way to the neighborhood public bath.

You could travel with no fuss, you never had to shoulder a heavy load, and daily necessities were cheap in Nepal, so it did, admittedly, make sense.

That kind of reasoned eccentricity was, in N's mind, subculture art with good taste behind it; he was proud of it, and it was one of his prize stories.

## N's Sense of Money

Back in Japan, N had made his living as a professional pachislot player — the slot machines in Japan's pachinko parlors — and the way he told it, it wasn't gambling, it was a job.

Every model had its own particular method for beating it, he said, and with enough training you could learn to land the hits reliably.

That kind of information got shared among the professional pachislot players, and armed with knowledge only the pros had access to, he would tour the neighboring prefectures on his days off and rake it in, or so the story went.

Maybe that sense of money was the reason, but N had no attachment to it at all — he spent freely, and on the flip side, extreme poverty never troubled him.

Travelers generally put more value on experience than on money, but N, in keeping with the rest of his character, was something you could call an experience fundamentalist, and he spent his life piling up unusual life experience.
