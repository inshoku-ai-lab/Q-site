## Kamaboko

Stuffing myself with fresh mandarin oranges and then getting a good bowl of udon made me happy enough, but what truly surprised me was the kamaboko, a steamed fish cake.

That anyone could be surprised by kamaboko is surprising in itself, but I really was.

As a kid, I never much liked fish-paste foods like kamaboko and chikuwa.

Part of the reason may have been that the udon and the oden, a simmered fish-cake-and-vegetable stew, we made at home were always lightly seasoned, and boring to a child's tongue.

## A Whole Other Level

But the kamaboko I ate in Shikoku was on a whole other level.

It was delicious.

A fresh, springy bite, and then the savory taste of fish slowly seeping out.

For the first time, I understood that kamaboko was not a lump of plastic or rubber but a food made from fish.

And that it tasted good.

For me, kamaboko had always been a side character, something I'd never once taken an interest in, but right there it was promoted, in one leap, to something that mattered.

The thrill of that kamaboko upgraded the whole udon set meal, which upgraded my rating of the diner, which upgraded my rating of Shikoku.

Thanks to one slice of kamaboko, everything felt worth it: coming to Shikoku, traveling by bicycle, all of it.

## Hot Springs

I pedaled on toward Cape Muroto.

I love the ocean, so the plan was to follow the coast all the way to Okinawa.

Besides, the coast stays flat, so there'd be no hills to climb.

I came across a hot spring in a good spot, so I decided to end the day with a soak.

Shikoku has hot springs all over, so it looked like I could soak in one every single day.

Once the hot water had eased my body, I pitched my tent on the empty lot behind the bathhouse and settled in for the night.

On Honshu, Japan's main island, sleeping in a tent beside a national highway had always given me a vague sense of danger, but after I came to Shikoku that unease vanished in an instant.

Something about Shikoku put a traveler at ease.
