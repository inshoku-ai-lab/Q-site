## A Sudden Turn

According to B, the others had gone on waiting around aimlessly even after I slipped away.

Fed up with all that pointless waiting, H went to sort things out with the police.

It was H's second time in Manali, so he understood how things worked, and his English was fluent — the obvious choice to negotiate.

But he was a former professional boxer, which made him quick to flare up, and when he snapped his eyes went razor-sharp.

H negotiated with them politely, but nothing in this world is harder to get a straight answer out of than an Indian doing official business.

The talks went nowhere, and when he turned to walk off on his own an officer grabbed his arm, and he bellowed "Don't touch me!" at the man.

This was where things went bad: the offended officer began insisting that if H was going to talk like that, none of us would be released until the procedure had been carried out properly.

Absurdly childish emotional logic, but that is what Indian police are actually like.

The law does not matter to them in the slightest; the whole thing runs on childish emotion and greed.

## The Signature

The condition the officer then produced was this: none of us would be going anywhere until every person who had signed the paper was standing there together.

And with that, the whole mess came down on me at once.

Because the one who signed that paper and then disappeared was me.

So while everybody else was drifting around the police station, B alone had been given permission to come and tell me to get back there.

A cowardly way to do it, childish and absurd, but with my friends still being held there was nothing else for it.

It was right about the moment I had been thinking of getting into bed, but I decided to make one more push and head down to the town of Manali.

## Released

Once again I took a rickshaw into town, went to the police station, and showed them my passport.

At long last everyone was released and no harm was done, but the sheer stupidity of the Indian police had left all of us sick of it.

The officers did not really care about us one way or the other; it was all just to make an example of us, a childish power game they were acting out.

We ate at a Tibetan restaurant in town, and with one good memory to finish on, the day's disaster was over.
