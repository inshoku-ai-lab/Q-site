# Gambling Games

What I got hooked on during my junior high years was games.

I first held a game console in sixth grade, a handheld called the Nintendo Game Boy, bought for me when I went to visit my grandmother's house.

Compared to my classmates, it was a very late gaming debut.

Because the debut came late, once the Game Boy was in my hands I fell deeper and deeper into it.

All my free time went into games.

Games at friends' houses, games alone.

The games I sank into were not just computer games — I played cards, board games, chess and so on as well.

Three of us boys of the same age, Jehovah's Witnesses from the neighborhood, would gather and play cards and board games constantly.

The reason we did not play computer games was that none of us owned one.

All three of us were rebels among the Jehovah's Witnesses, obeying our parents on the surface while stealing from offering boxes and the like behind their backs.

It was the same with cards: outwardly we made a show of keeping score and enjoying the competition, but underneath we were gambling for money.

Borrowing books on card games from the library, we played every card game there was, always with money on the line.

![Playing cards fanned out on a dark table with stacks of poker chips, red dice and a face-down deck](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog11-1.jpg)

Our gambling was not for making money; we bet to make the game more serious.

So the stakes were extremely small, amounts we could cover with our monthly allowance.

When we played the board game Monopoly for money, what we earned in the game carried over into the real stakes, which let us enjoy a more realistic game of Monopoly.

# Fellow Rebels, Second-Generation Jehovah's Witnesses

We rebels played together all the time.

One was a classmate, one a year below me.

Both had moved into the neighborhood when I was in the upper grades of elementary school.

I think the reasoning was that Jehovah's Witness children would be a good influence on each other if they grew up nearby.

Even without meeting up deliberately, we ran into each other at the three meetings a week whether we liked it or not, and since we lived two minutes' walk from each other's houses and our parents knew each other well, we came and went freely.

One of the rules of the Jehovah's Witnesses is not to associate with worldly people.

My mother kept this rule strictly; as far as I know, in over ten years she went out socially with a worldly person (a colleague from work) exactly once.

She did not go so far as to stop me from playing with my classmates, but she hated having worldly friends come over to our house.

When I played with them, it was outside or at their place.

Playing with other Jehovah's Witness children, on the other hand, was encouraged, so my fellow rebels could drop by our house without a second thought.

The rebel who was my classmate walked home from school with me, so between the daily walk home, the Jehovah's Witness meetings, and cards at the house, we spent a great deal of time together.

He was one of the few friends with whom I could share both the Jehovah's Witness family situation I hid from my other classmates and the double life I hid from the other Jehovah's Witnesses.

Rather than close friends bound by real affection, we were more like fellow inmates sharing a truth we wanted hidden.

The only ones who could share the double life of a second-generation Jehovah's Witness, hidden from parents and friends alike.

To be precise, it was a triple life: the Jehovah's Witness face out front, the rebel face behind it, and the face I wore at school.

![A faceted drinking glass on a dark surface, glowing and casting a ring of light around its base](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog11-2.jpg)

Even though we walked home together, at school we kept a certain distance.

For a while, every day on the way home, I played a kind of mild bullying game where I would taunt him and then run.

But he was not the type to be bullied, so he would chase me down and hit me on the arm and back.

It was not that we were on bad terms; it was the way junior high boys played, with tension running through it.

On the way home, taunt him and get hit.

A few hours later, see him again at the meeting and play together.

The next day, on the way home again, taunt him and get hit.

Afterward, over to my house for cards.

We switched between any number of faces as it suited us.

We hated being Jehovah's Witnesses down to our bones, yet not one of us ever talked about quitting.

To us in junior high, the idea of becoming independent from our parents was unimaginable, and we lived a slave's life of grudging obedience to whatever they said.

For us, with no hope whatsoever for the future, games were a handy tool that let us forget the darkness in front of us.
