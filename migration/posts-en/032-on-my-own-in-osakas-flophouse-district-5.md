# The Internet

Having bought a Mac to make music, I decided I might as well get myself hooked up to the internet, which was all the rage.

Looking into it, I found a line called "ISDN" with a plan called Telehodai — a flat rate that let you use it all night long — and figured it suited me, so I signed up.

The internet back then was far simpler than it is now: basically you just looked at the text and images on a website.

The era of Web 1.0.

Video streaming did not exist, and digital video itself was a rarity.

A time before Hotmail or Gmail.

Internet users were still just a handful of enthusiasts, very much a niche pursuit.

Even so, the volume of material online was staggering, and this entirely new world of information overwhelmed me.

Anything I wanted to look up, I could look up twenty-four hours a day without going to a bookstore or a library.

I could really feel it: I had come to a different world from the one I had known.

# HOTLINE

Underground information was still my thing, and while making the rounds of shady sites I came across a piece of communication software called "HOTLINE."

It was a Mac-only application, something like what we would now call a chat app, but since you could send and receive data, it was prized by users who wanted to trade illegal files.

![Four overlapping speech bubbles in pink, orange, blue and green, each holding a question mark](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog32-1.jpg)

Converting a three-minute song to MP3 took about twenty minutes in those days, so trading video or music data over the net was beyond anyone's imagination.

All the same, all sorts of applications were circulating, so I downloaded music apps and image apps and played around with them.

For learning to really handle a Mac it was tremendously useful, and it also became the spark that set my own creativity to work.

Trading illegally copied files is now completely against the law, but back then it was still a gray area, and as long as no money changed hands it was left alone.

I took an interest in this back-alley internet world and went deeper and deeper into it.

Not that I actually did anything shady — it was a kind of adolescent posturing, and simply having underground information brought a certain self-satisfaction.

# DTM

With the help of "illegally copied software" and the like, I got hooked on making music on a computer.

Around then I had broadened out beyond hardcore punk techno and was listening to the genre in general, and I was beginning to wake up to the joy of creating techno music myself.

I had bought the computer to compose with my older brother, but I mostly used it for my solo music work, and a considerable amount of my time went into composing.

![Torn, yellowed pages of handwritten sheet music with staves, notes and faded lettering](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog32-2.jpg)

What brought a breath of fresh air to the world of DTM — desktop music, composing on a computer — at the time was the software synthesizer: no actual hardware required, everything done inside the computer.

It was a huge shift from the method that had been standard until then — connecting equipment and recording — to composing with nothing but mouse clicks.

My favorite was a software synthesizer called "RB-338," which could create the squelchy sound peculiar to techno, and its weirdly bent tones held techno obsessives in thrall.

Being the type who goes deep into whatever interests me, I illegally modified the drum section of RB-338, changing it to the sounds I liked, and used it that way.

It is nothing special as a feature now, but at the dawn of DTM it was something to get genuinely fired up about.

Composing music yourself changes how you hear other music, too.

I started listening to techno music from the maker's point of view, and that set my techno fever blazing.
