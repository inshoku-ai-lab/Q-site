# Coming Home to Japan

I came back to Japan from Peru early in the new year, in winter.

Life in Japan began, just my grandfather and me.

"Living together," though, was a loose way to put it: my grandfather was either off at the pachinko parlor or in his own room doing jigsaw puzzles, so in practice it was no different from living alone.

I had quit the Jehovah's Witnesses and quit high school; the half-confined life in Peru was over, I was back in Japan, and there was no mother around to nag me.

It was the freest situation I had ever been in, and yet I did nothing in particular — watching TV, playing games, standing and reading in bookstores, the same days as before.

I had quit high school saying I wanted work connected to movies and comedy, but in reality that was partly an excuse to make quitting look more justified, so there was not enough drive in me to act on it right away.

After quitting the Jehovah's Witnesses and high school, I hardly saw anyone at all; the only people I met in this period were my grandfather, my aunt, and my older brother.

I did nothing, just idly kept on with games, and when a few weeks had passed,

my mother came back from Peru.

# Shut-In

Even after my mother came back, the same kind of life continued.

Barely talking with her, I shut myself in my room and escaped into games, TV, movies, and books.

My mother in this period did not nag the way she used to.

She probably had no idea how to deal with me now that I was out from under her control.

![A young man in a black hooded sweater sitting outdoors, looking off to one side](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog24-1.jpg)

In the end, the whole period came to about four months, shut in and playing games.

The time in Peru had been a kind of shut-in life as well, so it amounted to spending both the second and third terms of my second year of high school as a shut-in.

I had quit high school thinking I wanted to sharpen my instincts for surviving on my own, but in reality I could not get moving right away, and spent a long stretch stagnating.

Yet that stagnant period was, seen from a wider perspective, an adjustment period, storing up power for a big jump — something that becomes very clear looking back.

Having been raised under repression, I was bad at dealing with people and used media as an escape from reality, but in fact media was my only point of contact with the outside world, and from it I was learning a model of what it means to live.

In this period, the only friendly contact I had with anyone outside my family came in those few days playing with kids my own age in Peru.

The friendship with my fellow rebels among the Jehovah's Witnesses had also been cut off the moment I left the religion.

That friendship was a battlefield friendship between people who shared a common enemy in our Jehovah's Witness parents, and once I withdrew from the battlefield our friendship vanished in an instant.

From what I heard later, the other Jehovah's Witness children apparently quit as well, following after me.

Only much later did I notice that I often end up playing this role, the one who charges ahead first.

# Getting Ready to Go Out into the World

My mother would leave newspaper ads for part-time jobs where I could see them, putting pressure on me — work, work.

Not mentally prepared enough to handle that pressure, I ignored it and kept playing games.

At her wit's end, my mother consulted my sister, and an international call came in directly from my sister in Peru.

![A teddy bear propped beside a landline telephone with a lit keypad, in blue light](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog24-2.jpg)

I was relatively open with my sister, so it was, I think, a good way to get an opinion across to me.

Persuaded by her, I came to see the obvious fact that I could not keep being a shut-in forever, and resolved to get some kind of job.

Even so, there was not much work available to a seventeen-year-old high school dropout, and I was at a loss as to what to do.

For now, my goal was to get out of my family's house and live alone in central Osaka, but at seventeen I could not rent an apartment on my own, and so I stagnated at home.

Some kind of job was essential just to save up the money for living alone, but for me, with my shut-in habit now set in, it was quite a challenge.

What I found was a job delivering pizza.

As long as I was out delivering on a moped, there was no need to talk to people.

This, I thought, was something I could do without any awkwardness.

Not having a moped license, I went to a driving school and got one.

It cost about ten thousand yen (around eighty dollars) and took a single day.

The preparations for the part-time delivery job were complete.

It was a delivery job at a pizza place in the neighborhood, and since my sister had worked there part-time in the past, the hurdle was relatively low compared to other jobs.

And so I managed to escape the stagnant period of my life.
