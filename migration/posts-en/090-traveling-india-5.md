# Satoshi

Stay on Sudder Street, Calcutta's strip of cheap guesthouses, and after a while there is someone you are guaranteed to meet.

That someone is Satoshi.

He spoke Kansai dialect, and he said he was best buddies with Matsu-chan of Downtown, the famous comedy duo.

He had full command of nori-tsukkomi — playing along with a joke before wheeling around and snapping at it, second nature to anyone from Kansai and a foreign custom to everyone else — and he kept up a fast, well-timed back-and-forth with me, a Kansai man myself.

And yet, for all that flawless Kansai dialect and all that fast-talking comedy culture he embodied, from any angle you looked at him he was Indian.

The first time a man who looked one hundred percent Indian came up and spoke to me in the dialect I grew up with, it floored me.

There were faint differences of pitch in his speech, the kind only another Kansai native would catch, but anyone from a different part of Japan would hear him and take him for the real thing.

That was the level Satoshi worked at.

"Me and Matsu-chan from Downtown, we're best buddies," he kept saying, and since there was no way that was true, I grilled him until I had the truth about who he was.

# Who Satoshi Really Was

He turned out to be a resident of Sudder Street itself, running an incense stall down at the end of the road.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog90-1.jpg)

He had picked up the language as a boy, he said, from staying glued to a traveler from Kansai who was there for the long haul.

Indians tend to have a gift for languages, but his gift stood out.

Satoshi had Kansai dialect in his bones, more by instinct than most foreigners actually living in Japan, and his cultural grasp of comedy was enough to satisfy even a comedy nerd like me.

His shop itself, I suspect, was pretty dead.

He came by our guesthouse constantly to run the fool-and-straight-man routine with the Japanese travelers, over and over.

Anyone who got friendly with him almost always bought a mountain of incense off him as souvenirs to take back to Japan, so for Satoshi, messing around with Japanese travelers probably made more sense than minding the shop.

K, the one I mentioned before, had nine months in this city behind him, and sure enough he and Satoshi were best buddies in the literal sense.

The two of them were kindred spirits, and their interracial nori-tsukkomi was worth seeing at least once.

# Moving On

My stay in Calcutta had been far more fun than I ever expected, and I was ready to close it out and head for the next place.

Having come this far and lived through a flood in the slums, I was in a frame of mind where solo travel or anything else could come — bring it on.

The fear I had carried about India was fear of the unknown, and now that I knew Calcutta's thickest parts, there was nothing left to be afraid of.

![](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog90-2.jpg)

Ready in my head and giddy with the excitement of moving again, I went straight from there to the railway station and booked a sleeper ticket to Varanasi.

# Analog

I keep saying this, but travel back then — and society itself, for that matter — was nothing like it is now.

One big reason was that this was before the internet spread, so looking up train information, buying tickets, all of it ran on analog means.

And in India, that analog quality had evolved in an extreme direction.

Every country has people with manners and people without, but there can't be many countries where the gap is as extreme as it is in India.

When a country builds its social systems, gearing them to the well-mannered is not going to hold the place together.

Inevitably you need systems built around the ones without manners.

That shows up in every corner of life, and getting a train ticket is part of it.

In India the concept of waiting your turn does not exist, so it comes down to whoever is strongest, or whoever is most desperate.

A foreign traveler raised soft in a civilized country stands no chance against ordinary Indians living flat out with everything they have, and for a foreigner even buying a ticket properly is hard.

Anyone with money to spare can leave it to a travel agent, but a broke backpacker out to save whatever he can wants to save at every turn.

All of that together is why the stations in the big cities have windows reserved for foreigners.

On trains in particular, foreigners are heavily favored, and can find an open seat more easily than Indians can.

Further back there were no such measures for foreigners, so it was much harder — and the travel, I think, was that much more real for it.
