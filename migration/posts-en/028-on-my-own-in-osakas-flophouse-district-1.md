# Living Alone Begins

Moving from my parents' house to the new place was easy.

The only thing I had that counted as a large item to move was my bicycle, and since it was not far from my parents' house, hauling my clothes and a few shelves over in a small truck was all it took.

At last, a new and free life was beginning.

An experience entirely unlike anything before it.

Having no idea in the first place how a person is supposed to live alone, I bought one of those living-on-your-own handbooks and used it for reference.

Buying a small gas burner, buying a pot, filing the applications for gas and electricity — every trivial preparation for living alone was a pleasure.

A Western-style one-room apartment, the room itself six tatami mats, about a hundred square feet.

A sink and a counter roughly the size of a small desk, a small shower and toilet in one space, and on the small balcony, a little washing machine.

Past the balcony, close enough to touch, was the side wall of a factory.

![A small jade-plant succulent in a metal cup on a shelf in front of books](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog28-1.jpg)

It was a long way from what you would call a lovely home, but the bare minimum was packed neatly into that narrow space and it met my needs perfectly.

A castle of my own, completely free.

# Finding the Ideal Job

There was a reason I had moved close to the center of Osaka.

Wanting to work at TSUTAYA, the largest video rental store in western Japan, which at the time stood near Tennoji Station — that was the reason.

In truth I did not particularly want to work; I just wanted to watch a lot of movies and listen to a lot of music for free.

The store was enormous, with a part-time staff of over a hundred.

The number of rental videos was beyond compare — they had everything — and there were a considerable number of music CDs as well.

The store was not actually advertising for part-time help, but that did not bother me.

I went to the store in person and told the clerk at the register that I wanted to work there.

The manager came out right away, and the interview took place on the spot.

In the interview I made it clear that I loved movies and wanted a job connected to them.

Probably they were simply short-staffed, but they agreed to hire me immediately.

If I had applied through one of those part-time job magazines, the requirements would probably have said high school graduate or above and I likely would not have been hired, so going in person was the right call, I think.

Living alone had gotten off to a good start, the work I wanted had turned up, and my life had begun to flow smoothly.

# A Teenager's Philosophy of Life

Around this time my interests were movies and music and books and manga, and nothing else.

The moment I started living alone, my interest in video games vanished, just like that.

Having things I wanted to do, I thought getting absorbed in games was a waste of time.

That I could break away so easily from six years of being hooked on games was something I never expected.

What interested me was

sharpening my own sensibility through cultural works.

That one point alone.

I pursued that single point quite stoically, I think.

Perhaps it was the blood of my mother, who is thoroughly immersed in religion?

I had no intention of living by anything other than art, and I believed the best way to make the most of the sharp senses I had right then at eighteen was to absorb works of art without limit.

That absence of hesitation, that power of concentration, is youth's greatest strength, I think.

# Economizing

When I started living alone I had about five hundred thousand yen (roughly four thousand dollars) in savings, my part-time job was steady and the rent was cheap, so I had money to spare.

Even so, the urge to live in a hurry was strong, and I meant to economize to the extreme and concentrate every yen on sharpening my sensibility.

![A hand holding a pencil over a long receipt, beside a spiral notebook and calculator](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog28-2.jpg)

I was utterly convinced that this was the most important thing for my own happiness.

My greed for the act of living was off the charts.

Twelve years of repression as a Jehovah's Witness were accelerating that explosion of living.

In those days I had almost no appetite for food and thought that as long as I stayed alive, that was enough.

For one stretch, udon at the discount supermarket in the neighborhood was twenty yen (about fifteen cents) a portion, so I ate nothing but udon.

Fried udon and grated-yam udon were the two main menu items.

Over that particular stretch I was eating more fried udon than anyone else in the world.

Looking back, it seems pretty stupid, but at the time I took pride in being the best in the world at something.

The first email address I ever created was "yakiudon," and I basked in how special I was.

After a while, thinking there was a nutritional problem with this, I added natto rice and curry rice to the menu.

Cut the food budget, whatever it takes.

I was in love with my own stoicism, and pursuing art to that extreme struck me as cool.

To turn being born and raised poor into a strength, I figured, the thing to do was not to mind economizing.
