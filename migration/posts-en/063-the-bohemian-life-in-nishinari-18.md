# Moving Back to My Parents' House

As part of getting ready to leave, I gave up living alone in Nishinari for good and moved back into my parents' house.

Once I was back from the trip, I planned to come to this neighborhood one more time and see my friends from the part-time job.

I meant to keep the label going, and my parents' house wasn't that far from the city, so I would still be able to run that side of my life from there.

Moving back in was as easy as moving out had been.

The furniture I didn't need went out with the bulk trash, and the rest I hauled over to my parents'.

What had accumulated was some clothes and a big pile of books, records, CDs, and MiniDiscs.

Even so, it all fit into a few cardboard boxes.

My brother helped with this move.

We carried my things out onto the balcony at my parents' house and packed them into big plastic bins.

Now I could leave Japan for a while without my things getting in anyone's way.

# Staying at My Parents' House

I stayed there a few days before setting out.

Since something might happen to me in China or India and they might never see me again, I also made the rounds of my relatives to say goodbye.

Luckily every one of them lived within bicycle range of my parents' house, so it was easy.

All of them acting like this was the last time they would ever see my face.

At the time I thought they were making an enormous fuss, but now I understand their worry perfectly well.

It's true that going off alone to China and India in your teens, unable to speak anything but Japanese, is a real challenge, and anything could have happened.

# Why China

Why go to China at all when India was where I wanted to go? There were a few reasons.

One was that it was cheap.

At a time when discount airfares were not yet common, taking a boat to China rather than flying was the cheapest way to travel.

And traveling by nothing but ship and overland roads struck me as about the coolest thing there was.

For a self-proclaimed artist, being cool and being interesting counted for more than anything else.

The other reason was that, having grown up in the city of Osaka and in a Jehovah's Witness household — a world bolted shut with rules — I wanted to experience real chaos.

When it came to chaos in Asia, China and India were the two great giants in my imagination.

Life in Nishinari was chaotic enough, but it was nothing you could set beside those countries.

The unknown, the uncharted, the Third World, developing countries, communist chaos and capitalist chaos — China and India were what would satisfy that adolescent fantasy running in my head.

I had an idea that maybe what was truly valuable lay not in the order built by human hands but in the chaos beyond their reach.

I wanted to see real chaos with my own eyes and live it.

Chaos was the thing that stirred my curiosity more than anything.

# The Language Barrier

But the biggest reason I chose China was language.

I was bad enough at English that I once scored a single point on a junior high English test.

I studied after that and got up to around seventy, but nothing changed about the fact that I still couldn't speak it.

On my trip to Thailand, my English ran to numbers, how much, and thank you.

The locals were at about the same level, so that worked out fine in its own way.

In China, unlike India, English gets you nowhere.

Since I couldn't speak it anyway, that suited me better.

The advantage was that the Chinese and I could both read and write kanji, the Chinese characters Japanese uses too.

I could read what the shop signs said, and in a restaurant I could get the gist of a menu.

When I had something to get across, I could write the characters on a piece of paper.

My kanji writing was at rock bottom, the same as my English, but it was still the script of the country I was born and raised in, so it went a lot further than English did.

The idea was that if I held my conversations in kanji and got used to traveling, I would manage somehow by the time I reached India.
