## Section Chief

When I got back to the guesthouse, my friend N had returned.

We traded a warm hug to celebrate seeing each other again after so long.

We'd parted in Goa about five months earlier, and since then, apparently, he had stopped over in Thailand to renew his India visa before coming back into the country.

He had already been traveling all over Asia for some three years, and in his mid-thirties he was a bit older than the travelers around him, so everyone looked up to him as the senior of the group.

Most travelers were somewhere between their mid and late twenties.

Everyone, me included, called him by the nickname Kachō — Section Chief, the middle-manager rank in a Japanese company — and he liked being called that.

His gentle, breezy manner, the oddly high-strung streak in him, the domestic penny-pinching that seeped out of him over trivial things — all of it called up the title of section chief, and everyone agreed it fit.

Not a department head or a company president, and not a rank-and-file employee either.

Perhaps because of that stingy but lovable character, people were always gathering around him, and there was something of a leader about him.

Everyone made a fuss over him, Section Chief this and Section Chief that, and he would toss jokes back — "One of the men in my section..." — until we had the easy in-group air of colleagues in the same trade.

I, for my part, had the look of the twenty-year-old rookie, the new hire.

## R

R was the friend I'd hung out with every day for months in Goa, and he was supposed to be in this village, so I asked N — the Section Chief — where he was, and he told me R lived down on the lower slope of the mountain, about a twenty-minute walk from the center of the village.

The next day, in high spirits, I set off to pay him a visit.

R had no email address back then, so we hadn't been in touch, and word of mouth was the only source of news, which meant he had no idea I had come to Vashisht.

The plan was to turn up out of nowhere and surprise him.

I walked the mountain path for a while, looking for his house.

I followed the route I'd been given, but his place seemed to be somewhere genuinely out of the way, and it wasn't turning up.

Asking locals whether they had seen a Japanese man, I wandered around until at last I found the house he was living in.

He had rented a house of his own in Goa, and here too, it seemed, he had one to himself.

A traditional Himalayan build, mud walls and a stone roof, with a good feel to it.

In one sense you could call it ramshackle; in another, a house that lived right up against nature.

I knocked at the door, and nobody came out.

Thinking he might be asleep, I shouted R's name from outside, and after a while the door opened.
