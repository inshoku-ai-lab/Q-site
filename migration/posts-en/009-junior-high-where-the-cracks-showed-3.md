# Junior High Life

As soon as I entered junior high, most of my classmates from elementary school joined some kind of sports club.

Since I had to devote myself to Jehovah's Witness activities, I was encouraged not to take part in clubs.

It was not that club activities were forbidden to Jehovah's Witnesses, but the unspoken sense was that it was better not to do them.

Convincing myself that it was not that I could not join a club but that clubs simply did not interest me, I got through it that way.

This mental wiring was my usual pattern, an extremely unhealthy way of thinking, but it did me the favor of letting me look away from the heavy days I could not face head-on.

There were quite a lot of things I could not do:

singing the school song was out (no praising anyone but Jehovah God),

the cavalry battle at sports day, boys riding on each other's shoulders, was out (no acts of violence),

the traditional Japanese dance at sports day was out (no praising Shinto),

birthday parties were out (no bringing pleasure to anyone but Jehovah God),

Christmas parties were out, and so were the first shrine visit of the New Year, Valentine's, the Doll's Festival, and the rest.

Listing them all would be endless — that was how far I was kept away from ordinary, everyday activities.

What I wished for back then was to be born into a normal family and try living a normal life.

Not wanting to (not being able to) join a club, I belonged to what people called the go-home club, the ones who went straight home.

When there was time after getting home, I played games with friends, went out on shoplifting runs, or hit the arcade.

It was a thoroughly unhealthy mind and an unhealthy life, I think.

# Puberty

In puberty a boy turns into an adult.

I was already crawling along the bottom of a pyramid-shaped society, warped out of shape, and now puberty came on top of that as one more twist.

Maybe every boy raised in a fatherless household goes through something similar, but my mother doted on me excessively.

That love was a mother's love for her child, but subconsciously I think my mother was giving off the energy of love between a man and a woman.

![A young man in a black leather jacket and white face mask holding his palm up toward the camera](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog9-1-1.jpg)

For a boy entering puberty, doting and overprotection are the most uncomfortable things there are.

Building the wall around my heart even higher, I withdrew further inside it.

An ordinary boy going through puberty would take an interest in girls and find ways to express it, but as a Jehovah's Witness I was forbidden to get involved with the opposite sex, and even talking with the girls in my class was not something I could freely do.

Perhaps from the trauma of the divorce, or from the puritanical streak of Jehovah's Witnesses, my mother would make blatantly snide remarks whenever I showed any sign of being interested in women, so at home I had to play the part of a boy with no sexuality.

With the natural flow of being human blocked and suppressed, the warping turned further inward and deepened.

# Grandpa

In my second year of junior high, my mother's father, my grandpa, moved in with us.

The reason was his age: leaving him to live alone would be a serious problem if anything happened.

Taking the opportunity, we moved within the same district to a public housing block with more rooms.

Even so we stayed poor: my grandfather had a four-and-a-half-mat room; my sister, who was around twenty, and I, in puberty, shared a six-mat room between us; another six-mat room was the living room and my mother's room; and there was a six-mat kitchen-dining room.

Going by how it feels to me now, it seems far too cramped for a human life, but at the time it did not bother me at all.

For me that was what living was, and I thought we had a reasonable amount of space.

It helped that all my classmates came from more or less the same kind of household.

My grandfather had taken up with a woman outside the marriage when he was young and walked out of the house, and my mother and the others apparently had a very hard childhood.

Then, when my mother was young, her mother (my maternal grandmother) died in a traffic accident, and my mother, who could get no support from her father (my maternal grandfather), seems to have gone on carrying a knot of unresolved feeling toward him.

This grandfather of mine, like my father, liked gambling, and went to pachinko parlors constantly.

For them, men from the day-laborer quarter, gambling was part of life, the most ordinary hobby there was.

![A man standing with a bicycle at a night street corner outside a brightly lit Japanese storefront](/images/wp/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Blog9-2-1.jpg)

My grandfather would burn through his once-a-month pension on pachinko in a week or two, then spend the remaining days until the next pension in his room, putting together puzzles.

When he won at pachinko now and then he would give me pocket money, and my mother would get angry, saying he was handing his grandson spending money without putting anything into the household.

It sounds like a sorry state of affairs, but on the surface the family relationships still worked.

That probably held together because the two great powers, my father and my mother, were not in conflict, and my mother, taking on both parental roles, reigned as the absolute authority.

My grandfather, my sister, and I kept the peace by not defying my mother.

The only fights that broke out were between my sister and me, but by the time she had reached adulthood and I had reached puberty, the fights had naturally stopped.
